  Today we're beginning with the most audacious structural decision Dickens made — the choice to tell this story through two entirely different voices and two entirely different relationships to time.  (@0.98x *audacious,time*)
  The dual narration of Bleak House — one voice omniscient and present tense, the other intimate and retrospective — is unlike anything else in Victorian fiction.  (<800ms> @0.98x *omniscient,retrospective*)
  Eleanor, you've written about this at length — let's start with you.  (<1500ms> @0.98x)

[Eleanor Hartley]
  What Dickens does with this novel's structure is, I think, the bravest thing he ever attempted on the page.  (+220ms @1.01x *bravest*)
  You have two narrative streams that never directly acknowledge each other, yet together they constitute a single, coherent moral argument about England.  (@1.01x *moral argument,England*)
  The third-person narrator — that cold, satirical, omniscient presence in the present tense — opens the novel with fog.  (@1.01x *fog*)
  That famous, relentless, accumulating fog.  (@0.98x [setup] *accumulating*)
  > "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping."  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *everywhere,defiled*)
  That narrator is withering, ironic, almost merciless — the voice of public indictment.  ([commentary] *indictment*)
  And then — without transition, without apology — we get Esther Summerson, writing in the past tense, apologising for her own existence in the very first paragraph.  (@1.01x *apologising,existence*)
  The structural contrast is the argument: England's institutions are cold and present and crushing; the human heart is warm and retrospective and scarred.  (<1500ms> *institutions,heart*)

[Oliver Trevelyan]
  From a performer's point of view, the switch between those two voices is one of the most exciting moments in all of Dickens — every single time it happens.  (+200ms @1.02x *exciting,every single time*)
  The third-person narrator has this quality of — I want to say relish — a barely concealed delight in how terrible everything is.  (@1.02x *relish,terrible*)

  > "This is the Court of Chancery; which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire; which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse, and its dead in every churchyard."  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *decaying,blighted,dead*)

[Caroline Woodcourt]
