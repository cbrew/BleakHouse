### Richard's Slide: The Seduction of the Suit (deep_dive)

[Host]

  We've spent time with the fog, with Chancery, with the great grotesques of the novel.  (@0.98x)

  Richard begins the novel as a charming, warm-hearted young man — full of possibility.  (@0.98x *charming,possibility*)
  He ends it, not yet thirty, as a hollow man consumed by a legal fiction.  (@0.95x *hollow,legal fiction*)
  The question is: how does Dickens make that seduction feel so utterly convincing — and so utterly terrible?  (<800ms> @0.97x *seduction,convincing,terrible*)

[Oliver Trevelyan]

  Richard doesn't fall — he drifts.  (*drifts*)
  He tries medicine, he tries the law, he tries the army — and each time he abandons the thing, the suit is waiting there like a comfortable chair.  (@1.02x *waiting,comfortable chair*)
  Dickens gives us this gorgeous, devastating line when Richard explains his thinking to Esther.  (@0.98x [setup])
  He says something to the effect that the suit must be attended to — that it would be wrong to let it go, that there is a fortune in it for Ada and himself.  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *fortune,Ada*)
  And every time he says it, it sounds fractionally more rehearsed — as though he's convincing himself rather than Esther.  ([commentary] *rehearsed,convincing himself*)

[Edmund Leigh]
  I want to be precise about what Dickens is actually diagnosing in Richard, because I think it's misread rather frequently.  (+200ms @0.94x *precise,misread*)
  This is not primarily a story about the wickedness of Chancery.  (@0.94x *wickedness*)

  The suit doesn't corrupt a strong man — it simply provides the perfect alibi for a weak one.  (@0.93x *alibi,weak*)

[Caroline Woodcourt]
