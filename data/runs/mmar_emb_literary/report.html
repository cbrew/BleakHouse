Welcome to Miss Marjoribanks Unpacked, a close reading of one of Victorian fiction's most underrated masterpieces. Mrs Oliphant published Miss Marjoribanks in 1866, and it has been quietly astonishing readers ever since. It is a novel about a young woman who returns from school to her widowed father's house in a provincial town, and proceeds to reorganise every social arrangement within reach. She does this with such total conviction and such devastating competence that you are never quite sure whether to admire her or be slightly afraid. Joining me today are three readers who have thought long and hard about why this novel is so extraordinary. Dr Eleanor Hartley is a novelist and creative writing teacher who cares deeply about how fiction is actually built — the sentences, the architecture, the sleight of hand. She is precisely the right reader for a novel this carefully engineered. Professor James Blackstone is a legal and social historian who specialises in the real institutions behind the fiction — what the world of Carlingford actually looked like from the outside in. He has the enviable gift of making the past feel immediate and, on occasion, genuinely alarming. And Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who first read Miss Marjoribanks as a teenager and has returned to it more times than she can count. She brings the perspective of the dedicated reader — the one who notices what makes you stop and read a sentence twice. I want to start where the novel itself starts — with the question of social engineering, and with Lucilla's almost breathtaking self-belief. Eleanor, she is a remarkable creation from the very first pages. What does Oliphant do to make her so immediately compelling?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Oliphant does — and she does it almost immediately — is give Lucilla a kind of psychological architecture that most novelists would spend three chapters establishing. Lucilla's self-belief is not vanity in the ordinary sense. It's something stranger and more interesting — a genuine conviction that her own concerns are universally compelling. The narrator spells it out with this wonderful surgical precision. The passage reads: "Lucilla, for her part, had the calmest and most profound conviction that, when she discussed her own doings and plans and clevernesses, she was bringing forward the subject most interesting to her audience as well as to herself." That word "clevernesses" is doing extraordinary work — it has this slightly comic, almost affectionate edge that keeps the satire from becoming cruel. And then Oliphant adds one devastating sentence: "Such a conviction is never without its fruits." That is the narrator stepping back and saying — she's right, you know. It works.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me about that passage — and Eleanor is quite right about its precision — is that it describes something we now have an entire vocabulary for in behavioural economics. The projection of confidence as a mechanism of persuasion. Lucilla doesn't ask permission to be important — she simply assumes it, and the social world around her adjusts accordingly. In 1866, for a young woman of Lucilla's station, that was not merely unusual. It was almost structurally impossible — and yet Oliphant makes it entirely credible. Because the tools Lucilla deploys — the dinner parties, the drawing room entertainments — those were the only legitimate instruments available to her. She is engineering a world within the only space she is legally permitted to occupy.

I want to start with something that makes readers laugh and then immediately makes them uneasy. Lucilla is caught by the matrons in what ought to be an awkward confession.
