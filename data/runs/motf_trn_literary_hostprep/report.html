Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
It is funny — and I think refusing to say so actually does Eliot a disservice. She's not asking us to weep over Maggie here. She's asking us to recognise her. Look at what Maggie actually plans — she'll run away to the gypsies, but she'll send her father a letter via a small gypsy who will run away without telling where she is. That is the logic of a child who has read too many stories and not enough practical geography. And Eliot sets it up with this extraordinary opening — listen to the scale of it: "No! she would run away and go to the gypsies, and Tom should never see her any more." That exclamation mark is doing enormous work. It's Maggie's voice breaking through the narrator's — this vast, operatic fury compressed into a nine-year-old running toward a common. The comedy and the heartbreak are the same thing. That's what makes it Eliot.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I don't dispute that it's funny. But I'd want to ask what the laughter is doing, and whether we're comfortable with what it costs. Because the passage that really stops me isn't the gypsy fantasy — it's the narrator's account of Maggie's education. Eliot tells us she had so few books that she sometimes read the dictionary. "in travelling over her small mind you would have found the most unexpected ignorance as well as unexpected knowledge." That phrase — 'small mind' — is the one that snags me. Eliot means it affectionately, I think. But a girl with Maggie's hunger, given what she'd been given, would have had a very large mind indeed. The comic ignorance about groceries and gypsies is charming, yes — but it's the symptom of a deprivation that isn't charming at all. My concern is that laughing at Maggie's blind spots lets Eliot — and us — off the hook for naming what actually produced them.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What's extraordinary to me, structurally, is that Eliot gives us three beats in very rapid succession — and they mirror the arc of the whole novel. First the fantasy: Maggie constructs an elaborate escape narrative with perfect internal logic. Then the education beat — the narrator steps back and gives us that wonderful, devastating account of what Maggie actually knows and doesn't know. And then reality collapses the fantasy entirely — violently, and in exactly the direction you'd least expect. Maggie's imagination is her gift and her undoing, because it has no anchor in material reality — it runs entirely on literary substitutes. She can deduce 'poly' means 'many.' She cannot deduce that gypsies have groceries. And that gap — between what language can do and what life requires — is the fault line the whole novel is built on.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I think Eliot refuses to let that be a clean either/or — and that refusal is itself the argument. Maggie's imagination is excessive — Eliot doesn't pretend otherwise. But the novel also shows us, very carefully, what that imagination has been fed on. The dictionary. The scraps. The brother who gets the proper schooling while she reads whatever she can find. Her thoughts are, as Eliot puts it — and this is the sentence I keep coming back to: "the oddest mixture of clear-eyed acumen and blind dreams." The acumen is innate. The blindness is manufactured by circumstance. Eliot holds both simultaneously — and the design flaw and the character flaw are, in the end, inseparable.

The trap is built from several overlapping systems, none of which were accidents. You have the legal system, which rewards the professionally literate — men like Wakem — at the expense of men like Tulliver who operate on older, oral notions of right. Tulliver's guiding principle — that 'water was water' and Pivart hadn't a leg to stand on — is a principle of natural justice. It has nothing to do with the actual machinery of English property law, which is technical, procedural, and expensive to navigate. And Tulliver's response to that dependency — sending that extraordinary letter to Mrs Glegg — tells you everything. He will repay the debt within the month, and, as Eliot puts it, "he desired no favours from her, either for himself or his children." He has just placed himself deeper in the hands of Wakem in order to free himself from the Dodsons. Pride and the credit market — those two forces together spring the trap. Wakem simply turns the key.

She had fallen asleep before nine, and had been sleeping for six hours before the faintest hint of a midsummer daybreak was discernible. She awoke from that vivid dreaming which makes the margin of our deeper rest. She was in a boat on the wide water with Stephen, and in the gathering darkness something like a star appeared, that grew and grew till they saw it was the Virgin seated in St Ogg’s boat, and it came nearer and nearer, till they saw the Virgin was Lucy and the boatman was Philip,—no, not Philip, but her brother, who rowed past without looking at her; and she rose to stretch out her arms and call to him, and their own boat turned over with the movement, and they began to sink, till with one spasm of dread she seemed to awake, and find she was a child again in the parlour at evening twilight, and Tom was not really angry. From the soothed sense of that false waking she passed to the real waking,—to the plash of water against the vessel, and the sound of a footstep on the deck, and the awful starlit sky. There was a moment of utter bewilderment before her mind could get disentangled from the confused web of dreams; but soon the whole terrible truth urged itself upon her. Stephen was not by her now; she was alone with her own memory and her own dread. The irrevocable wrong that must blot her life had been committed; she had brought sorrow into the lives of others,—into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love. The feeling of a few short weeks had hurried her into the sins her nature had most recoiled from,—breach of faith and cruel selfishness; she had rent the ties that had given meaning to duty, and had made herself an outlawed soul, with no guide but the wayward choice of her own passion. And where would that lead her? Where had it led her now? She had said she would rather die than fall into that temptation. She felt it now,—now that the consequences of such a fall had come before the outward act was completed. There was at least this fruit from all her years of striving after the highest and best,—that her soul though betrayed, beguiled, ensnared, could never deliberately consent to a choice of the lower. And a choice of what? O God! not a choice of joy, but of conscious cruelty and hardness; for could she ever cease to see before her Lucy and Philip, with their murdered trust and hopes? Her life with Stephen could have no sacredness; she must forever sink and wander vaguely, driven by uncertain impulse; for she had let go the clue of life,—that clue which once in the far-off years her young need had clutched so strongly. She had renounced all delights then, before she knew them, before they had come within her reach. Philip had been right when he told her that she knew nothing of renunciation; she had thought it was quiet ecstasy; she saw it face to face now,—that sad, patient, loving strength which holds the clue of life,—and saw that the thorns were forever pressing on its brow. The yesterday, which could never be revoked,—if she could have changed it now for any length of inward silent endurance, she would have bowed beneath that cross with a sense of rest.

Yet, strange to say, under this vigorous treatment Tom became more like a girl than he had ever been in his life before. He had a large share of pride, which had hitherto found itself very comfortable in the world, despising Old Goggles, and reposing in the sense of unquestioned rights; but now this same pride met with nothing but bruises and crushings. Tom was too clear-sighted not to be aware that Mr Stelling’s standard of things was quite different, was certainly something higher in the eyes of the world than that of the people he had been living amongst, and that, brought in contact with it, he, Tom Tulliver, appeared uncouth and stupid; he was by no means indifferent to this, and his pride got into an uneasy condition which quite nullified his boyish self-satisfaction, and gave him something of the girl’s susceptibility. He was of a very firm, not to say obstinate, disposition, but there was no brute-like rebellion and recklessness in his nature; the human sensibilities predominated, and if it had occurred to him that he could enable himself to show some quickness at his lessons, and so acquire Mr Stelling’s approbation, by standing on one leg for an inconvenient length of time, or rapping his head moderately against the wall, or any voluntary action of that sort, he would certainly have tried it. But no; Tom had never heard that these measures would brighten the understanding, or strengthen the verbal memory; and he was not given to hypothesis and experiment. It did occur to him that he could perhaps get some help by praying for it; but as the prayers he said every evening were forms learned by heart, he rather shrank from the novelty and irregularity of introducing an extempore passage on a topic of petition for which he was not aware of any precedent. But one day, when he had broken down, for the fifth time, in the supines of the third conjugation, and Mr Stelling, convinced that this must be carelessness, since it transcended the bounds of possible stupidity, had lectured him very seriously, pointing out that if he failed to seize the present golden opportunity of learning supines, he would have to regret it when he became a man,—Tom, more miserable than usual, determined to try his sole resource; and that evening, after his usual form of prayer for his parents and “little sister” (he had begun to pray for Maggie when she was a baby), and that he might be able always to keep God’s commandments, he added, in the same low whisper, “and please to make me always remember my Latin.” He paused a little to consider how he should pray about Euclid—whether he should ask to see what it meant, or whether there was any other mental state which would be more applicable to the case. But at last he added: “And make Mr Stelling say I sha’n’t do Euclid any more. Amen.”

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I find that I don't want to choose between your readings — which may itself be Eliot's most significant achievement. What the mill remembers structurally is the gap — the constitutive gap between what was promised and what was delivered. Tom grieving Maggie's absence, suddenly understanding — in that dim, boyish way — that Latin was alive once. That sentence is absurd and tender and structurally heartbreaking all at once — because it tells us he already had his teacher, and she'd just been put in the gig and sent home. The mill remembers everything the novel couldn't fix — and Eliot built it to do exactly that.
