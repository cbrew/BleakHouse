Opening: The Drawing Room as Kingdom (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to Miss Marjoribanks Unpacked — a close, argumentative, deeply affectionate reading of one of the most underrated novels in the English language. Miss Marjoribanks, published by Margaret Oliphant in 1866, follows Lucilla Marjoribanks — a young woman of extraordinary intelligence and iron will — as she returns from school to run her widowed father's household in the provincial town of Carlingford. She declares, almost on arrival, that she intends to be a comfort to her dear papa — and promptly turns the drawing room into the social engine of the entire town. To discuss this novel — and to disagree about it with real feeling — I have three guests who have each read it and reached, I think it's fair to say, completely incompatible conclusions. Oliver Trevelyan is an actor and writer, the voice of more classic novel audiobooks than anyone alive — he hears prose the way a conductor hears a score, and he has a particular gift for Oliphant's comic timing. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don and lifelong conservative who reads novels as moral theatre — he's interested in character tested by circumstance, and he is almost certainly going to be withering about the more politicised readings you'll hear today. And Dr Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads novels as anatomies of class power — every institution, every drawing room, every Thursday evening is, for Daniel, a mechanism that tells us something about who holds power and who is crushed by it. Oliver — before the show, you used the phrase 'chosen the perfect stage.' Can you unpack that? Because there's something provocative in the word chosen. Is Lucilla actually choosing, or is Oliphant just very good at making it look like a choice?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
It's the most important question you can ask about this novel, and I'll tell you why it kept me up the night before I first recorded it. Because when you read Lucilla aloud — when you actually have to give her a voice — you feel her intelligence operating at full pressure on every sentence she speaks. She is not being swept along by convention. She is deploying convention the way a great comedian deploys a straight man — with absolute control and genuine relish. Now — Oliphant establishes this in the very early chapters, when Lucilla announces her intention to be a comfort to her papa and to make Thursday evenings the social institution of Carlingford. What's extraordinary is that she says it completely without irony. She means it. And yet the reader is howling with laughter. That double register — earnest and absurd simultaneously — is the mark of a genius performer. And I think Oliphant is celebrating that genius, not anatomising it. So yes — chosen. Absolutely chosen. The drawing room is Lucilla's chosen stage, and she has studied its dimensions very, very carefully.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I love Oliver's reading — genuinely. But I want to press on what 'chosen' actually means in 1866 for a woman of Lucilla's class and position. The alternatives available to Lucilla are: make the drawing room her kingdom, or have no kingdom at all. That is not a free choice. That is the full extent of the permitted horizon. And what I find so fascinating — and so troubling — about Oliphant's novel is that Lucilla has not merely accepted this horizon. She has come to love it. She has made it magnificent. Which is exactly what the most effective systems of constraint do — they don't crush the brilliant people, they redirect them. They make the cage so beautifully furnished that the prisoner becomes its curator. When Oliphant tells us that Lucilla's great work is to be a comfort to her dear papa — I hear the voice of a woman who has perfectly internalised what her society needs her to believe about herself.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel, I think that reading is rather too tidy. Systems of constraint don't generally produce Lucilla Marjoribanks. What Oliphant gives us is a character of exceptional moral energy — a woman who chooses to govern a small world rather than lament that larger worlds are unavailable to her. That is not a case of a cage being beautifully furnished. That is a case of a will imposing itself on the materials at hand — which is, one might argue, the definition of practical wisdom. Burke would have recognised the type immediately — the individual who accepts inherited circumstances as the medium of achievement rather than the obstacle to it. The drawing room is not a diminishment of Lucilla. It is, on Oliphant's terms, precisely commensurate with her. And I think Oliphant intends us to admire, not mourn, that fact.
