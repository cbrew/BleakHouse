Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The comedy is real — I wouldn't dispute Oliver's ear for it for a moment. But I think the Megalosaurus does something more serious than make us laugh. It places us outside of time — or rather, it suggests that civilisation's progress is very much less certain than we flatter ourselves it is. Dickens describes the fog settling over London with an almost liturgical insistence. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That parenthetical — "and dirty" — is Dickens catching himself, as it were, in a moment of bathetic candour. He cannot maintain grandeur without pricking it — and that is, I think, a deeply moral reflex.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund's right that the fog is not merely atmospheric — but I'd push that argument somewhere else entirely. Look at who the fog touches in that passage. It reaches the Greenwich pensioners — it's "in the eyes and throats" of people the system has already used up. And then comes the detail that stops me every time: "fog cruelly pinching the toes and fingers of his shivering little 'prentice boy on deck." That word "cruelly" — Dickens gives moral agency to the weather itself, because the actual cruelty is economic. The boy is freezing not because November is cold, but because he is an apprentice — owned, essentially, by someone who is warm below deck.

The Vholes passage is magnificent — and rather savage, in fact. Kenge's speech is a perfect specimen of what one might call institutional self-righteousness. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver puts his finger on exactly the right thing, and I'd only add that Jarndyce sees what's coming before almost anyone else. His advice to Richard is one of the great moral speeches in the novel — direct, generous, and completely without illusion. He says to the boy — and I think this deserves to be heard in full — "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner." "Constancy in love is a good thing, but it means nothing, and is nothing, without constancy in every kind of effort." That word — constancy — is doing all the work. Richard's tragedy is not that he lacks intelligence or affection, but that he entirely lacks constancy. And Jarndyce — kind, patient, wise — can only state the truth; he cannot install it in the boy's character.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I think Jarndyce's speech is remarkable, but I'd push back slightly on reading it purely as moral counsel. What Jarndyce is really asking Richard to do is to behave as though the Chancery suit doesn't exist — to act as a free agent in a situation that has already corrupted his agency. Richard can't choose a profession and stick to it because the suit has hollowed out his sense of what work is for. Mrs. Badger sees it clinically — she says of Richard that he lacks the positive interest that makes medicine a vocation. "Young men like Mr. Allan Woodcourt who take it from a strong interest in all that it can do will find some reward in it through a great deal of work for a very little money and through years of considerable endurance and disappointment." Woodcourt is exactly what Richard cannot be — a man whose commitment is internal, rooted in something the law cannot touch. Chancery doesn't just delay the inheritance — it installs a mode of waiting that makes all genuine effort feel provisional and absurd.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that's the connection I want to press on — between Richard's slow dissolution and that dead child. Richard is being consumed by an institution — Chancery — just as the child is consumed by the conditions of poverty. The system has different tools for different classes — for the poor, material deprivation; for Richard, the corrosion of hope and volition. And Tulkinghorn stands somewhere in the middle of all this — the mechanism that keeps the wheels turning. Dickens gives us this extraordinary image of him — inscrutable, giving nothing away, described only through his shell. He is the perfect servant of a system that works best when no one can see inside it.
