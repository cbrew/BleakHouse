James Blackstone (social_historian)
The extraordinary thing about this opening is that Dickens doesn't begin with a person, or a house, or even an event. He begins with a condition — a condition that, as we come to understand, is the novel's true subject. The fog is everywhere, and so is the system of law that the fog comes to represent. Listen to how Dickens establishes that ubiquity. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That word "defiled" is doing enormous work. It isn't a meteorological term — it's a moral one. From the very first paragraph, Dickens is telling us that the fog is not merely atmospheric — it is ethical.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And notice what he does structurally with that fog — the anaphora, that relentless repetition of the word at the start of each clause. "Fog up the river... fog down the river... fog on the Essex marshes... fog on the Kentish heights." It performs the very thing it describes — the fog accumulates, clause by clause, until you feel you can't breathe. This is Dickens using syntax as weather. And then he scales it down — from the sweeping geography of the river to something devastatingly specific. "Fog cruelly pinching the toes and fingers of his shivering little 'prentice boy on deck." That single child — unnamed, cold, pinched by fog that is described as acting with intent — contains the whole novel's moral argument in miniature.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And then — as if he has established the fog as a general catastrophe — Dickens marches us directly to its source. The fog is thickest, he tells us, at a very specific address. "The raw afternoon is rawest, and the dense fog is densest, and the muddy streets are muddiest near that leaden-headed old obstruction, appropriate ornament for the threshold of a leaden-headed old corporation, Temple Bar." Now, Temple Bar is not merely a landmark — it was the gateway between the City of London and the legal quarter, the literal threshold of the law. Dickens knew this perfectly well, and his readers knew it too. "Leaden-headed" does triple duty — it means heavy, it means dull, and it means lead-coloured, the grey of fog and of a system that has lost its light.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That superlative construction — "rawest," "densest," "muddiest" — is another piece of virtuoso Dickens craft. He doesn't just say the fog is thick near Temple Bar — he says it is at its most extreme there. The law is the apex of the fog, the place where obscurity achieves its purest form. And then Dickens delivers the sentence that, for me, is one of the great rhetorical set-pieces in English prose. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." That catalogue — madhouse, churchyard, ruined suitor — moves from the abstract to the grotesquely specific, and it never lets you look away.

That sentence is the one I come back to, every single time. Because it's not describing Chancery in the abstract — it's describing what Chancery has done, everywhere, to everyone. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Caroline is entirely right to press on the literal truth of that. The Court of Chancery, at the time Dickens was writing, was in genuine crisis — cases that had lasted forty, fifty, sixty years were not unknown. The system was not corrupt in the sense of bribery — it was corrupt in a more insidious way: it was structurally incapable of resolving the disputes it was meant to adjudicate. So when Dickens writes about "walls of words" and "mountains of costly nonsense," he is not inventing — he is reporting.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
She does, and what's remarkable is that Esther never sounds censorious about it. She genuinely likes Richard — she's at pains to tell us so — and yet she can see the fault line running straight through him. What Dickens does here that I find structurally brilliant is to locate the problem not in Richard's character alone, but in the system that formed him. The passage is worth reading closely, because the sentence Dickens builds is itself an enactment of what he's describing. "They were good qualities, without which no high place can be meritoriously won, but like fire and water, though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." The pivot on 'but' is everything — good qualities, yes, but directed by what? That reversal — servant becoming master — is the entire architecture of Richard's tragedy compressed into one antithesis. And Dickens doesn't leave it there: 'If they had been under Richard's direction, they would have been his friends; but Richard being under their direction, they became his enemies.' He doubles the construction — chiasmus, almost — to make sure you feel the inversion, not just understand it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it matters enormously that Dickens pins this on education — on the system — rather than simply on Richard's weakness of will. Victorian readers would have recognized that critique immediately. The great public schools of the period operated on exactly the uniformity Dickens describes — the same curriculum, the same floggings, the same Latin verses, regardless of aptitude or temperament. Richard isn't a failure — he's a product. And the novel then delivers him into a second system — Chancery — which is equally indifferent to individual capacity, and which will finish what school began.

Eleanor is quite right about the structure, and I'd add that the legal specificity is doing enormous work here. The Six Clerks' Office was a real institution — abolished just twenty years before Bleak House was written — and Dickens' original readers would have known exactly what it was and exactly how it worked. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The Chesney Wold ending is one of the most controlled pieces of Gothic satire Dickens ever wrote. He gives us an image of institutional suppression — Sir Leicester buying silence — but then immediately undercuts it. The rumour doesn't die. It just becomes lame. Listen to how he phrases it: "It is a lame story, feebly whispering and creeping about, and any brighter spark of life it shows soon dies away." The story about the story is itself described as a dying thing. It's recursive — and it's brilliant. The suppression of truth, in Dickens, never quite works. It leaves a residue — a whisper, a creep, a lame and persistent ghost.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That image of the old aristocratic women with their large fans — "like charmers reduced to flirting with grim death" — is one of Dickens' most savage satirical strokes. These are the women who enforced Lady Dedlock's social world, who policed its codes, who now gossip about her desecrating the family mausoleum. And Dickens' response to their judgment — the dead Dedlocks taking it all "very calmly" — is quiet devastation. The dead are indifferent to class. It is only the living who maintain these fictions — and they look absurd doing it.

What moves me in this ending — really moves me — is Esther watching Richard disappear. Because he doesn't vanish all at once. He vanishes in glimpses.
