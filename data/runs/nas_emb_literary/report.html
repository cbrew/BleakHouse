James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me immediately about this arrival scene is that Gaskell gives us the industrial city as a weather event before she gives it to us as a place. You see it on the horizon as a discolouration of the sky — a lead-coloured cloud — before you ever see a building. That was entirely true to the experience of approaching any major cotton town in the eighteen-fifties. Manchester — Gaskell's obvious model for Milton — was visible from miles away, not because it was large, but because it was burning. And then there is this extraordinary image she gives us. "A great oblong many-windowed factory stood up, like a hen among her chickens, puffing out black 'unparliamentary' smoke." That word — unparliamentary — is doing a great deal of work. By the eighteen-fifties there had been repeated legislative attempts to regulate the smoke emissions of factory chimneys, and they had largely failed. Gaskell is not merely being picturesque — she's registering a political failure with a single sly word.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That domestic simile — the hen and her chickens — is so peculiar and so perfect, isn't it. Gaskell takes the most alarming feature of the landscape and makes it maternal, almost cosy. And that's precisely her technique throughout: she refuses to let the industrial world be simply monstrous. She insists it has a life, almost a personality, that has to be reckoned with. And what fascinates me about the passage immediately following the arrival — Margaret alone in her bedroom that first night — is how Gaskell compounds the claustrophobia architecturally. She gives us the physical dimensions: a narrow projection, a window looking onto a blank wall not ten feet away. And then this sentence, which I find genuinely haunting. "It loomed through the fog like a great barrier to hope." That blank wall is doing the work of a whole psychological state — despair, entrapment, exile. The architecture is the emotion.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That sentence stops me every time I read it. Because what makes it work is the word loomed — it's not a static wall, it's something active, something pressing toward her. And then Gaskell immediately cuts to Edith's letter — all sea breezes and dancing and trellised balconies in Corfu. The contrast is almost cruel in its precision. But I want to hold onto something else in these early chapters — the moment Margaret starts to encounter the mill workers on the street. Because it's one of the funniest passages in the novel, and it's also one of the most revealing about Margaret's character. Gaskell tells us that the factory girls would touch her shawl to ascertain the exact material, and even ask questions about articles they particularly admired. "The tones of their unrestrained voices, and their carelessness of all common rules of street politeness, frightened Margaret a little at first." At first — those two words are the whole story. She begins in fright, and Gaskell is honest about that. But the novel is about what happens to that fright.

In the back streets around them there were many mills, out of which poured streams of men and women two or three times a day. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The class dynamics in that street scene are fascinating to me precisely because Gaskell doesn't simplify them. The factory girls are not deferential, and the workmen are not respectful in any conventional sense. What we're seeing is a world in which the usual social grammar of public space simply doesn't apply. In the cotton towns of the eighteen-fifties, factory workers had a degree of economic independence — at least in good years — that their southern agricultural counterparts absolutely did not. That independence expressed itself physically, spatially — in exactly the bold, fearless faces that Margaret finds so disconcerting. It's not rudeness in any meaningful sense. It's a different social contract entirely.

And Gaskell prepares us for exactly that understanding — she nudges us gently, even as Margaret is still in the middle of her fright. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And she's right in ways that would have been legally demonstrable. Masters and workers in this period were bound by contract law — the Master and Servant Act gave employers coercive power that had no equivalent running the other direction. A worker who walked out could be imprisoned. A master who locked them out faced no such sanction. So when Margaret observes mutual dependence paired with mutual antagonism, she is describing a structural asymmetry that the law encoded and enforced. That context matters enormously when we get to the riot — because the workers knew exactly where the legal weight would fall.

It was about two miles from their house in Crampton Crescent to Marlborough Street. It was too hot to walk very quickly. An August sun beat straight down into the street at three o’clock in the afternoon. Margaret went along, without noticing anything very different from usual in the first mile and a half of her journey; she was absorbed in her own thoughts, and had learnt by this time to thread her way through the irregular stream of human beings that flowed through Milton streets. But, by and by, she was struck with an unusual heaving among the mass of people in the crowded road on which she was entering. They did not appear to be moving on, so much as talking, and listening, and buzzing with excitement without much stirring from the spot where they might happen to be. Still, as they made way for her, and, wrapt up in the purpose of her errand, and the necessities that suggested it, she was less quick of observation than she might have been, if her mind had been at ease. She had got into Marlborough Street before the full conviction forced itself upon her, that there was a restless, oppressive sense of irritation abroad among the people; a thunderous atmosphere, morally as well as physically, around her. From every narrow lane opening out on Marlborough Street came up a low distant roar, as of myriads of fierce indignant voices. The inhabitants of each poor squalid dwelling were gathered round the doors and windows, if indeed they were not actually standing in the middle of the narrow ways—all with looks intent towards one point. Marlborough Street itself was the focus of all those human eyes, that betrayed intensest interest of various kinds; some fierce with anger, some lowering with relentless threats! some dilated with fear, or imploring entreaty; and as Margaret reached the small side-entrance by the folding doors, in the great dead wall of Marlborough mill-yard, and waited the porter’s answer to the bell, she looked round and heard the first long far-off roll of the tempest;—saw the first slow-surging wave of the dark crowd come, with its threatening crest, tumble over, and retreat, at the far end of the street, which a moment ago seemed so full of repressed noise, but which now was ominously still; all these circumstances forced themselves on Margaret’s notice, but did not sink down into her pre-occupied heart. She did not know what they meant—what was their deep significance; while she did know, did feel the keen sharp pressure of the knife that was soon to stab her through and through by leaving her motherless. She was trying to realise that, in order that, when it came, she might be ready to comfort her father.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
A struck mill in this period was a genuine crisis — not merely economic but constitutional, in a loose sense. The machine stopping was a kind of inversion of the natural order as the masters experienced it — they had built this world, and here it stood inert. Gaskell is acute about the union's strategy — and the fragility of that strategy. Bessy tells Margaret exactly what the committee intended — and it is, frankly, sophisticated labour organizing for any era. "They would try and get speech o' th' knobsticks, and coax 'em, and reason wi' 'em, and m'appen warn 'em off; but whatever came, the Committee charged all members o' th' Union to lie down and die, if need were, without striking a blow." Disciplined non-violence as a calculated appeal to public sympathy — a century before Gandhi, a century before the civil rights marches. And Boucher wrecks it. One man, acting from desperate hunger, destroys the moral architecture of the whole campaign.
