James Blackstone (social_historian)
That contradiction — between surface and substance — is one of the central anxieties of the Victorian social order. And Dickens dramatises it with extraordinary comic precision in the figure of Sloppy. Sloppy has been dressed up — expensively, carefully — in a suit of black, with the tailor under explicit instructions to conceal every structural button. The idea is to make this rough, ungainly young man look like he belongs in a respectable drawing room. The result is magnificent failure. Dickens writes that the frailties of Sloppy's form proved more powerful than the strongest resources of tailoring science. "he now stood before the Council, a perfect Argus in the way of buttons: shining and winking and gleaming and twinkling out of a hundred of those eyes of bright metal, at the dazzled spectators." Argus — the hundred-eyed monster of Greek myth — pressed into service as a joke about waistcoat buttons. That is Dickens's comic register operating at full stretch — and there is something genuinely affectionate in it. The joke isn't at Sloppy's expense — it's at the expense of the idea that you can dress poverty into respectability by spending enough money on buttons.

What strikes me about that passage is the sheer accumulation — the verb list: shining and winking and gleaming and twinkling. James Blackstone (social_historian)
If the Sloppy passage gives us comedy about appearance, the Riderhood passage gives us something far darker — the logic of blackmail, which is to say, the logic of evidence. Riderhood has watched Bradley Headstone commit an act of savage violence, and he has kept the proof. He describes the whole thing with this relentless, almost prosecutorial specificity — where Bradley changed his clothes, where he hid them, how he staged the river dip to explain the wet state of himself. And then comes the climax of that speech. "I've got your Bargeman's clothes, tore this way and that way with the scuffle, stained green with the grass, and spattered all over with what bust from the blows. I've got them, and I've got you." That last short sentence — I've got them, and I've got you — is the whole logic of blackmail reduced to five words. The evidence and the person become equivalent — to possess the clothes is to possess the man. And Riderhood doesn't want justice — he is entirely explicit that he does not care whether Eugene Wrayburn is alive or dead. He wants to drain Bradley dry.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that anxiety was entirely grounded in lived social reality — which is worth dwelling on. The testamentary condition attaching an inheritance to a specific marriage was not merely a literary device. It was a recognisable legal mechanism — and a widely despised one — by which Victorian patriarchs extended their control from beyond the grave. So when Harmon says he came back 'mistrustful of my father's intention in thrusting that marriage on me,' he's naming something legally precise, not merely emotional. His father's will was a document of coercion dressed up as inheritance. The son understands this perfectly well — and the scheme he devises is his attempt to carve out some small autonomy within a system designed to deny him any.
