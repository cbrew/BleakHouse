James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right that the Prelude sets the terms — but I want to put some very specific stone into that geological metaphor. When Eliot writes Dorothea into 1829 Middlemarch, the Married Women's Property Act is still thirty years away. A woman upon marriage became, in law, a feme covert — her legal personality subsumed into her husband's. She could not own property independently, could not enter contracts, could not initiate legal proceedings. So when Eleanor talks about a closed system — yes, it's a narrative structure, but it's also a statute. The weather, to push Eleanor's metaphor, was manufactured in Parliament and in the common law courts. And there's a passage near the end of the novel — the famous 'unhistoric acts' passage — where Eliot makes the pressure audible in a way that the legal record simply can't. She writes: 'The growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been, is half owing to the number who lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs.' Verified (95%) What the law erased, Eliot insists on making count — that's the historical argument made into form.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and notice what Eliot does formally with that sentence. It's phrased as a conditional — 'if we had a keen vision' — which means we're immediately aware that we don't. The sentence enacts the limitation it's describing — it shows you the edge of perception while standing inside the fog. And that's what the narrative technique of the whole novel does — free indirect discourse, the narrator dipping in and out of consciousness, showing you the gap between what characters think they see and what's actually there. Dorothea in the early chapters is a perfect case — she's not stupid, she's ardent, but her ardour keeps landing on the wrong things. And Eliot shows us Dorothea's perspective from the inside with enormous sympathy — and simultaneously shows us its limits.
