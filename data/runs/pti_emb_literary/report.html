Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about Forster's opening is that he doesn't give us a character — he gives us a cosmology. The sky doesn't merely hover over Chandrapore — it governs. Forster writes that the sky settles everything — not only climates and seasons but when the earth shall be beautiful. That word — settles — carries legal weight. It's the word you use for disputes, for estates, for final verdicts. Forster is telling us, before we've met a soul, that in this world the final arbiter is not human. And then comes the moment that I think is the most quietly thrilling sentence in the opening chapters. "Only in the south, where a group of fists and fingers are thrust up through the soil, is the endless expanse interrupted. These fists and fingers are the Marabar Hills, containing the extraordinary caves." Fists and fingers — that image is violent, almost geological aggression. And then — after all that force — Forster simply drops the word extraordinary, and moves on. It's the restraint that makes it devastating.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to come at this from a slightly different angle — because what Forster is doing politically in this opening is as precise as anything he does aesthetically. The novel's second chapter moves us into the social world of Chandrapore — the club, the civil station, the bungalows. But by opening with sky and geology, Forster has already placed human affairs in a bracket — as something provisional, temporary, and rather small. Later, he returns to this idea with brilliant precision. He writes of a squirrel clinging to a wall while the gentlemen leave a bungalow in the heat. "It matters so little to the majority of living beings what the minority, that calls itself human, desires or decides." That parenthesis — that calls itself human — is devastating. It doesn't say the humans — it says the minority that calls itself human, as if the designation is self-awarded and not entirely convincing. In 1924, when the British Empire governed a quarter of the globe's population on precisely such self-awarded authority, that is not a neutral observation. It is a quietly lethal sentence.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Forster is precise about the mechanism too — and historically, the civil lines were precisely this kind of spatial technology. After the 1857 uprising, the colonial administration literally redesigned Indian cities to separate European from Indian quarters — wider roads, right-angle grids, open sightlines. The architecture of surveillance. Forster names it with precision: victorious generals. And that one remembered 'case' — the Indian gentleman turned back at the gate — that's doing enormous work. 'Only one case among thousands of visits to hundreds of officials, but its fame spread wide.' That is how intimidation operates at a systemic level — you don't need to personally humiliate every Indian doctor. One story is enough. The fame does the work.

Mr. McBryde, the District Superintendent of Police, was the most reflective and best educated of the Chandrapore officials. He had read and thought a good deal, and, owing to a somewhat unhappy marriage, had evolved a complete philosophy of life. There was much of the cynic about him, but nothing of the bully; he never lost his temper or grew rough, and he received Aziz with courtesy, was almost reassuring. “I have to detain you until you get bail,” he said, “but no doubt your friends will be applying for it, and of course they will be allowed to visit you, under regulations. James Blackstone (social_historian)
McBryde is, I think, one of Forster's most unsettling creations precisely because he is not a brute. The narrator is quite deliberate: 'There was much of the cynic about him, but nothing of the bully; he never lost his temper or grew rough.' He's courteous. Almost reassuring, the narrator tells us. He receives Aziz 'with courtesy, was almost reassuring.' And the courtesy makes it worse, not better — because it dresses the arbitrary detention in the language of professional procedure. Then Forster gives us McBryde's foundational belief — his 'theory about climatic zones.' 'All unfortunate natives are criminals at heart, for the simple reason that they live south of latitude 30.' The scientific register is doing ideological work there — 'latitude 30' gives racism the clothing of geography. And then the killer detail: McBryde himself was born at Karachi — south of latitude 30 — and the narrator notes he would 'sometimes admit as much with a sad, quiet smile.'

A rift had opened between them after the trial when Cyril had not joined in his procession; those advocacies of the girl had increased it; then came the post-cards from Venice, so cold, so unfriendly that all agreed that something was wrong; and finally, after a silence, the expected letter from Hampstead. Mahmoud Ali was with him at the time. “Some news that will surprise you. I am to marry someone whom you know. . .” He did not read further. “Here it comes, answer for me——” and he threw it to Mahmoud Ali. Subsequent letters he destroyed unopened. It was the end of a foolish experiment. And though sometimes at the back of his mind he felt that Fielding had made sacrifices for him, it was now all confused with his genuine hatred of the English. “I am an Indian at last,” he thought, standing motionless in the rain.
