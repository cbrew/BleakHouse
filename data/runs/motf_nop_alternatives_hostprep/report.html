Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I want to agree with you about the sentence — it is extraordinary — but I think the word 'diagnosis' actually gives the game away. A diagnosis implies a pathology — something deviant about Maggie that needs explaining. But what Eliot is actually doing — and this is what makes her so radical — is showing us the production of that hunger. Maggie does not arrive in the novel with desires already formed — she is surrounded by a world of Dodson aunts and anxious mothers and a father who loves her but cannot protect her. Her hunger, her intensity — it is shaped precisely by what she is denied. There's a moment when Maggie cuts off her own hair — that wild, impulsive act — and Eliot shows us Mrs. Tulliver's horror, not at Maggie's pain, but at what the neighbours will think. That is not a mother failing her daughter — that is a woman who has been so thoroughly colonised by respectability that she cannot see her child at all. To call Maggie's hunger a property of her soul is to do exactly what that world does to her — to make her the problem.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'd push back — gently — on that. Eliot is not in the business of producing victims — she is in the business of producing people.
