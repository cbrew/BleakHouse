### The Law's Long Shadow: Property, Debt, and the Tulliver Collapse (deep_dive)

[Host]

  But now we want to pull back and look at the structural forces — law, debt, property — that make that world so precarious.  (@0.98x *law,debt,property*)
  The Tulliver bankruptcy is not a subplot — it is the novel's hinge.  (@0.97x *hinge*)
  James, I want to start with you, because I think the legal machinery here is something readers often take for granted without fully understanding what it meant.  (<800ms> @0.98x *legal machinery*)

[James Blackstone]
  Yes, and it's worth being precise about what Tulliver actually loses — and how that loss was structured by law.  (+220ms @0.96x *precise,loses*)
  When Tulliver is declared bankrupt, the mill and the land attached to it pass to his creditors — most decisively to Wakem, who acquires them.  (@0.96x *Wakem*)
  What's devastating — and legally accurate for the period — is that Tulliver then becomes a hired tenant in his own mill.  (@0.95x *hired tenant,own mill*)
  He manages the very property that was his inheritance, now at Wakem's pleasure and for Wakem's profit.  (@0.96x *inheritance*)
  The early Victorian insolvency regime offered no real rehabilitation — it was structured around punishment and the satisfaction of creditors, in that order.  (@0.96x *punishment,rehabilitation*)
  George Eliot understood that precisely, and she dramatises the humiliation with surgical accuracy.  (<1500ms> @0.96x *humiliation,surgical accuracy*)

[Eleanor Hartley]

  There's that extraordinary scene where the bailiffs come and the household goods are inventoried — the furniture, the linen, even Mrs. Tulliver's prized china.  (*inventoried,china*)
  Eliot slows the narrative right down there.  (@1.01x)
  Each object listed is a small annihilation of a life that was built up with enormous effort and pride.  (@0.99x *annihilation,pride*)
  The narrator observes that Mrs. Tulliver had brought those things as part of her identity into the marriage — they were hers in a way the law simply didn't recognise.  (@0.99x *identity,law*)

[Caroline Woodcourt]
