Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It is a beautiful passage. I won't deny that for a moment. But I'd ask us to look at precisely what produces that beauty — because it is produced by an act of cruelty. The old man won't allow sixpence for coach-money to get the child to the steamboat. Sixpence. And that poverty — that deliberate, punitive withholding — is what creates the shivering box, the scanty clothes, the child who has to be warmed by strangers. The Boffins' tenderness is real. But it exists because the system — here embodied by Old Harmon's patriarchal tyranny — has made tenderness the only recourse. And Boffin doesn't shy away from telling us how that tyranny operated. He gives us an extraordinary detail about standing up to the old man — repeatedly — and paying for it. "Dropped her." Two words. Boffin doesn't dramatise it. He just states it — and the flatness of the statement is the horror. The violence against Mrs Boffin — a woman who had the temerity to defend a child — is the founding act of this entire plot.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right that it's music — but I'd say it's something older than music. It's the rhythm of moral examination — the kind one finds in the seventeenth century confessional tradition. What strikes me most is that Harmon returns not for the money, but in spite of it. He describes 'knowing of nothing but wretchedness that my father's wealth had ever brought about' — and that is not a socialist's complaint. It is the complaint of a man whose moral formation has been poisoned — not by a system, but by a particular human failure: a father's cruelty. And what does Harmon do with that inheritance of moral damage? He turns it into a test — not a selfish one, but a genuinely protective one. He fears, as he says, 'perpetuating the fate that seemed to have fallen on my father's riches — the fate that they should lead to nothing but evil.' That is a man accepting moral responsibility across generations — which is the noblest kind.
