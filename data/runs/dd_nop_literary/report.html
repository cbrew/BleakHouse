Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The opening gambit of this novel is one of the great structural jokes in Victorian fiction — and I mean that with complete admiration. Eliot begins in medias res — we're already in the casino, already watching this woman at the roulette table, before we know anything about who she is or how she got there. And the very first sentence of the novel is a question — the narrator, or Deronda, asking: was she beautiful or not beautiful? "Was she beautiful or not beautiful? and what was the secret of form or expression which gave the dynamic quality to her glance?" Verified (100%) What Eliot is doing there is extraordinary — she's making us see Gwendolen through a male gaze that she immediately makes us distrust. We're invited to objectify this woman at the very moment we're being warned that objectification is the subject of the novel. It's a trap she sets for the reader — and most of us walk right into it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What I'd add is that the casino setting is doing a great deal of social work before Eliot has written a single word of backstory. A young English gentlewoman gambling at a Continental casino in the 1860s was a figure fraught with ambiguity — not exactly disreputable, but not quite safe either. The Continental resorts — Baden-Baden, Leubronn as Eliot calls it — were places where class rules loosened, where the normal English codes of conduct were, shall we say, on holiday. So when we see Gwendolen at that table, we're already being told that this is a woman who exists at the edge of what society will permit her. And crucially, she knows it — and she chooses to be there anyway.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I re-read that opening is how physically vivid Gwendolen is. Eliot gives us this image of her — green and white, tall, with a kind of reckless self-command — and you feel her presence before you understand it. There's a phrase Eliot uses — that Gwendolen had "that peculiar smile which was observable in her whole person" — and it's almost uncanny. A smile that lives in the whole body, not just the face. That tells you immediately that Gwendolen performs — that she is always, consciously or not, an actress in her own life. And then Deronda does something that completely unnerves her — he watches her back. For the first time in the novel, someone is looking at Gwendolen not with admiration, but with — what exactly? Judgment? Compassion? Eliot won't quite say, and neither will Deronda.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That moment of being looked at — it's the pivot of the whole opening, isn't it. Gwendolen loses at roulette, and she loses badly — and Eliot frames it as a moral disclosure, not just a run of bad luck. The narrator draws that connection explicitly — that this woman who believes herself exceptional, fated to win, is shown by the game itself to be ordinary, subject to chance like everyone else. And then, caught in the humiliation of losing, she looks up and finds Deronda watching her — and she retreats. For a woman who is never embarrassed, this is remarkable — it tells us that Deronda's gaze has a quality that ordinary admiration doesn't. It sees her.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to press on something Caroline raised — this idea of Gwendolen as a performer. When we meet her properly — in the chapters set back in England, before the casino — she's the undisputed queen of her small social world. She's charming, she's witty, she's vain — and she's also genuinely intelligent, which makes her situation all the more punishing. Because what are the actual options available to a clever, beautiful woman of her class in 1865? Marry well, or fall. Gwendolen knows this perfectly well — she's just gambling that she can hold fate off with sheer force of personality.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And this is where Grandcourt comes in — Henleigh Grandcourt — and the novel really starts to tighten its grip. He's one of the most terrifying figures in Victorian fiction, and Eliot doesn't make him a villain in any obvious way. He's cold, he's languid, he barely speaks — and yet his will operates in every room he enters like a change in atmospheric pressure. What breaks my heart — every single time — is how clearly Gwendolen sees what he is. She's not deceived. She marries him anyway — because the alternative, as James says, is to fall — and that makes every chapter that follows a tragedy of clear-eyed surrender.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The way Eliot handles that decision — the proposal, the refusal, and then the reversal — is astonishing craft. Gwendolen actually turns Grandcourt down the first time — there's a scene where she simply walks away from him in the garden. And then her family's financial ruin intervenes — her stepfather's death, the loss of income — and she has no ground left to stand on. She comes back to Grandcourt and accepts him, and Eliot describes that moment with a kind of terrible clarity. The narrator notes that Gwendolen's acceptance was something like the numbness of a person who has taken a resolute step — not peace, not happiness, but the terrible quiet after decision. That's not romance. That's a woman calculating the cost of survival.

So we have, from the very first pages, a woman who sees clearly, chooses knowingly, and pays for it — which is one of Eliot's most devastating moral structures. And threaded through all of this — watching, wondering, never quite explaining himself — is Daniel Deronda. It happened that the very vividness of his impressions had often made him the more enigmatic to his friends, and had contributed to an apparent indefiniteness in his sentiments. His early-wakened sensibility and reflectiveness had developed into a many-sided sympathy, which threatened to hinder any persistent course of action: as soon as he took up any antagonism, though only in thought, he seemed to himself like the Sabine warriors in the memorable story—with nothing to meet his spear but flesh of his flesh, and objects that he loved. His imagination had so wrought itself to the habit of seeing things as they probably appeared to others, that a strong partisanship, unless it were against an immediate oppression, had become an insincerity for him. His plenteous, flexible sympathy had ended by falling into one current with that reflective analysis which tends to neutralize sympathy. Few men were able to keep themselves clearer of vices than he; yet he hated vices mildly, being used to think of them less in the abstract than as a part of mixed human natures having an individual history, which it was the bent of his mind to trace with understanding and pity. With the same innate balance he was fervidly democratic in his feeling for the multitude, and yet, through his affections and imagination, intensely conservative; voracious of speculations on government and religion, yet loth to part with long-sanctioned forms which, for him, were quick with memories and sentiments that no argument could lay dead. We fall on the leaning side; and Deronda suspected himself of loving too well the losing causes of the world. Martyrdom changes sides, and he was in danger of changing with it, having a strong repugnance to taking up that clue of success which the order of the world often forces upon us and makes it treason against the common weal to reject. And yet his fear of falling into an unreasoning narrow hatred made a check for him: he apologized for the heirs of privilege; he shrank with dislike from the loser’s bitterness and the denunciatory tone of the unaccepted innovator. A too reflective and diffusive sympathy was in danger of paralyzing in him that indignation against wrong and that selectness of fellowship which are the conditions of moral force; and in the last few years of confirmed manhood he had become so keenly aware of this that what he most longed for was either some external event, or some inward light, that would urge him into a definite line of action, and compress his wandering energy. He was ceasing to care for knowledge—he had no ambition for practice—unless they could both be gathered up into one current with his emotions; and he dreaded, as if it were a dwelling-place of lost souls, that dead anatomy of culture which turns the universe into a mere ceaseless answer to queries, and knows, not everything, but everything else about everything—as if one should be ignorant of nothing concerning the scent of violets except the scent itself for which one had no nostril. But how and whence was the needed event to come?—the influence that would justify partiality, and make him what he longed to be, yet was unable to make himself—an organic part of social life, instead of roaming in it like a yearning disembodied spirit, stirred with a vague social passion, but without fixed local habitation to render fellowship real? To make a little difference for the better was what he was not contented to live without; but how to make it? It is one thing to see your road, another to cut it. He found some of the fault in his birth and the way he had been brought up, which had laid no special demands on him and had given him no fixed relationship except one of a doubtful kind; but he did not attempt to hide from himself that he had fallen into a meditative numbness, and was gliding farther and farther from that life of practically energetic sentiment which he would have proclaimed (if he had been inclined to proclaim anything) to be the best of all life, and for himself the only way worth living. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the social weight of that position is immense, and I think we risk underestimating it if we read Deronda purely as a philosophical archetype. He is a ward — effectively the ward of Sir Hugo Mallinger — and in Victorian England that status carried a very precise social meaning. He has no legal surname that he can claim with certainty, no lineage he can trace, no estate that is his by right. In a society where identity was inseparable from property and inheritance, that is not a minor inconvenience — it is a fundamental dispossession. Eliot captures this in Deronda's boyhood scene — when the young Daniel asks Sir Hugo, almost trembling, whether he is Sir Hugo's son. And Sir Hugo deflects the question with affection but without honesty — which is its own kind of cruelty, however kindly intended. The institution of wardship and the laws around illegitimacy in the 1860s and 70s were not abstractions — they determined who you were, legally and socially.
