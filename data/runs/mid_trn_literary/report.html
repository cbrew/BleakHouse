Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
For me, it's the narrator — that voice that seems to hold the whole of human experience without ever becoming cold about it. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I come at it differently — as a historian, what grips me is how precisely Eliot maps the social machinery of provincial England. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and what strikes me about it is how legally precise Will's argument is, even though he's writing from a position of apparent dependency. He doesn't deny the obligation — he reframes it. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a darker version of this same dynamic later in the novel — with Bulstrode. But there, the benefaction was built on fraud, and the obligations it created were poisoned from the start. Eliot gives us this remarkable compressed history of how respectability is assembled over decades, layer by layer. And then — in a single sentence — she demolishes it. "When this respectability had lasted undisturbed for nearly thirty years — when all that preceded it had long lain benumbed in the consciousness — that past had risen and immersed his thought as if with the terrible irruption of a new sense overburthening the feeble being." "Terrible irruption" — that's the language of geological catastrophe, of a volcano breaking through. Thirty years of careful construction — undone not by external exposure first, but by the past forcing itself back into consciousness.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That phrase 'unmanifested perfections' is doing something architecturally brilliant. It's not simply that Dorothea is naive — it's that she is an active creator of Casaubon. She is projecting a figure onto a blank screen, and she's doing it with tremendous imaginative energy. To those who have looked at Rome with the quickening power of a knowledge which breathes a growing soul into all historic shapes, and traces out the suppressed transitions which unite all contrasts, Rome may still be the spiritual centre and interpreter of the world. But let them conceive one more historical contrast: the gigantic broken revelations of that Imperial and Papal city thrust abruptly on the notions of a girl who had been brought up in English and Swiss Puritanism, fed on meagre Protestant histories and on art chiefly of the hand-screen sort; a girl whose ardent nature turned all her small allowance of knowledge into principles, fusing her actions into their mould, and whose quick emotions gave the most abstract things the quality of a pleasure or a pain; a girl who had lately become a wife, and from the enthusiastic acceptance of untried duty found herself plunged in tumultuous preoccupation with her personal lot. The weight of unintelligible Rome might lie easily on bright nymphs to whom it formed a background for the brilliant picnic of Anglo-foreign society; but Dorothea had no such defence against deep impressions. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the marriage, of course, provides no insulation at all. Eliot is very precise about what Dorothea has actually married: not a person, but an idea of devotion. What I find myself returning to, at the end of the novel, is the sheer institutional weight that crushes these people. Lydgate's ambitions are destroyed not by his own weakness alone but by a medical system that has no place for reform, a financial world that has no patience for idealism. Dorothea is hemmed in by inheritance law, by the terms of a dead man's will, by the sheer machinery of Victorian property.
