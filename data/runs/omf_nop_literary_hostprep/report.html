Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that's where I want to push gently on James's framing — because you say prosecution, and I completely understand why, but I think it undersells what Dickens is doing formally. A prosecutor presents evidence and keeps himself out of it. What Dickens does is the opposite — he contaminates the prose itself with the substance he's describing. The mud isn't a backdrop. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I take the formal point — I do — but I want to resist the word 'modernist' just slightly. Because if we start calling Dickens a proto-modernist, we risk making this novel about literary history rather than the people dying in its pages. The dust-mounds at the centre of the novel — old Harmon's mountains of refuse on the edge of the city — those weren't symbolic first. They were real. Dust contractors in Victorian London accumulated massive fortunes from sorting through the city's waste — ash, bones, rags, worse. Old Harmon is a real type. And when Dickens makes his fortune the engine of a plot about identity and inheritance, he's not reaching for a symbol — he's pointing at something everyone in his readership already understood as obscene.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that reframes the whole title, doesn't it. 'Our Mutual Friend' — on the surface it refers to John Harmon, the dead man around whom all these characters' fates are arranged. But 'mutual' is doing something deeper — it's the word for a connection that flows both ways, that isn't transactional, that doesn't have a creditor and a debtor. Every relationship in the novel is being tested against that standard. The Lammles fail it spectacularly. The Boffins pass it. Lizzie and Eugene — just barely, and only because she pulls him out of the river. It's an earned ordinariness. Not triumph — endurance, and maybe that's harder.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd put it differently — though not, I think, incompatibly. 'Mutual' in the Victorian financial sense meant shared liability — a mutual society is one where you're all equally exposed. The Veneering social world is built precisely on the fiction of mutual connection — everyone reflects everyone else, no one actually knows anyone. So the title might be Dickens's darkest joke: in a society organized around property, 'our mutual friend' is money itself. The one thing everyone shares. The one thing that connects the Harmon dust-heaps to the Veneering table to Lizzie's river.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And yet — and this is what I keep returning to — Dickens doesn't leave us with that joke. He leaves us with Jenny Wren on her rooftop, with Lizzie on the river at night, with Betty Higden dying on the road rather than accept the workhouse. These are people the system has completely failed — and they are the most morally serious people in the novel. So I think Dickens is putting both things to us simultaneously, without resolving them. The system is broken. And within it, some people choose to be mutual. Both things are true.
