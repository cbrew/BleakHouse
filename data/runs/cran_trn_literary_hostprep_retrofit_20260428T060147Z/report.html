Gaskell gives Miss Pole the argument and gives Miss Matty the grammar. Miss Pole wins the debate. Miss Matty wins the sentence. And the sentence is the novel. Gaskell is a novelist whose deepest effects are tense effects — not plot, not character, but tense — and the pluperfect 'had looked forward' at the end of c12:p34 is not grammatical tidiness but the novel's most quietly devastating technical choice: the tense of permanent past, of anticipatory joy now sealed off forever.

Caroline Woodcourt
The dashes in c3:p87 are not a failure of speech but the most sophisticated rhetorical instrument in the passage — more sophisticated than anything in the surrounding narration. Miss Jenkyns uses them to do, in thirty words, what it takes Miss Matty an entire late-night conversation to achieve: she both discloses feeling and simultaneously provides her audience with the means to politely ignore it. The dashes are the mechanism for offering and withdrawing intimacy in the same breath, and crucially, the narrator Mary performs the same operation on a large scale throughout the novel — the dashes run all the way through.
Key points:
  • The three passages form a triptych about the grammar of self-correction, always moving downward and away from the self's own intensity — a structural pattern Gaskell repeats from a single utterance in c3:p87 to a whole life's thought-report in c12:p34.
  • Miss Jenkyns's dash-laden speech in c3:p87 performs five distinct grammatical moves to deliver one piece of news, enacting a structure of approach-retreat-approach that mirrors the shape of the women's lives under social constraint.

Disagreement angles:
  • Against an Austen-comparison reading: Austen's irony is architectonic and reveals a system, whereas Gaskell's irony is intimate and almost private — Mary's observation that she and Miss Matty were more thankful the robbers had left than that they had never married undercuts Miss Pole's speech not with a witty reversal but with a detail of domestic psychology. Austen shows us the system; Gaskell shows us the person who has quietly declined to participate in it.
  • Against a purely elegiac or sentimental reading: reading c12:p34 as a pathos set-piece makes Miss Matty a victim, but the structural parallel with c3:p87 reveals something more active — both women approach feeling, retreat from it, and approach again from a safer angle. That is not just sadness; it is a skill, a form of emotional self-management developed under conditions of social constraint. The elegiac reading makes them victims; the structural reading makes them practitioners.
James Blackstone
Miss Matty is not remembering a romantic hope when she recalls wanting to marry — she is remembering a time when she expected to have the legal protection that coverture, for all its horrors, actually provided for women without independent means. Her quiet dignity and her suppressed longing are two sides of the same legal coin: the feme sole condition gives her personhood and strips her of protection simultaneously.
Key points:
  • The word 'protector' in c12:p34 carries precise legal weight: under coverture doctrine, a married woman's legal personality was subsumed into her husband's, making her a feme covert and erasing her independent legal existence. Miss Matty yearned for the very instrument of her own legal annihilation.
  • Miss Pole's witty anti-marriage speech in c12:p33 accidentally articulates the argument that reformers like Barbara Leigh Smith Bodichon were making in print the very next year — that coverture was the mechanism of women's social infantilisation — but Miss Pole lacks the legal vocabulary to know this.
  • The passive gerund 'being married' in Miss Pole's speech mirrors the legal structure of coverture itself: the agent of the marrying is suppressed, encoding the very female passivity Miss Pole claims women should escape. She is angrier than her grammar can contain.
  • Miss Matty's famous quiet dignity is partially constituted by her legal condition as a feme sole — she is a legal person, able to hold property and make contracts — but the bank failure reveals this as curse as well as freedom: she has no husband legally obligated to maintain her.

Eleanor Hartley (novelist_and_craft_teacher)
It seals something off. That's the function of the pluperfect — it marks the past of the past, an action completed before the narrative present even began. And Gaskell reaches for it here with absolute precision. The passage gives us Mary Smith quietly noting that Miss Matty privately disagrees with Miss Pole's triumphant anti-matrimonial speech. And then Gaskell slips into something close to free indirect thought — we're inside Miss Matty's mind — and we get this: 'she remembered the time when she had looked forward to being married as much as any one.' That 'had looked forward' — not 'looked forward,' not 'wanted' — places the hope somewhere that can no longer be reached from where Miss Matty now stands. It's not grief exactly. It's the grammar of foreclosure. And what makes it so devastating is that Gaskell doesn't linger there — she moves straight on, and that restraint does more work than any extended lament could.

James Blackstone (legal_and_social_historian)
I want to be precise about what kind of hope is sealed off in that tense, because I think it matters enormously. The passage is framed, just before that sentence, by Miss Matty's private reflection that a husband would be 'a great protector against thieves, burglars, and ghosts.' That list — thieves, burglars, and ghosts — is comic, of course. But it isn't merely comic. Under the doctrine of coverture — which remained largely intact in the 1840s and 1850s when Gaskell was writing — a married woman had no independent legal existence. She could not own property in her own name, could not enter contracts, could not sue or be sued independently. As a feme sole — an unmarried woman — Miss Matty technically has legal personhood. And yet she's remembering that she once wanted to surrender precisely that — to be absorbed into a husband's legal identity. So when Eleanor says the pluperfect seals off a hope — I'd ask: a hope for what, exactly? Is it the romantic hope, or the hope for the very specific legal shelter that marriage represented — particularly for a woman facing financial precarity alone?

Let me read it, because you can't really argue about this without hearing it. 'I have been so much startled—no, I've not been at all startled—don't mind me, my dear Miss Jessie—I've been very much surprised—in fact, I've had a caller, whom you knew once, my dear Miss Jessie.' Eleanor Hartley
Cranford's deepest subject is partial knowledge affectionately held. Mary never knows everything — she supposes, she overhears, she reads letters not addressed to her. And Gaskell's structural genius is that this epistemic limitation produces the novel's warmth rather than undermining it. Every great comic novel about community is secretly about exclusion, and Mary Smith is always the perpetual guest. That's the position Gaskell puts us in too.
Key points:
  • The parenthetical in c12:p59 — '(but I had been brought up near the Avon in Warwickshire, so that flowing noise sounded like home)' — is a masterpiece of form enacting content: the interruption of the sentence performs the involuntary, acoustic mechanism of homesickness itself. You cannot paraphrase it without destroying what it does.
  • The carter's testimony in c3:p50 uses continuous and participial verb aspects — 'was a-reading,' 'a-waiting,' 'came toddling' — to create what I call the grammar of imminence: the disaster is held in permanent syntactic suspension, never resolving, which is precisely what makes it devastating.
  • Cranford is not a novel about a community. It is a novel about the experience of being told things. Every passage is a relay of transmission, and the transmission verbs — 'I seed it myself,' 'ticketed in a frail hand,' 'sounded like home' — are the real architecture. Mary Smith is the perpetual guest, always just arriving or just leaving, and Gaskell puts the reader in exactly that position.
  • Children appear in three of the four passages as grammatical objects rather than subjects — 'a little lass,' 'carrying my child' — and this mirrors the condition of the women in the novel. The people who matter most affectively are the ones most consistently acted-upon in the syntax. I call this the grammar of Cranford's tenderness.

The 'small rebellions of kindness' are not rebellions against men — they are acts performed for men, at cost to the women themselves, and Gaskell has documented this with such syntactic precision that the reader who looks closely enough can see the structure clearly enough to grieve it; she has compressed the entire Victorian marriage economy into two words, each three letters long, separated by a slash.
Key points:
  • Each of the three passages performs a structural self-correction at the syntactic level — someone begins to say something true, then swerves — and this is Gaskell operating at the level of grammar, not just theme
  • The subjunctive construction 'as if she were afraid' in the When/If passage is a deliberate epistemic choice: Gaskell writes hypothesis, not fact, giving Miss Matty grammatical deniability and keeping the narrator humble — the difference between 'because she was' and 'as if she were' is the difference between a writer who reports and one who understands that some truths must be spoken obliquely
