Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund is absolutely right that the passage is devastating — but I'd push on where the blame is located. Dickens frames this as a question of individual character — Murdstone's failure of imagination, his failure of kindness. But Murdstone is not an aberration — he is a type, a recognisable Victorian patriarch who believes discipline is love's superior form. The system rewards men like Murdstone — it gives them legal authority over women and children, property over persons. And look at what happens to Clara Copperfield in that passage — she watches her son standing scared and strange, and the word is not spoken. She cannot speak it — not because she doesn't feel it, but because Murdstone has already colonised the domestic space entirely. That silence is not individual failure — it is the structure of mid-Victorian marriage made visible in a single agonising moment.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And yet — and this is what I find so thrilling about the novel's construction — Dickens is equally capable of sheer comic delight in the very next breath. Because this is not a tragedy — it is a novel, in the great capacious Victorian sense, and it holds grief and comedy in the same hand simultaneously. Think of Miss Mowcher — the description of her entrance in Chapter twenty-four is one of the great comic set-pieces in English prose. David is waiting for her to appear in the doorway — and nothing appears. "There came waddling round a sofa which stood between me and it, a pursy dwarf, of about forty or forty-five, with a very large head and face, a pair of roguish grey eyes, and such extremely little arms, that, to enable herself to lay a finger archly against her snub nose, as she ogled Steerforth, she was obliged to meet the finger half-way, and lay her nose against it." The nose meets the finger half-way — it is the funniest piece of physical comedy in the novel, and it is entirely written for the voice. You cannot appreciate that sentence on the page the way you can when you perform it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver's right that it's brilliantly funny — but I want to notice something about the energy behind the comedy. Miss Mowcher is described with an almost invasive physical specificity — throat she had none, waist she had none, legs she had none worth mentioning. The comic catalogue runs through every part of her body as though cataloguing a deficiency. And she exists — at least in this introduction — entirely to serve Steerforth's vanity, ogling him, catering to him. The novel will complicate her later — she earns her dignity — but here she is framed by the powerful young man's drawing room. Even comedy in Dickens encodes a social position.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I wonder if we might also pause over Aunt Betsey's great confession — the passage about her lost investments — because it shows a quite different register of the novel's comedy. It is comedy in the service of genuine pathos — which is Dickens at his most characteristic. She recounts her financial ruin in the third person — as though speaking of a stranger — which is itself a small masterpiece of self-protection. "Betsey funded her property for some time, and then, by the advice of her man of business, laid it out on landed security. That did very well, and returned very good interest, till Betsey was paid off. I am talking of Betsey as if she was a man-of-war." "So she took her pigs to a foreign market; and a very bad market it turned out to be. First, she lost in the mining way, and then she lost in the diving way — fishing up treasure, or some such Tom Tiddler nonsense — and then she lost in the mining way again, and, last of all, to set the thing entirely to rights, she lost in the banking way." Tom Tiddler nonsense — that one phrase does the work of a whole chapter of financial satire. And the way she accumulates the losses — mining, then diving, then mining again, then banking — is the rhythm of a woman who will not be defeated by self-pity. She names the disaster completely, and then she closes it: least said, soonest mended.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Yes, and the place to start — really the foundational image of institutional cruelty in this novel — is the placard. David is made to wear a sign at Salem House that reads 'Take care of him. He bites.' It reduces a child to a dangerous animal — and the school enforces it with bureaucratic relish. But what Dickens captures — and this is the psychological genius of the passage — is not just the humiliation, but what it does to the child's mind. Listen to this — Dickens writes it in the first person, which makes it all the more devastating: 'What I suffered from that placard, nobody can imagine. Whether it was possible for people to see me or not, I always fancied that somebody was reading it.' The surveillance has been internalised. He doesn't need the gatekeeper watching him — he watches himself. And then the text does something extraordinary — David begins to believe he actually is the wild boy the placard describes.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That internalisation is real, and it speaks to the lasting damage cruelty inflicts on an impressionable character. But I'd resist the temptation to treat the gatekeeper and his wooden leg as representatives of some systematic apparatus. He is a petty tyrant — as petty tyrants have existed in every age and every arrangement of society. What matters morally — what Dickens cares about — is the individual cruelty of Murdstone, of the gatekeeper, of Miss Murdstone with her steel purse and her cold will. Speaking of Miss Murdstone — there is a scene where she refuses even the mildest comparison between David and Murdstone's infant, and it reveals the depth of her territorial malice. She declares — and Dickens gives her magnificent comic venom here — 'They are not at all alike. They are exactly unlike. They are utterly dissimilar in all respects. I hope they will ever remain so.' Three parallel negations — and then that final spite: I hope they will ever remain so.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And institutions don't need to be visibly brutal to do their damage — sometimes they operate through sheer somnolent indifference. The Doctors' Commons sequence is Dickens at his most Swiftian — and it's one of the great satirical set-pieces of the novel. David enters this court expecting the majesty of the law, and finds something closer to a gentlemen's club in light hibernation. Dickens builds to the perfect verdict, and I want to read the whole sentence because the accumulation of adjectives is the argument: 'Altogether, I have never, on any occasion, made one at such a cosey, dosey, old-fashioned, time-forgotten, sleepy-headed little family-party in all my life; and I felt it would be quite a soothing opiate to belong to it in any character—except perhaps as a suitor.' That final word — suitor — lands like a blade. For everyone inside the machine, it is cosy and warm. For the person the machine is supposed to serve, it is something else entirely.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd only add that the novel is clear-eyed about what Steerforth's power is made of — it is the power of class, of ease, of never being struck. Creakle beats every boy but Steerforth. That is not charm — that is social immunity, and David absorbs the lesson without knowing he's learning it. What this novel does — and it does it with devastating consistency — is show us how a sensitive, decent person can be educated into deference. David's education at Salem House is not in Latin or arithmetic. It's in who counts and who doesn't.

But the greatest wonder that I heard of Mr. Creakle was, there being one boy in the school on whom he never ventured to lay a hand, and that boy being J. Steerforth. Steerforth himself confirmed this when it was stated, and said that he should like to begin to see him do it. On being asked by a mild boy (not me) how he would proceed if he did begin to see him do it, he dipped a match into his phosphorus-box on purpose to shed a glare over his reply, and said he would commence by knocking him down with a blow on the forehead from the seven-and-sixpenny ink-bottle that was always on the mantelpiece. We sat in the dark for some time, breathless.

I heard that one boy, who was a coal-merchant’s son, came as a set-off against the coal-bill, and was called, on that account, ‘Exchange or Barter’—a name selected from the arithmetic book as expressing this arrangement. I heard that the table beer was a robbery of parents, and the pudding an imposition. I heard that Miss Creakle was regarded by the school in general as being in love with Steerforth; and I am sure, as I sat in the dark, thinking of his nice voice, and his fine face, and his easy manner, and his curling hair, I thought it very likely. I heard that Mr. Mell was not a bad sort of fellow, but hadn’t a sixpence to bless himself with; and that there was no doubt that old Mrs. Mell, his mother, was as poor as job. I thought of my breakfast then, and what had sounded like ‘My Charley!’ but I was, I am glad to remember, as mute as a mouse about it.
