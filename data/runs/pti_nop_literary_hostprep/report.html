Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The comedy is the point — and it's also the trap. Forster gives us that absurd syllable, 'ou-boum,' and by doing so he's almost daring you to dismiss it. But what the echo does, technically, is catastrophic — it strips all utterance of its distinctiveness. Forster tells us that whatever is said in the caves — a match struck, a prayer, a cry — returns as the same sound. He writes something to the effect that the echo begins in some indescribable way to undermine the visitor's hold on life. Now, as a narratological device, what that does is devastating — it collapses the hierarchy of language. The echo doesn't discriminate between meaning and noise, between prayer and assault, between truth and accusation. And that, formally, is exactly where the novel lives from that point forward.
