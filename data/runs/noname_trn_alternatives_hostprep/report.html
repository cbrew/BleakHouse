Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What you have to do, first of all, is resist it. Because the temptation is to rush — to ride the rhythm of the prose — and Collins has written this passage so that the rhythm itself is the trap. The setup is deceptively gentle — a father who has never quite noticed that his daughter has grown up. She romped with him in her frocks, Collins says, and then — with the deadpan nerve of a great comedian — she went on romping with him in her gowns. That single word — gowns — is where a reader has to land with full weight, because the joke is also the tragedy. And then Collins detonates the whole thing in one sentence — and you have to read it slowly, I mean very slowly, because he's earned it. 'And now, in one breathless instant, the conviction that she was a woman rushed over his mind.' He felt it in the trouble of her bosom pressed against his; in the nervous thrill of her arms clasped around his neck. The Magdalen of his innocent experience — a woman — with the master-passion of her sex in possession of her heart already. That em-dash before 'a woman' — that is where you pause so long it becomes uncomfortable. Because the discomfort is the point — a man discovering that the creature he thought he knew is a stranger. And in the same instant, the reader discovers it too.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I love what Oliver does with that passage, and he's right about the prose rhythm. But I want to stay with what actually happens in that moment — what Collins is recording, structurally. Vanstone has, until this moment, experienced Magdalen as something like his own extension — his playmate, his amusement, his creature. And the moment he recognises her sexuality, his relationship to her doesn't warm — it reclassifies. Paternal love, in this society, is always also a form of ownership — and the passage where she hides her face against his shoulder makes that frighteningly clear. Collins tells us: 'His cheek, still wet with her tears, answered for her.' Her sincerity has to be vouched for by her own body — she cannot simply speak and be believed. That's not a tender detail — that's a social condition. Vanstone's love is real and I don't want to diminish it — but it is surveillance with a kind face.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
And here, Daniel, is precisely where I must disagree — though I do so with the greatest respect for your consistency. You say her dispossession was encoded before it began. But Collins gives us something rather more troubling — a young woman who actively compounds her situation through a form of self-deception she mistakes for nobility. When Magdalen makes her extraordinary vow about Frank — that he shall not take her until she can be the making of his fortune — Collins is not simply showing us a brave girl. He is showing us a character whose capacity for grand feeling has already outrun her self-knowledge. She says — and this is a line worth attending to carefully — 'He shall take no burden, when he takes me; I promise you that!' 'I'll be the good angel of Frank's life; I'll not go a penniless girl to him, and drag him down.' That is a magnificent speech — and it is also, almost entirely, a lie she is telling herself. The real dispossession Collins traces is not economic — it is the slow erosion of her capacity to see herself clearly.

Collins writes it as suspense, as comedy almost — and it is funny, in the same way that a trap snapping shut on something living is briefly, horribly graceful.
