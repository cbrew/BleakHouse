I heard Caroline, and I'll confess there's a version of what she's saying that I find entirely compelling. But I want to push on the word 'truer.' Truer than what, precisely? James Blackstone (social_historian)
Stephen Blackpool — a mill hand, a man with nothing — is shown into Bounderby's parlour. Bounderby is eating chop and sherry. Mrs. Sparsit is nearby, netting, in what Dickens calls 'a side-saddle attitude, with one foot in a cotton stirrup.' It's a tableau of class so precisely rendered it would be funny if it weren't also an act of cruelty toward Stephen, who has to stand in that room and ask for help. Mrs. Sparsit doesn't eat because eating would make her human, and being human would undermine her social utility to Bounderby.

The thing that strikes me every time is that Dickens gives his working-class characters a physical life on the page that his middle-class characters simply don't get. Look at the Stephen Blackpool passage — and I mean really look at it, not read through it. "In the strength of his misfortune, and the energy of his distress, he fired for the moment like a proud man." That verb — 'fired' — is extraordinary. It's kinetic, it's thermal, it's gone in an instant. And then Dickens immediately extinguishes it: 'In another moment, he stood as he had stood all the time — his usual stoop upon him.' The sentence enacts the defeat it describes. That's not sentiment — that's architecture.
