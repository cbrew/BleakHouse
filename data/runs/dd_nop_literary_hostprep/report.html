James Blackstone (social_historian)
I do — and I want to say it plainly: Gwendolen Harleth is not pathological. She is rational. Modern readers — and I think this happens even in very sophisticated readings — tend to treat the gambling as a symptom of character. But Gwendolen in 1866 has no income, no property of her own, no profession, and is about to watch her family's money evaporate. The casino is not an indulgence — it is one of the very few places where a woman of her class and situation can exercise independent agency over money. The Married Women's Property Act — giving wives legal ownership of their own earnings and property — is still over a decade away when this novel is set. Eliot knows this. The novel knows this. The question is whether we know it when we're reading.
