Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline, I love that reading, and I want to push on it slightly — because I think something more technically startling is happening in that sentence. Gissing is predominantly a free indirect discourse novelist — he slides into his characters' perspectives so smoothly you often can't tell where character ends and narrator begins. But 'never had it occurred to Widdowson that a wife remains an individual' — that's the narrator breaking cover. That 'remains' is present tense, pitched above the story — it's the narrator asserting a truth independent of what any character believes. So yes, the syntax enacts the hierarchy — you're absolutely right about that — but the sentence is also a tiny act of authorial fury. The narrator steps out from behind Widdowson's bland domestic contentment and says: this is wrong. Categorically.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Both readings are persuasive, and I don't want to be churlish about the prose — but I do want to make sure we register something that the word 'supremacy' is carrying that isn't just tonal. Widdowson's 'supremacy' is not a character flaw or a narrative technique — it is the law of coverture. Under English common law in 1893, a married woman's legal personality was subsumed into her husband's. She could not own property independently, could not enter contracts, was not — in any full legal sense — a person. The Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 had begun to chip at this, but 'chip' is the right word — it was still largely intact. So when Gissing writes that Widdowson 'took for granted that it was his to direct, hers to be guided' — he isn't diagnosing a bad marriage. He's describing the legal default. What Gissing is doing — and this is where I think he's genuinely radical — is making you feel the weight of that default through a man who is not even aware he's exercising it. The system is scarier than any individual tyrant. Because at least a tyrant knows he's tyrannising.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's one of the most formally strange moments in Victorian fiction, and I don't think that's accidental. Rhoda is arguing with Barfoot about celibacy, about what the feminist cause demands of women — and she says this: "The necessity of the case demands what you call a strained ideal." "I am seriously convinced that before the female sex can be raised from its low level there will have to be a widespread revolt against sexual instinct." "Christianity couldn't spread over the world without help of the ascetic ideal, and this great movement for woman's emancipation must also have its ascetics." Now — what's formally strange is that this doesn't read like novelistic speech. It reads like a manifesto. Every other character in this novel, including the articulate ones, speaks in the register of their social situation — there are hedges, there is negotiation. Rhoda speaks as though she's already outside history, delivering a verdict on it. Which means either Gissing is giving her something he doesn't give anyone else — or he's quietly noting that this is what ideology sounds like when it doesn't yet know whether it can be lived.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I think the second reading is closer to the truth — and this connects to a phrase I keep returning to when I think about Rhoda. She is making a virtue of systematic exclusion. The 'widespread revolt against sexual instinct' that Rhoda is calling for — in historical terms, what does that look like? It looks like exactly the conditions that were already imposed on women by poverty, by the marriage market, by the surplus of women in the population that Gissing's title refers to directly. Rhoda is reframing necessity as choice, constrained circumstance as principled asceticism. Which is psychologically understandable. But as a feminist programme it asks women to go on denying themselves in order to prove they don't need to.

It's one of Gissing's most precise uses of free indirect discourse, and it's slightly devastating once you see it.
