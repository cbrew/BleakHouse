The New Woman and Her Convictions: Opening Rhoda's World (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to The Odd Women Unpacked — a close, spirited reading of George Gissing's remarkable 1893 novel. Each episode, we work through the novel chapter by chapter, bringing together literary, historical, and readerly perspectives. The Odd Women is one of the most searching novels about women's lives, work, and freedom ever written in English. It centres on the Madden sisters — daughters of a country doctor left suddenly without means — and on Rhoda Nunn and Mary Barfoot, two women who have built something extraordinary in their refusal to be quietly ruined. Today we open with the world Gissing constructs for Rhoda — her convictions, her contradictions, and what it means to call her a New Woman. Let me introduce our three guests, each of whom brings something indispensable to this conversation. Dr. Eleanor Hartley is a novelist and creative writing teacher whose obsession is how great fiction is built — the architecture of the sentence, the weight of a single image. She is exactly who you want in the room when a passage repays close attention — which, in Gissing, is most of the time. Prof. James Blackstone is a legal and social historian who specialises in the institutions literature depicts — the law, the workplace, the marriage contract. He has a gift for making the world behind the page feel urgent and real — and occasionally, quietly furious about it. And Ms. Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who first read The Odd Women as a teenager and has never quite let go of it. She attends to the experience of reading — the moments that make you stop, re-read, and feel something shift. Let's begin where Gissing begins — with the world his women inhabit, and with the extraordinary figure of Rhoda Nunn at its centre.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately about how Gissing opens this novel is the brutality of the prologue — if we can call it that. We get the Madden family in what feels like a pastoral sketch — Dr. Madden, his daughters, a summer evening of apparent security — and then Gissing simply removes the floor. The father dies, the money is gone, and the daughters must face a world that has no interest in their refinement or their virtue. It's a structural choice that announces the novel's argument before a single word of it has been made — that genteel female poverty is not a misfortune, it is a system. And against that system, Gissing sets Rhoda Nunn — who enters the novel as a young girl in the Madden household and returns, years later, as someone who has decided what she thinks. The distance between those two appearances is the novel's spine.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor's point about the system is the key to reading the novel historically. In 1893 — the year of publication — there were something in the region of half a million more women than men in England. The demographic reality was that a great many women would never marry — not by choice, but by arithmetic. And a woman without a husband, without a profession, without capital, was — to use a blunt word — destitute. Gissing's title is drawn from that surplus — these are the women left over, uncounted in the social arithmetic. What Mary Barfoot and Rhoda Nunn are doing, in their typewriting school, is not charity — it is a strategic intervention in that arithmetic. They are trying to make women economically legible in a world that has refused to see them.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I want to come back to Rhoda herself, because I think she is one of the most extraordinary characters in Victorian fiction — and also one of the most uncomfortable to spend time with. When we first meet the adult Rhoda through Monica Madden's eyes, she is striking, purposeful, and faintly alarming. Gissing gives her a face that is not conventionally pretty but arresting — there's an energy in her that the novel keeps describing as something almost combative. And her convictions — her belief that women must be self-reliant, that marriage as presently constituted is a trap — are held with a fervour that makes other characters uneasy. Gissing gives her a line that I have never forgotten, about the odd women — about the surplus women who, in his framing, might become the vanguard of a new order rather than its victims. She speaks of making women who are not constrained by the old feminine ideal — and she means it absolutely. The question the novel asks — and never quite resolves — is whether Rhoda's certainty is wisdom or another kind of cage.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline's word — cage — is the right one, and I think Gissing earns it through a very deliberate narrative strategy. He never tells us directly that Rhoda is wrong — he is too intelligent and too sympathetic a writer for that. But he places her convictions under pressure through the plot — specifically through the arrival of Everard Barfoot and what happens to Rhoda when she is looked at and found interesting by a man of genuine intelligence. The irony Gissing is constructing — very quietly, very patiently — is that Rhoda's theory of women's freedom may not have accounted for Rhoda's own desires. That is a devastating thing to do to a character, but it is also an act of profound respect — he takes her seriously enough to test her.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a legal and economic dimension to Everard's arrival that I think sharpens what Eleanor is describing. Everard Barfoot is a man of independent means — he has capital, he has leisure, he has the freedom to philosophise about free unions because he can afford the consequences. Rhoda has none of that backstop. When she debates the question of marriage with him, she is debating it from a position of genuine economic precarity — and he is not. Gissing understood — as few of his contemporaries did — that freedom of the mind is much harder to achieve when freedom of circumstance is withheld.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What moves me most, reading the opening sections again, is the tenderness Gissing shows for the Madden sisters alongside his rather steelier portrait of Rhoda. Alice and Virginia Madden are not equipped for what life has brought them — and Gissing never condescends to them for that. They are casualties of an education that trained them only for dependency — and that critique is as sharp now as it was in 1893. Monica is the youngest, still with some brightness in her, and her predicament — the shop counter, the grinding hours, the respectable poverty — is almost unbearable. These are the women Rhoda is trying to save — and it matters that Gissing makes us feel the weight of their actual lives before he gives us Rhoda's programme.
Host (host)
That distinction — between the women who need saving and the woman with the theory of how to save them — feels like the engine of the whole novel. And it opens directly onto the questions we'll take up next — about class, about the marriage market, and about what Gissing really thought society owed its women.

A Society in Flux: Class, Gender, and the Marriage Question (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been exploring Rhoda Nunn's fierce intellectual convictions and the world she's built at the typewriting school. But those convictions don't exist in a vacuum — they're a direct response to a society whose institutions are themselves cracking under pressure. So let's widen the frame now and look at the broader social landscape Gissing is mapping: class anxiety, the marriage market, and what it actually meant for a woman to be financially dependent in 1893.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's worth pausing on what Gissing means by the title itself — the odd women. He's drawing on the demographic reality of mid-Victorian England, where census data consistently showed a surplus of women — hundreds of thousands who would simply never find husbands. These women were statistically odd, in the sense of being the remainder — left over once the pairing was done. And the novel opens by confronting that arithmetic brutally, through the Madden sisters. Their father has given them accomplishments — a little piano, a little French — but no practical training whatsoever, because he assumed marriage would take care of everything. His death is catastrophic not just emotionally but economically, and Gissing makes absolutely certain we feel the causal chain.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And the way Gissing handles that opening is structurally very bold. He compresses years of decline into the first chapter — almost like a time-lapse of a family's ruin. Each sister's fate is sketched with this quiet, clinical precision — a marriage here, a death there, a gradual slide into poverty. It reads almost like a ledger. And I think that ledger quality is intentional — Gissing wants us to understand that women's lives in this period were subject to exactly this kind of cold economic accounting. The novel's form is doing ideological work from the very first page.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What breaks my heart every time I reread that opening is Alice Madden specifically. She's the eldest, she's diligent, she tries so hard — and the world simply has no place for her trying. Gissing gives her this painful religiosity that she retreats into, and it reads as the only consolation available to a woman with no economic recourse. Mary Barfoot says something quite devastating about women like the Maddens at one point — she talks about them being trained for a function that may never be required of them. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Mary Barfoot is really the novel's great explainer of systems, isn't she. She understands that the problem isn't individual misfortune — it's structural. And the historical reality she's describing is precise: middle-class women had almost no access to education that led to employment, and almost no employment that was socially acceptable if they did manage to get educated. The typewriting school isn't a fantasy — it's a direct response to the actual opening up of clerical work to women in the 1880s and 1890s. Gissing knew that world. He was writing from within it, not at a comfortable distance.
Host (host)
And yet for all Barfoot and Rhoda's clarity about the system, the novel keeps testing their convictions through the marriage question itself. Everard Barfoot arrives and rather complicates the programme.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Everard is such a technically interesting creation precisely because Gissing refuses to make him simply a villain or simply a hero. He's genuinely attracted to Rhoda's intelligence. He claims to want a free union, a companionship of equals — and on the surface those are progressive positions. But Gissing keeps showing us the gap between what Everard says and the possession instinct that runs just below his liberal surface. He wants Rhoda to choose him freely — but on his terms, in his frame, at his timing. There's a very sharp moment when he essentially proposes a secret arrangement, and Rhoda recognises that even this supposedly radical offer is still asking her to sacrifice her public credibility — her usefulness to other women.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That confrontation is one of the most electrically written scenes in the novel, I think. Because Rhoda is genuinely tempted. Gissing doesn't let her be simply a programme. She feels desire — intellectual and physical — and that vulnerability is what makes the scene agonising. She asks herself at one point what it would mean to give up the work — and Gissing renders her answer as something discovered rather than preached. "It would be the defeat of a cause." James Blackstone (social_historian)
What I find historically fascinating is Everard's proposal of a free union specifically — without legal marriage. That position was very much in the air in the 1880s and early 1890s — the question of whether the marriage contract itself was a form of property law applied to women. The Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882 had changed some of that, but the social stigma of any irregular arrangement fell almost entirely on the woman. So when Everard offers Rhoda freedom from the institution, he's actually offering her the full weight of public shame with none of the legal protections. That's not liberation — that's a rather elegant trap.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Exactly — and Gissing encodes that trap in the language Everard uses. He speaks of equality and freedom but the grammar of his proposals is consistently that of a man who expects to be heard. There's a recurring pattern where he poses questions that are really demands with a question mark attached. And he is convinced — with what Gissing calls almost a kind of intellectual vanity — that Rhoda will ultimately come to his position. The craft achievement is that we can see exactly why Rhoda finds him compelling, even as we see through him.

The Trap of Domesticity: Monica's Descent and Widdowson's Obsession (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been exploring how Gissing maps the larger social pressures bearing down on women in the 1890s. Now I want to narrow the lens — to Monica Madden, and to the marriage that was supposed to save her.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Monica is the character who breaks your heart most cleanly in this novel, I think. She begins as someone genuinely alive to possibility — she's young, she's restless, she's working in a draper's shop and finding it suffocating. And Gissing makes you feel the texture of that suffocation — the long hours, the cramped dormitory, the relentless smallness of it. So when Edmund Widdowson appears with his income and his respectability, you understand exactly why she says yes. It isn't love, and Gissing doesn't pretend it is. It's a calculation made by someone who has run out of better options — and that is the trap closing.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And legally, socially, once that trap closes, it locks from the outside. What Gissing renders so precisely is the Victorian doctrine of coverture — the legal fiction that husband and wife are one person, and that person is the husband. Monica's earnings, her movements, her very right to be in public space — all of it becomes subject to Widdowson's authority the moment they marry. He isn't portrayed as a monster, which is what makes him so disturbing. He genuinely believes that keeping Monica confined to the home is an act of love and protection. That gap between his self-perception and the reality of what he does to her — that is Gissing's sharpest critique of the institution.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Gissing actually gives us Widdowson's interiority quite generously, which is a brave structural choice. There are passages where we're entirely inside Widdowson's perspective — his jealousy, his bewilderment, his sense of righteous grievance. And Gissing doesn't editorialize heavily — he lets the reader do the moral work. Widdowson's surveillance of Monica — following her through London, timing her movements — is rendered in his own terms as vigilance, as husbandly duty. The narrative lets that self-justification stand, and then shows us what it looks like from Monica's side. The technique is almost cinematic — cutting between perspectives to create an irony the characters themselves cannot see.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That sense of total possession isn't aberrant for its time — it's almost the mainstream ideal. Conduct manuals of the 1880s and 1890s said almost exactly what Widdowson believes — that the well-ordered home required a wife's complete devotion. What Gissing is showing us is that Widdowson is not a villain — he's a symptom. And that indictment is more devastating than any villain could produce.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
There's a scene I keep returning to, where Monica attempts to articulate her unhappiness to Widdowson. She reaches for the words — and Gissing shows us the reaching, the groping toward a language she doesn't quite possess. Because the vocabulary for a wife's legitimate grievance within marriage barely exists in her world. Rhoda Nunn has that vocabulary — she's developed it through reading, through Barfoot, through conscious rebellion. But Monica was never given those intellectual tools, and that makes her vulnerability structural, not personal.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And then Everard Barfoot enters Monica's orbit — and it feels, for a moment, like oxygen. He's witty, he treats her as an intelligent person, he isn't Widdowson. But Gissing doesn't let us be comforted by that — Barfoot is charming in the way of men who can afford to be charming. He's pursuing Monica as much for the intellectual game of it as for any genuine feeling. And Monica, desperate for any acknowledgment of her inner life, mistakes the attention for something it is not.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The geography of their meetings is telling — they meet in public spaces because those are the only spaces Monica can access unsupervised. The park, the street, the railway — these become sites of a kind of illicit freedom for her. And of course those same public spaces are where Widdowson's surveillance is most ferocious. Gissing understands that for women of Monica's class, public space was itself contested territory — simultaneously the only escape and the site of maximum danger.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me as a formally daring choice is how Gissing handles the novel's end for Monica. She dies — in childbirth, essentially of despair — and the novel doesn't dramatize her death as tragedy in the grand operatic sense. It's quiet, almost reported, as if the system that killed her is so routine it barely requires ceremony. That flatness is the point — Monica's life is swallowed by the institution of marriage and what remains is an administrative fact. Gissing refuses her the dignity of a dramatic exit, and I think that refusal is itself a form of rage.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I find Monica's final weeks — the estrangement, the failed reconciliation, the slow physical collapse — almost impossible to read quickly. Gissing keeps returning Widdowson to her bedside, still not quite understanding what he has done. He grieves, but his grief is for the wife he imagined — not the woman she actually was. There's something unspeakably sad about that — that Monica dies still unseen by the man who believed he loved her most.

Irony and Unease: Gissing's Narrative Voice and Comic Distance (close_reading)

Host (host)
We've been tracking Monica's entrapment — the slow suffocation of her marriage to Widdowson. But Gissing doesn't deliver that story in anguished, melodramatic prose — and that's what makes it so unsettling. His narrative voice maintains a cool, ironic distance — even as the characters around him are falling apart. So let's talk about Gissing as a stylist — that voice, that comic distance, and what it costs the reader emotionally.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The thing that strikes me every time I return to this novel is that Gissing writes irony as a form of restraint — not detachment. There's a crucial difference, and it's the difference between a novelist who doesn't care and one who cares almost too much. Think about how he handles Everard Barfoot — that rakish intellectual who circles around Rhoda Nunn. Gissing gives Barfoot wonderfully persuasive arguments for free unions, for women's emancipation — and then quietly lets us watch him deploy those arguments as seduction. The irony is structural — it's built into the gap between what characters say and what they want. And Gissing never annotates it for us — he just holds the frame steady and trusts us to see.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That structural irony has a social dimension too, and I think it's where Gissing is most uncomfortably precise. He's writing about a particular stratum of English society — the genteel poor, the educated but unprovided-for — with the intimacy of someone who has lived it. And the comedy, such as it is, comes from the gulf between the pretensions of respectability and the grinding material reality underneath. There's a moment where the narrator describes Widdowson's domestic arrangements — his routines, his little proprieties — with a precision that reads almost like a naturalist cataloguing a specimen. The comedy and the horror are inseparable — because the specimen doesn't know it's being observed.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
James's word — 'horror' — is exactly right, and I want to hold onto that for a moment. Because when you actually read Gissing's prose aloud, you feel the comedy and the dread arriving simultaneously — in the same sentence, sometimes the same clause. His narrator has this quality of seeming to report neutrally — and yet every word is chosen with a kind of cold surgical precision. There's a line I keep coming back to — from the passages where Rhoda reflects on the women she's trying to help — where Gissing writes something to the effect that their lives had 'the monotony of hopeless effort.' Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Caroline is pointing to is something I'd call Gissing's tonal tightrope. He is writing comedy of manners — and yet every laugh curdles slightly as it leaves your mouth. Think about the scenes involving Mr. Micklethwaite — Barfoot's old friend who finally marries his long-betrothed after decades of waiting. On paper that's warm and comic — fidelity rewarded, love enduring poverty. But Gissing frames it so that the very happiness of Micklethwaite and his wife reads as an indictment of a society that made them wait so long for so little. The sentiment is real — and so is the critique embedded inside it.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Micklethwaite episode is interesting because it's one of the few places where Gissing permits himself something approaching sentimentality — and he immediately quarantines it. He places it well away from the main action — it's almost a parenthetical in the novel's emotional economy. As if Gissing allows himself one warm room in the house — and then locks the door. The dominant register elsewhere is that measured, slightly weary irony — which is the irony of a man who has looked at his society and found it wanting, but who will not shout. He whispers, very carefully, with excellent diction.
