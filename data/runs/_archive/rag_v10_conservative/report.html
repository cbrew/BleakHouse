Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to start, if I may, with a passage that I confess gives me enormous pleasure every time I return to it. It concerns Sir Leicester Dedlock in his London house — warm, resting, utterly self-satisfied. Dickens gives us the house first — and the prose itself seems to become warm and enclosed, almost suffocating in its luxury. He writes, and I ask you to savour this: "As warm and bright as so much state may be, as delicately redolent of pleasant scents that bear no trace of winter as hothouse flowers can make it, soft and hushed so that the ticking of the clocks and the crisp burning of the fires alone disturb the stillness in the rooms, it seems to wrap those chilled bones of Sir Leicester's in rainbow-coloured wool." Rainbow-coloured wool — what a phrase. It is both tender and faintly ridiculous — the great man swaddled like an infant, all that dignity dissolving into domestic comfort. Dickens is satirising, of course — but his satire here is not cruel. It is almost affectionate.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Edmund has just identified — that softness in the satire — is something I find structurally fascinating. Because Dickens builds the warmth so elaborately before he delivers the joke. That long, coiling, anacoluthic sentence — clause piling on clause — mimics the very enfolding of the wool. And then he drops us into the art collection — and the comedy sharpens. The mock auction catalogue — listing the paintings as miscellaneous goods — is one of the great deadpan jokes in Victorian fiction. He gives us: "One stone terrace (cracked), one gondola in distance, one Venetian senator's dress complete" — and so on, with that glorious accumulation of the grandiose and the merely described. The parenthetical — cracked — does the whole work of social criticism in a single word. Sir Leicester's entire world, in one contemptuous bracket.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I want to turn, if I may, to a very different register — Esther's narration. Because one of the things Dickens does with the dual narration is keep two emotional temperatures running simultaneously. The omniscient narrator is brilliant, ironic, panoramic. Esther is close, warm, slightly self-doubting. There is a passage where Esther is talking to herself in the mirror — quite literally — about her future life at Bleak House. "When you are mistress of Bleak House, you are to be as cheerful as a bird. In fact, you are always to be cheerful; so let us begin for once and for all." She is literally instructing herself — coaching her own reflection into joy. What breaks my heart is the phrase 'for once and for all' — as if cheerfulness were a resolution she has been failing to keep. The comedy and the sorrow are completely inseparable. That is Dickens at his most architecturally refined.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Caroline has put her finger on precisely the right word, and I'm glad she dwells on it. Self-deception of this quality is not the province of social machinery — it is a failure of the will, a failure of character. Burke observed that men qualify for freedom in exact proportion to their willingness to put moral chains upon their appetites. Richard cannot. He wants the inheritance too much, and so he constructs, brick by sincere brick, a justification that is indistinguishable from self-knowledge. What is remarkable about Dickens here is that he does not condemn Richard cheaply. The tragedy is classical in its shape — a young man of real promise undone by a single flaw that looks, to the sufferer, indistinguishable from virtue.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
They do, markedly. Richard is a man destroyed by hope — Bucket is a man who has surgically excised it. Consider the portrait of Bucket in Chapter 54. Dickens reaches for a very particular analogy. "From the expression of his face he might be a famous whist-player for a large stake — say a hundred guineas certain — with the game in his hand, but with a high reputation involved in his playing his hand out to the last card in a masterly way." What Dickens gives us is mastery without self-deception — the exact inversion of Richard. Bucket knows precisely what he has, precisely what it costs, and precisely how he must play it. There is even — Dickens allows — a possible touch of compassion toward Sir Leicester, lurking beneath the professional composure. Richard has none of that self-knowledge, and that absence is his tragedy.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Dickens is at his most Burkean here, I think — and I use that word with full intention. Burke understood that a society which neglects its stewardship of property neglects something far more fundamental — the chain of obligation between generations. And Jarndyce, explaining to Esther what Chancery has wrought, gives us that chain in its broken form. He says — and listen to the accumulation here — "It is a street of perishing blind houses, with their eyes stoned out, without a pane of glass, without so much as a window-frame, with the bare blank shutters tumbling from their hinges and falling asunder, the iron rails peeling away in flakes of rust, the chimneys sinking in, the stone steps to every door (and every door might be death's door) turning stagnant green." Every door might be death's door. That parenthesis is not gothic decoration — it is Dickens naming precisely what Chancery does to a life. It kills not with violence but with neglect — which is, morally speaking, the more damning indictment.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I find extraordinary about that passage — and Edmund has put his finger on something real — is the syntax itself enacts the decay. Sentence after sentence lists another thing failing, another thing absent — without a pane, without so much as a frame — until the architecture of the prose is as derelict as the street it describes. Then Dickens turns on a sixpence — because the very next chapter gives us Bleak House itself, warm and lit and alive. Esther describes it with this extraordinary phrase — she says the house had "its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw." A hospitable jingle — that is a masterpiece of aural description. You can hear the kitchen, you can hear life being prepared — and it is set against that silent, eyeless street with devastating precision.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And then at the end of the novel, Dickens does something structurally audacious — he gives the house away. Jarndyce, who has sacrificed his own happiness to ensure Esther's, makes this speech — and it is one of the most genuinely moving moments in the whole novel. "My dearest, Allan Woodcourt stood beside your father when he lay dead — stood beside your mother. This is Bleak House. This day I give this house its little mistress; and before God, it is the brightest day in all my life!" Before God — that oath is everything. Jarndyce isn't performing generosity — he is bearing witness to it, consecrating it. The house passes from guardian to lovers — and the naming of Esther's parents in the same breath is Dickens binding together every thread of the plot. Everything that was hidden, everything that was lost — it is restored, in a house.
