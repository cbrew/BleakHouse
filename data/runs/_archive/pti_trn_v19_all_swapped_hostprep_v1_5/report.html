Edmund Leigh
Forster gives Godbole the most beautiful voice in the novel and the most morally evasive philosophy, and I do not think he notices the contradiction. The novel cannot simultaneously honour individual love as morally weighty and endorse a philosophy in which individual moral acts have no individual agents — and its failure to resolve this is not complexity but incoherence.
Key points:
  • Forster arranges his three passages in ascending order of philosophical dangerousness: c7:p23 (taxonomy of friendship), c13:p36 (self-immolating love), and c19:p44 (Godbole's dissolution of moral agency) — and may not fully recognise what he has done.
  • Godbole's proposition in c19:p44 — that all perform an action when one performs it — is not Eastern wisdom but the abolition of moral accountability. Its passive grammatical constructions enact philosophically what its content asserts: agency disappears.
  • Fielding's warmth toward Aziz in c7:p23 is contaminated at its root by a generalising habit of mind ('so emotional a people') that is the intellectual expression of imperialism — not friendship in Aristotle's sense of philia, which requires seeing this person, not this type.
  • Forster's novel is a literary demonstration of the incoherence MacIntyre diagnoses in liberal moral vocabulary: it celebrates individual moral feeling (Aziz's love) while endorsing a philosophy (Godbole's universalism) that makes individual moral feeling impossible. These are not complexities — they are unresolved contradictions.

Disagreement angles:
  • Against Oliver Trevelyan: the celebrated handshake and Aziz's love must be interrogated — Fielding's warmth is taxonomic, not genuinely individual, and 'so emotional a people' is an act of generalisation, not recognition.
  • Against Daniel Rosen: admiring Godbole's proposition for its exotic novelty risks suspending critical faculties (Johnson's warning in the Preface to Shakespeare); the question of whether a philosophy that distributes all responsibility to all persons can guide anyone in conduct is universal, not merely postcolonial.
Daniel Rosen
These three passages together constitute a grammar of dispossession — specifically economic dispossession made invisible. Forster is not a propagandist for empire; he is genuinely critically engaged with it. But his novel has a structural unconscious: the classificatory syntax of c7:p23, the paternalist translation of dependency into devotion in c13:p36, and the cosmic dissolution of accountability in c19:p44 are each doing the same work — making the material relations of colonial capitalism beautiful, warm, and invisible at precisely the moment they are most fully operative.
Key points:
  • The colonial economy of Chandrapore is a precise, tiered wage structure that Forster never names directly — Aziz as subordinate IMS officer earns roughly Rs. 300–400/month, Fielding as college principal Rs. 800–1,000, Godbole as professor perhaps Rs. 150–200, against an ICS collector's Rs. 2,000-plus — and this invisible balance sheet determines who can afford to be whose friend and at what cost.
  • In c7:p23, the phrase 'with so emotional a people' enacts a colonial classificatory move at the grammatical level: Fielding is the stable subject who is 'not surprised,' while Aziz is rendered a contextual variable, a kind of people rather than a particular person — the same taxonomic logic that organized differential pay scales in the Indian Medical Service.
  • In c13:p36, Aziz's love for Mrs. Moore — 'There was nothing he would not do for her. He would die to make her happy.' — translates economic dependency into emotional obligation. Mrs. Moore is embedded in the administrative class of her son the City Magistrate; the love is real but not free, and its syntax tracks a performance of self-erasure that colonial service required of educated Indians seeking advancement.
  • Godbole's collective moral philosophy in c19:p44 — 'All perform a good action, when one is performed' — is delivered by the novel's least economically powerful character. A philosophy of universal participation, in which agency is dissolved and the passive voice depersonalizes all action, functions as a grammar of universal unaccountability that makes the specific economic mechanisms of colonial extraction invisible and unactionable.

The warmth of c7:p23 and the pathos of c13:p36 are real, and they are purchased — Forster never makes us count the cost to Aziz of being the one who extends himself across the divide. Who absorbs the risk of the friendship? Who has more to lose?
  • Against Edmund Leigh: Godbole's philosophy cannot be abstracted from the person speaking it. When we situate him as the lowest-paid Indian intellectual in the novel with the least institutional leverage, his philosophy of universal participation looks less like Vedantic transcendence and more like the rationalization available to a man with no other options — not a dismissal, but a demand to read it whole, as thought embedded in a life embedded in a structure of power.
  • Both panelists are likely to accept the passages at face value as accounts of genuine feeling or genuine philosophy. My challenge is that Forster's deepest ideology — deeper than his conscious political sympathies — is liberal individualism's inability to see economic structure. Each warm, generous, philosophically interesting passage quietly and systematically hides who has power, who has money, and who will bear the cost of what happens next.
Oliver Trevelyan
Godbole's speech in c19:p44 is structurally an echo machine: the active/passive grammatical oscillation dissolves the distinction between agent and recipient at the level of syntax, enacting the same obliteration of distinction that the Marabar caves perform acoustically — and this is Forster's deepest structural joke, completely invisible unless you track the sentence-level grammar the way a performer must.
Key points:

  • Free indirect discourse in c13:p36 creates an impossible third thing: a narrator temporarily possessed by a character's emotional register, and performing this requires being two voices at once, slightly out of phase

Fielding is not the novel's moral hero — he is its moral cautionary tale, and the novel knows it even if most readers do not. Oliver Trevelyan
The trial scene in A Passage to India is not a scene about justice — it is a scene about the failure of performance, and the most revolutionary act in it is a woman going silent. Adela's retraction is an actor refusing her role: McBryde's 'I suggest to you that the prisoner followed you' is a director giving a line-reading to an unwilling performer, and when she will not take it, the entire colonial apparatus collapses. Forster understood this theatrically before he understood it politically — colonial power is always a performance, and its vulnerability is always the same: it requires the cooperation of the performer cast as victim.
Key points:
  • The five passages contain five completely different vocal registers — declamatory (Ronny), elegiac (narrator), desperate (Das), coercive (McBryde), aspirational (Aziz) — and Forster switches between them with almost no transition, revealing the novel as polyphony requiring different instruments in performance
  • The trial scene is not structurally farcical or chaotic as critics who read it silently tend to claim; in performance it reveals itself as meticulously choreographed farce in which every character is simultaneously following the wrong script — a more damning critique of colonialism than actual chaos would be
  • McBryde's re-examination of Adela (c24:p134) is a masterclass in prose rhythm: the parenthetical 'or whatever we term it' is a fractional wobble in which the entire colonial apparatus loses the script, and 'I suggest to you' betrays prosecutorial collapse through its very grammar
  • The bathos in c23:p1 — 'in which so many elderly people are involved' — is doing profound comedic work that silent reading absorbs into solemnity; the word 'involved' punctures the mysticism, and Forster is simultaneously writing his most spiritual passage and quietly undercutting it
