Wonderful to be here. The Mill on the Floss is one of those novels I've returned to at different moments of my life and found it had changed — or rather, that I had, and it had noticed. What I want to talk about, above all, is how Eliot uses her narrator — this extraordinarily present, almost intrusive voice — to do things the plot alone could never manage. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Delighted to be here. And Eleanor, that image of the hard rind is exactly where I want to begin — because what Eliot is also describing is the epistemology of a certain class formation. Tom's unimaginative certainty is not a personal failing alone — it is the product of a particular educational system, one designed to produce exactly that kind of confident narrowness. And Eliot shows us this with a kind of rueful comedy, earlier in the novel. When Maggie is sent away from school, Tom suddenly notices what he has lost. Eliot tells us he had come to "a dim understanding of the fact that there had once been people upon the earth who were so fortunate as to know Latin without learning it through the medium of the Eton Grammar." The irony is surgical. Tom's great intellectual breakthrough — thanks entirely to Maggie's curiosity — is that education could be something other than punishment and rote. And yet it is Tom who inherits the world, and Maggie who is expelled from it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that flood is not merely a narrative device — Eliot builds toward it from the very earliest pages. In that moment on the water, before Maggie has even grasped what is happening to her, there is a passage that seems to collapse time entirely. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The Dodsons are one of Eliot's supreme comic achievements, but let's not be seduced by the comedy into missing what she's actually doing. The Dodson code — the bleaching of linen, the proper hat-bands at funerals, the correct bottling of gooseberries — is not mere eccentricity. It is a complete ideology of social distinction, maintained through ritual and exclusion. Listen to how Eliot captures their central psychological mechanism — the passage on the Dodsons in their collective self-satisfaction is extraordinary. "While no individual Dodson was satisfied with any other individual Dodson, each was satisfied, not only with him or herself, but with the Dodsons collectively." That is the precise logic of class consciousness — contempt for one's peers combined with absolute solidarity against those below. And then there is this extraordinary detail — a female Dodson, when in strange houses, Eliot tells us, always ate dry bread with her tea. "A female Dodson, when in 'strange houses,' always ate dry bread with her tea, and declined any sort of preserves, having no confidence in the butter." The preserves of other families cannot be trusted — which means, of course, that other families cannot be trusted.

The Miss Dodsons had always been thought to hold up their heads very high, and no one was surprised the two eldest had married so well,—not at an early age, for that was not the practice of the Dodson family. Funerals were always conducted with peculiar propriety in the Dodson family: the hat-bands were never of a blue shade, the gloves never split at the thumb, everybody was a mourner who ought to be, and there were always scarfs for the bearers. When one of the family was in trouble or sickness, all the rest went to visit the unfortunate member, usually at the same time, and did not shrink from uttering the most disagreeable truths that correct family feeling dictated; if the illness or trouble was the sufferer’s own fault, it was not in the practice of the Dodson family to shrink from saying so. In short, there was in this family a peculiar tradition as to what was the right thing in household management and social demeanour, and the only bitter circumstance attending this superiority was a painful inability to approve the condiments or the conduct of families ungoverned by the Dodson tradition. There were some Dodsons less like the family than others, that was admitted; but in so far as they were “kin,” they were of necessity better than those who were “no kin.” And it is remarkable that while no individual Dodson was satisfied with any other individual Dodson, each was satisfied, not only with him or herself, but with the Dodsons collectively. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And notice the structural move Eliot makes there — she embeds Mrs Tulliver inside the Dodson system by the end of that passage, precisely by calling her a mild Dodson. The comparison is devastating: Mrs Tulliver is to the Dodsons as small-beer is to ale. She is a diluted version of the type — and yet, Eliot insists, still the type. That's brilliant taxonomy — you can't escape the family even by being the weakest specimen of it. What I find equally fascinating is how Eliot uses Tom as the Dodson counter-weight to Maggie — he takes after his mother's family in features, in liking salt, in eating beans. Even appetite becomes an index of social belonging.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
I want to stay with the Dodsons for just a moment, because that passage is one of the great comic set-pieces in Victorian fiction and it rewards being heard, not just read. Eliot has exquisite timing — funerals conducted with peculiar propriety, hat-bands never of a blue shade, gloves never split at the thumb. Each item in that list is funnier than the last, and the list itself is the joke — it keeps going, like the family's self-regard. But then there's a character who operates entirely outside the Dodson world — Bob Jakin. And when Bob meets Mrs Glegg, he flatters her with his whole chest — this magnificent speech about the shopmen with their stiff stocks. "It's a thousand pities such a lady as you shouldn't deal with a packman, i' stead o' goin' into these newfangled shops, where there's half-a-dozen fine gents wi' their chins propped up wi' a stiff stock, a-looking like bottles wi' ornamental stoppers." Bottles with ornamental stoppers — it's a perfect image, and you can hear Eliot laughing as she writes it. Bob is the novel's great democratic spirit — he sells to anyone, flatters everyone, and has no patience for the theatre of respectability.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Bob is fascinating precisely because he's working the system from outside — he's the packman, the itinerant seller, the man who pays no rent. And he makes that economic argument explicitly — you pay three times the price in those shops because someone has to fund the stiff stocks and the ornamental manner. Eliot gives Bob the sharper class analysis, wrapped in flattery and dialect. But I want to pivot to something darker — the way class and gender work together to trap Maggie. The scene where Tom confronts Maggie about Philip is one of the most morally complex passages in the novel. Eliot gives us, with terrible precision, how Tom's power over Maggie functions. "Tom had his terrible clutch on her conscience and her deepest dread; she writhed under the demonstrable truth of the character he had given to her conduct, and yet her whole soul rebelled against it as unfair from its incompleteness." Demonstrable truth — and yet unfair from incompleteness. That is the precise structure of patriarchal moral authority — it is not wrong, exactly, but it is never the whole picture.

What's so devastating about that passage is what follows — Eliot turns the lens on Tom's interiority, and it's not pretty. He did not know, Eliot tells us, how much old boyish repulsion and personal pride was underlying his severity. And Eliot is doing something very precise with narrative technique here — she is showing us what Tom cannot show himself.

Certainly the religious and moral ideas of the Dodsons and Tullivers were of too specific a kind to be arrived at deductively, from the statement that they were part of the Protestant population of Great Britain. The Dodsons were a very proud race, and their pride lay in the utter frustration of all desire to tax them with a breach of traditional duty or propriety. To be honest and poor was never a Dodson motto, still less to seem rich though being poor; rather, the family badge was to be honest and rich, and not only rich, but richer than was supposed. To live respected, and have the proper bearers at your funeral, was an achievement of the ends of existence that would be entirely nullified if, on the reading of your will, you sank in the opinion of your fellow-men, either by turning out to be poorer than they expected, or by leaving your money in a capricious manner, without strict regard to degrees of kin.
