Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Bella Wilfer is one of the great achievements of this novel, and I think she's underestimated. She's funny — genuinely, sharply funny — in a way that Dickens usually reserves for his comic grotesques. But she's also the novel's conscience in embryo. There's a passage that crystallises this perfectly — Bella alone in her room after a conversation with Mr Boffin, interrogating herself in the looking-glass. She thinks to herself: 'What he said was very sensible, I am sure, and very true, I am sure. It is only what I often say to myself.' And then: 'Don't I like it then? No, I don't like it, and, though he is my liberal benefactor, I disparage him for it.' And finally she turns on herself: 'Then pray, what do you mean by this, you inconsistent little Beast?' That word — 'Beast' — is extraordinary. It's comic, it's self-lacerating, and it's the voice of someone genuinely beginning to know herself.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first is how Dickens separates the problem of money into two distinct registers — the psychological and the theatrical. On the psychological side you have John Harmon, who is perhaps the most genuinely conflicted figure in the novel when it comes to inherited wealth. His great confession passage is remarkable for the way it loops and doubles back on itself — as though the character must audit his own conscience in real time. The setup is so careful: 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife.' That triple repetition — shrinking, shrinking, mistrustful — is doing enormous structural work. It isn't rhetorical ornament; it's Dickens enacting the psychology of a man who cannot advance without first circling back.

The comedy is beautifully done — Oliver is quite right on that count. But I think we should be careful not to flatten what Dickens is doing into mere social satire. Burke observed that hypocrisy is the tribute vice pays to virtue. What Dickens anatomises in these letters is not simply a corrupt class — it is a corrupt moral imagination. Dickens understood something about the unrepentant that most novelists — most moralists, frankly — prefer to ignore. Remorse, at least, is a form of moral engagement — it acknowledges the wrong and the victim. But what does one do with a nature that refuses remorse entirely?
