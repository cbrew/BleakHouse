James Blackstone (social_historian)
What it signals to me is that the novel is going to be about the weight of the past on the present — which is not just a poetic concern but a deeply practical one. In the world Eliot is depicting — the provincial English Midlands, the 1820s and 30s — the past really does sit on the present with physical force. Old mill tenancies, inherited debt, family reputation — these are not sentimental concerns, they are legal and financial realities. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And one mustn't forget that Tom's authority here is not merely fraternal — it has, in the novel's world, a quasi-legal dimension. A young woman in the 1830s without property or independent income was, in a practical sense, under the governance of her male relatives. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That structural observation is important, but I want to hold the economic mechanism up to the light for a moment. Tulliver's plan is to call in the three-hundred-pound promissory note from his brother-in-law Moss in order to offset what he owes Mrs Glegg. In mid-nineteenth-century rural England, these informal family loans were the entire credit infrastructure for people who couldn't access banks. Calling in a promissory note from a struggling farmer like Moss could quite literally dispossess a family. And yet the narrator frames this as Tulliver worrying about the — and this phrase is choice — "the fallacious air of inconvenience which Mr Tulliver's spirited step might have worn in the eyes of weak people who require to know precisely how a thing is to be done." The satire is biting: Tulliver's concern is not with Moss's ruin, but with his own appearance of competence. Eliot understood exactly how self-deception and social performance conspire to produce genuine harm.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James has identified the injury, but I think we should name the structure that makes it possible. Tulliver's pride is not some personal pathology floating free of history — it is the ideology of the small independent proprietor who believes he stands on his own ground. Tom genuinely believes he acts from duty — duty to his father's memory, to family honour, to everything the novel has shown us breaking them apart. The system has taught him to call its imperatives his own — and that is the deepest violence of the novel's world. When the flood comes, it doesn't resolve that — it just ends it.

But to minds strongly marked by the positive and negative qualities that create severity,—strength of will, conscious rectitude of purpose, narrowness of imagination and intellect, great power of self-control, and a disposition to exert control over others,—prejudices come as the natural food of tendencies which can get no sustenance out of that complex, fragmentary, doubt-provoking knowledge which we call truth. Let a prejudice be bequeathed, carried in the air, adopted by hearsay, caught in through the eye,—however it may come, these minds will give it a habitation; it is something to assert strongly and bravely, something to fill up the void of spontaneous ideas, something to impose on others with the authority of conscious right; it is at once a staff and a baton. Every prejudice that will answer these purposes is self-evident. Our good, upright Tom Tulliver’s mind was of this class; his inward criticism of his father’s faults did not prevent him from adopting his father’s prejudice; it was a prejudice against a man of lax principle and lax life, and it was a meeting-point for all the disappointed feelings of family and personal pride. Other feelings added their force to produce Tom’s bitter repugnance to Philip, and to Maggie’s union with him; and notwithstanding Lucy’s power over her strong-willed cousin, she got nothing but a cold refusal ever to sanction such a marriage; “but of course Maggie could do as she liked,—she had declared her determination to be independent. For Tom’s part, he held himself bound by his duty to his father’s memory, and by every manly feeling, never to consent to any relation with the Wakems.”

And the terrible grace of the ending is precisely that — Tom and Maggie are reconciled in the moment of drowning, not before. The flood doesn't redeem the world Eliot has built — it simply overtakes it. Everything that was rigid — Tom's certainties, St. Ogg's judgements, the Eton Grammar — all of it goes under. What survives is only the earliest thing — two children who loved each other before the world taught them how not to.
