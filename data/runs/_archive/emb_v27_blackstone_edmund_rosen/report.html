James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me first is that Dickens doesn't begin with a character or a plot. He begins with a condition — with weather, with atmosphere, with an environment that has already defeated the people living inside it. And the specific choice of November, of Michaelmas term — that's not atmospheric decoration. Michaelmas term was when the law courts reopened after summer recess — when Chancery resumed its grinding work. Then he reaches for something almost absurd — and read in context it's anything but. "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A forty-foot lizard waddling up Holborn Hill — it sounds comic until you realise the point is geological. London in 1852 is not a city of progress — it's a city that has slid back to the primordial, to mud and slime older than human law.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James is right about the geological resonance, though I'd push back gently on the idea that this is primarily social diagnosis. What Dickens is doing here is something older and stranger — he is writing in the tradition of what I'd call moral meteorology. The fog is not capitalism's fog or Chancery's fog — it is the fog of fallen human nature, of original disorder. Consider what comes next — the passage is worth reading in its entirety for what it does with repetition. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That word 'defiled' — Dickens doesn't choose it carelessly. It is a word from scripture, from theology — it belongs to a moral vocabulary, not an economic one. The fog defiles because the city is already defiled — spiritually, morally, before any court of law opens its doors.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I have genuine respect for that reading — and I think Edmund is identifying something real in Dickens' language. But I want to notice what the fog actually does in this passage — who it touches, and in what order. It reaches Greenwich pensioners, a skipper, a shivering little apprentice boy on deck — and then the bridges, with their 'chance people.' The fog is universal in its reach, yes — but it is not universal in its cruelty. Dickens writes — and the word selection here is extraordinary — that the fog is 'cruelly pinching the toes and fingers' of the apprentice boy. "Fog cruelly pinching the toes and fingers of his shivering little 'prentice boy on deck." The skipper sits below in his close cabin — irritable, yes, but sheltered. The boy is on deck in the cold, already working, already being consumed by the conditions that sustain someone else's voyage. This is what the fog hides — not fallen human nature in the abstract, but a very specific hierarchy of who bears the cold.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel has put his finger on something I want to extend — because the hierarchy doesn't stay metaphorical. Dickens moves — and it's a deliberate structural move — from the fog in the streets to the fog inside the Court of Chancery. And in the court, the fog is not a natural phenomenon anymore — it is the institution itself, made visible. The Lord High Chancellor sits at the centre, looking up at a lantern that has no light in it — that image alone would be enough. But then Dickens delivers one of the great sentences in Victorian prose — and it builds like an indictment. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." Every shire. Every madhouse. Every churchyard. Dickens is not describing a single corrupt court — he's describing a system that has reproduced its damage at every point in the national geography. What is extraordinary from a legal historian's point of view is how accurate that is — Chancery cases could and did drag on for generations. Solicitors inherited them from their fathers, as the text says — and the fortune being made was always the lawyers', never the suitors'.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James, I won't dispute the accuracy — and I think it's important that Dickens had been in the galleries of Chancery himself. He knew whereof he wrote. What I'd add is that the rhetorical power of that sentence — 'decaying houses,' 'blighted lands,' 'worn-out lunatic' — comes from its comprehensiveness. Dickens is writing a total world — a world in which there is no corner that Chancery has not reached and corrupted. And that totalising vision is, in my view, finally a moral one — not a political programme, but a cry of the conscience. The sentence ends, and Dickens gives us the final warning — spoken, he tells us, by every honourable man among the practitioners. "Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here!" A system condemned by the very insiders who profit from it — that is not mere political satire. That is Dickens reaching for something more like Greek tragedy — the institution as fate, inexorable and self-aware.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to stay with that phrase — 'gives to monied might the means abundantly of wearying out the right.' "Which gives to monied might the means abundantly of wearying out the right." Edmund calls it Greek tragedy — I hear something more specific than fate. Dickens is naming a mechanism: money, deployed as patience, deployed as process, defeats justice. Not because the law is broken — the law is working exactly as designed. Those solicitors who inherited the cases from their fathers did not corrupt the system — they are the system, operating at full efficiency. The fog, then, is not moral chaos — it is the visible form of a system functioning precisely as it was built to function.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
It begins, I think, with something that looks very much like a virtue. Richard has a buoyancy of spirit — an openness to the world — that Dickens renders with genuine affection. But buoyancy, without anchorage, becomes drift. And drift, in the orbit of Chancery, becomes destruction. Jarndyce — who has watched this process consume others — tries to warn Esther. "A young man so unfortunate cannot at first believe — who could? — that Chancery is what it is. It procrastinates, disappoints, tries, tortures him; wears out his sanguine hopes and patience, thread by thread." Thread by thread. Dickens means us to feel each one. This is not a sudden fall. It is an unravelling — slow, inexorable, and all the more terrible for that.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the detail that makes Jarndyce's warning so devastating is that he isn't exaggerating. The Court of Chancery, in the first half of the nineteenth century, was genuinely capable of consuming a life. Cases ran for decades. The case of Jennens v. Jennens lasted over a century and a half. Costs devoured estates. Heirs died waiting. Suitors went mad or went broke — frequently both. Jarndyce says there are grown men who would be vitally changed within one year. That's not rhetoric. That's the historical record. What Dickens understood, with remarkable precision, was the psychological mechanism — the way hope itself becomes the instrument of torture.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that psychological mechanism doesn't operate in a vacuum. Chancery isn't a natural disaster. It's a set of human choices — about who can afford to wait, and who cannot. Richard has no capital, no income, no safety net. He needs the suit to resolve, because the suit is what he has. The narrator puts it with surgical clarity — Richard "hankers after it, and finds his whole world treacherous and hollow." Treacherous and hollow — because the entire social world around him is organised to sustain the fiction that the suit might still deliver. Vholes benefits from that fiction. The lawyers benefit. The whole apparatus feeds on Richard's expectation. Richard's ruin isn't incidental to the system. It is the system working exactly as intended.

Richard is the novel's great cautionary figure — and I find him more moving than Jo, precisely because his failure is one of character as much as circumstance. He is not ground down by poverty. I take Edmund's point — but I'd argue the novel is careful not to let us draw too easy a lesson from Richard's weakness. The system that corrupts Richard is the same system that kills Jo — it just offers different poisons to different classes. Jo gets the street. Richard gets the lawsuit. Both are destroyed. That symmetry is not accidental. And then there is Chesney Wold — the aristocratic conclusion. Dickens ends the Dedlock strand with exactly the prose texture it deserves — hushed, evasive, gothic, and faintly contemptuous. He writes of how the truth about Lady Dedlock's death is systematically suppressed. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That passage is one of the finest examples in the novel of Dickens' satirical method — and it deserves unpacking. Sir Leicester paid for silence. That was not a metaphor in Victorian England — that was a recognisable social transaction. The aristocracy's relationship to scandal was managed through money, through marriage, through the control of what was printed and said. And then Dickens turns that withering attention on the old friends — those peachy-cheeked charmers with skeleton throats — who dared to judge Lady Dedlock from beyond the grave. But the dead Dedlocks in the mausoleum, Dickens tells us, take it very calmly — and have never been known to object. That is the driest, most precise joke in a very long novel.

Dickens earns that joke. He has spent nine hundred pages earning it.
