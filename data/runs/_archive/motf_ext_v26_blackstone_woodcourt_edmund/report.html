James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me every time I return to this novel is how early Eliot states her moral thesis — and how unsparing it is. We tend to think of wickedness as deliberate — as scheming and plotting. Eliot will have none of that. She tells us that plotting covetousness and deliberate contrivance are nowhere abundant but in the world of the dramatist. And then she delivers the real indictment: "It is easy enough to spoil the lives of our neighbours without taking so much trouble; we can do it by lazy acquiescence and lazy omission, by trivial falsities for which we hardly know a reason, by small frauds neutralised by small extravagances, by maladroit flatteries, and clumsily improvised insinuations." That list is devastating precisely because every item on it is so recognisable. These are not the crimes of villains — they are the daily habits of ordinary, respectable people. And that, I think, is precisely what makes the social world of this novel so suffocating.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me first — and it never quite loses its force on re-reading — is the precision of the comedy. Eliot is doing something technically audacious in that scene by the river. She invokes the whole machinery of classical tragedy — the Greek requirement of magnitude — and then deflates it in a single sentence. She writes, and I must read this precisely as she wrote it: "There were passions at war in Maggie at that moment to have made a tragedy, if tragedies were made by passion only; but the essential τι μέγεθος which was present in the passion was wanting to the action." The Greek phrase is Aristotelian — the necessary greatness of action. Eliot is saying: the passion is Lear's, but the action available to an eight-year-old girl in rural England is — pushing Lucy into the mud. That gap — between the inner enormity and the outer impotence — is, I would argue, the novel's central tragedy.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And we get the narrator's gloss on exactly what kind of knowledge Maggie has — and hasn't — and it is devastating in its specificity. There's a line Tulliver speaks to Maggie — just six words, and they contain everything the novel knows about time and regret. "Ay, my little wench, but I shall never live twice o'er." I won't quarrel with that — though I'd say the manifesto and the lamentation can coexist. George Eliot doesn't sentimentalise Tulliver's regret — she shows you exactly how he arrived at it, thread by ill-judged thread. And that insistence on showing the mechanism — not just the suffering — is what makes this a novel rather than an elegy.

Mr Tulliver, when under the influence of a strong feeling, had a promptitude in action that may seem inconsistent with that painful sense of the complicated, puzzling nature of human affairs under which his more dispassionate deliberations were conducted; but it is really not improbable that there was a direct relation between these apparently contradictory phenomena, since I have observed that for getting a strong impression that a skein is tangled there is nothing like snatching hastily at a single thread. It was owing to this promptitude that Mr Tulliver was on horseback soon after dinner the next day (he was not dyspeptic) on his way to Basset to see his sister Moss and her husband. For having made up his mind irrevocably that he would pay Mrs Glegg her loan of five hundred pounds, it naturally occurred to him that he had a promissory note for three hundred pounds lent to his brother-in-law Moss; and if the said brother-in-law could manage to pay in the money within a given time, it would go far to lessen the fallacious air of inconvenience which Mr Tulliver’s spirited step might have worn in the eyes of weak people who require to know precisely how a thing is to be done before they are strongly confident that it will be easy.
