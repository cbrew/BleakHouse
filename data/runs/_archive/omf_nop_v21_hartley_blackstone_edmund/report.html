Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It does something very precise — it refuses comfort. Dickens usually gives us a little threshold — a coach journey, a description of a place — before he drops us into the action. Here, the very first sentence puts us in a boat on a dark river, with a man whose face is described as if it belongs to the river itself — all damp and rust. And what Dickens is doing architecturally is announcing the novel's central method: everything in this book will be about what floats up from below. The river is the novel's unconscious — it keeps returning things the surface world would rather forget. And that includes, crucially, money — because the whole plot is set in motion by an inheritance, by a dead man's will, by wealth that smells of dust and decay. Dickens is telling us from the very first page: in this world, getting and spending are inseparable from death.
Host (host)
James, Eleanor mentions the dust-heap inheritance — and I know that's something you've thought about in real historical terms. What actually was a dust-heap in 1864, and why would inheriting one make a man rich?
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The dust-heap is one of those details that sounds like Dickensian exaggeration until you look at the historical record — and then it turns out to be, if anything, understated. In mid-Victorian London, the collection and sorting of household waste was a substantial industry. Dust-heaps contained cinders that could be sold back for brickmaking, rags for paper manufacture, bones for fertiliser and glue — the whole revolting complex was, in fact, a recycling economy. The historical figure Henry Dodd ran such an operation in Islington in the 1840s and left an estate worth close to forty thousand pounds — an enormous sum. So when old Harmon — Noddy Boffin's late master — accumulates a fortune from dust, Dickens is describing a real mechanism of Victorian capital accumulation. What the novel adds is the moral charge — the insistence that this wealth, built from the city's waste and refuse, carries its origins with it. You cannot launder the dust out of it, however many fine houses you buy.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
James is right about the historical substance, and I wouldn't dispute a word of it. But I'd gently suggest that what makes the Boffins so memorable — so genuinely moving — is not the economic symbolism attached to their fortune, but their characters. Noddy Boffin is, in some respects, the novel's moral centre — a man of entirely native goodness who has never needed refinement to be kind. When Dickens has him say — and I'm paraphrasing the spirit of several passages — that he'd rather be called 'the Golden Dustman' without shame than a gentleman with none, the joke is entirely at the expense of gentility, not of trade. The novel's interest, to my mind, is in what wealth does to people who were good before it arrived — and whether goodness can survive it. That is a moral question, not an economic one — and it is the older and more interesting question.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Edmund, I don't disagree about Boffin — he's one of the great comic-tender creations in all of Dickens. But I think the moral and the structural are the same thing here — which is what makes this novel so extraordinary. The title itself is a kind of mirror trick — 'Our Mutual Friend' is a phrase that exposes the transactional nature of social connection. People in this novel are constantly introduced through intermediaries, vouched for by third parties — and that vouching is always also a transaction. The Veneerings — that whole society that assembles around their brand-new table with their brand-new everything — are Dickens's great satirical anatomy of a world where all human relationships have been financialised. And the prose enacts it — the narration adopts the language of novelty and display whenever they appear. Dickens's technique is his argument — they're inseparable.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Veneerings are, I'd agree, a kind of sociological specimen case — and a historically precise one. The 1860s saw an explosion of what contemporaries called 'new money' — fortunes made in railway speculation, in trade, in finance — entering London society through the mechanism of dinner parties and political patronage. What Dickens captures — and this is where I'd gently push back on Edmund's framing — is that 'character' in this world is itself a social performance, calibrated to audience. The Veneering table is a mirror — Dickens tells us this explicitly — and what the guests see in it is their own reflection, flattering and false. That is not merely satire of manners — it is a diagnosis of how a particular form of capitalism deforms the self.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
James uses the word 'capitalism' — and I notice he uses it with considerable satisfaction. But Dickens, I think, would have been puzzled by it — not because he was indifferent to suffering, but because his imagination was always personal rather than systemic. When he gives us the Veneerings, he is not writing a critique of an economic system — he is writing about the specific, corrosive vanity of specific people. Vanity is a vice as old as Ecclesiastes — as old as literature itself. And the mirror in the Veneering dining room is not a symbol of capitalism — it is a symbol of what happens to any soul that loses the habit of honest self-examination. Dickens would have known what Dr. Johnson meant when he said that the purpose of literature is to enable us to enjoy life, or endure it.

The Weight of Dust: Boffin's Moral Reckoning (deep_dive)

Host (host)
Welcome back to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked. We've spent time in the dust mounds and the river — the novel's great engines of death and inheritance. Now I want to turn to a character who sits at the very centre of all that inherited wealth — Nicodemus Boffin, the Golden Dustman. Boffin is, on the surface, one of Dickens's great benevolent figures — warm, generous, a little comic. But the novel does something deeply unsettling with him as it proceeds. He appears to harden — to become a miser, a tyrant, a bully toward the very people he once cherished. And then — in one of the novel's great reveals — we discover it was all a performance, an elaborate stratagem to protect John Rokesmith and test Bella Wilfer. So the question we're asking today is: what does Dickens actually achieve with this moral reckoning — and does it entirely convince?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first is the sheer structural daring of what Dickens attempts with Boffin. He essentially asks the reader to misread a character for several hundred pages — and then pulls the rug away. The miser performance is written with such conviction — Boffin's sneering at Rokesmith, his sudden obsessive interest in the biographies of famous misers — that many readers, original readers included, were genuinely deceived. There's a moment where Boffin demands that Silas Wegg read him the life of Dancer — the notorious real-life miser — and he listens with a kind of hungry relish. Dickens gives us just enough of Boffin's interior glee to confuse us — is this corruption or theatre? That ambiguity is the engine of the whole device. And I think Dickens is reaching for something about what money does to the way we read people — even people we trust.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That institutional point about reading people through money is worth dwelling on, because it has a very precise social location in this novel. Boffin inherits the Harmon dust fortune — and in mid-Victorian England, dust contracting was a serious, lucrative, and deeply unglamorous trade. The dust mounds of London were literally the city's waste — ash, cinders, bones, rags, refuse — but also extraordinarily valuable as building material and fertiliser. So Boffin's fortune is built on what society throws away. When he performs miserliness — when he pretends to become old Harmon reborn — he is inhabiting the spirit of the man who accumulated that wealth through parsimony and cruelty. There is something historically very pointed in Dickens's implication that great wealth, however inherited, carries the character of its original accumulator. The dust does not become clean simply because it changes hands.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That is elegantly put, though I think it risks flattening what is, at heart, a story about individual moral courage rather than social determinism. Boffin does not succumb to the corrupting force of the dust fortune — he resists it, actively and at considerable personal cost. What Dickens shows us is that a man of good character — genuinely good, not sentimentally good — can handle great wealth without being devoured by it. And the stratagem he devises — the performance of miserliness — is an act of considerable moral imagination. He submits himself to being thought ill of — by Bella, by the reader, by Society — in order to secure a genuine human good. That is not social critique — that is what the older moral tradition would call self-abnegation in service of love. Johnson, I think, would have recognised something admirable in it.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I don't disagree with Edmund about Boffin's moral courage — but I want to push on the craft question of whether the reveal actually works. Because Dickens, when he wrote the preface defending the Boffin plot, was clearly aware that readers had objected to it. He insisted the clues were there all along — and he's not wrong, exactly. There are moments — Mrs Boffin's knowing glances, Boffin's slight overcorrections in his cruelty — that hint at performance beneath the surface. But I think the more interesting question is this: does it matter whether the reader is deceived, if the moral argument ultimately holds? Dickens is testing Bella alongside us — and when she passes that test, choosing love over money, the machinery of the plot snaps satisfyingly into place.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Bella element is the one that gives me the most pause, historically speaking. Bella Wilfer is, at the novel's opening, refreshingly honest about her mercenary ambitions — she wants money, she says so, she's not ashamed. And Dickens clearly relishes her for it — she is one of the most vivid young women in his fiction. But the moral reckoning that Boffin's stratagem imposes on her is also the novel requiring Bella to renounce that honesty — to be reformed away from her most interesting quality. Boffin becomes, in that reading, not just a moral tester but a mechanism of social discipline — the wealthy patron who shapes the poor young woman into acceptable femininity. That is a Victorian social structure Dickens seems unaware he is reproducing even as he criticises it elsewhere.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think that reading is rather too severe on Dickens — and, if I may say, on Bella herself. What Bella undergoes is not the suppression of her vitality but its redirection — she does not become tame, she becomes purposeful. And there is a scene — when she goes to her father, old Rumty Wilfer, and speaks with him in the quiet corner of a riverside inn — that is one of the most genuinely tender things in the novel. She is not a reformed woman in that scene — she is a woman who has found what she actually values. That is not social discipline — that is what we used to call moral growth, before the term became suspect.
Host (host)
There's a lovely tension in this conversation between Boffin as moral hero and Boffin as narrative mechanism — and I suspect Dickens would have enjoyed both readings enormously. What strikes me in all three of your readings is how central Boffin is to the novel's great question — whether goodness can survive inherited wealth. The dust mounds cast their shadow over every character — and Boffin, perhaps uniquely, manages to stand in that shadow without being darkened by it. Whether that constitutes a satisfying moral resolution — or a convenient one — is a question we'll keep turning over. Coming up: we look at the novel's great villain, Silas Wegg, and the dark comedy of what the mounds contain — both literally and morally.

A Woman Remade: Bella's Long Becoming (deep_dive)

Host (host)
Welcome back to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked, the podcast that takes Dickens's great last completed novel apart at the seams to see what makes it work. I'm joined today by Dr. Eleanor Hartley, literary critic and novelist, Professor James Blackstone, legal and social historian, and Sir Edmund Leigh, whose close moral readings of fiction have been shaping the conversation for decades. Today we're diving into what I think is one of the most ambitious character arcs in all of Dickens: the transformation of Bella Wilfer. She begins the novel as a self-declared mercenary — a young woman who announces, almost cheerfully, that she intends to marry for money. She ends it as something far more complex — a woman who has genuinely wrestled with her own nature and come through changed. How does Dickens bring that off? And does he fully convince us?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Bella is the novel's great formal gamble, I think. Dickens gives her the most frankly unattractive opening position of any heroine he ever created. She tells her father — that wonderful, defeated Pa — that she is an article for the market, and she intends to fetch the best price. What's so clever is that Dickens makes her self-awareness part of her appeal from the very start. She isn't deluded. She knows exactly what she's doing and she's furious about it. That fury is the engine of the whole arc — she's angry at the world that made her this way, angry at herself for complying with it.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it's worth sitting with just how accurate that self-diagnosis was, in material terms. Bella comes from a household of absolute genteel poverty — the Wilfer family in Holloway, with the magnificent Mrs. Wilfer presiding over a kind of comedy of deprivation. For a young woman of that class and time, there were genuinely very few routes to financial security that didn't run through a husband. What Dickens does — and this is where the novel is quietly radical — is refuse to simply condemn her for responding rationally to irrational conditions. He shows us the system that produced her, not just the symptom standing in front of us.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'd accept the social context, of course — Dickens knew his London intimately. But I think one risks flattening Bella if one explains her chiefly as a product of circumstance. What makes her interesting is precisely that she has a will — a rather fierce one — and the drama is whether she will exercise it well or badly. Dickens puts her in the Boffin household, surrounded by comfort and wealth, and he watches what it does to her — and what she does with what it does to her. That is a moral laboratory, not a sociological one.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
A moral laboratory — I love that phrase, and I think Edmund is right that it's also a structural device. Dickens removes Bella from her family, places her in wealth, and then — crucially — he plants John Rokesmith in the same house as the secretary. So she is simultaneously being tested by money and by love, and the test is whether she can tell the difference between the two in time. There's that painful scene where Rokesmith declares himself to her and she rejects him with what she herself calls a mercenary little wretch's answer. She actually uses those words about herself — mercenary little wretch — and they land like a verdict she's pronouncing on her own soul. The self-knowledge is searing. And it is also, structurally, the hinge moment of the arc.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That moment is also interesting because of what Rokesmith represents — or rather what he conceals. He is, of course, the presumed-dead John Harmon, heir to the Harmon dust fortune — the very fortune that had been intended to come to Bella through a will. So the novel has set up an extraordinary irony: the man she rejects on the grounds that he isn't rich enough is, in fact, the richest man in the plot. But Dickens needs us — and Bella — to not know that when the rejection happens, or the whole moral architecture collapses. Her eventual acceptance of Rokesmith the penniless secretary has to be a genuine choice, made against her known inclinations, or it means nothing.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
And this is where I think the novel is at its most serious — in its insistence that transformation must be earned, not merely announced. Dickens does not allow Bella a sudden conversion. The change accumulates. We see her watching Mr. Boffin's apparent descent into miserliness — his cruelty toward Rokesmith — and being genuinely troubled by what wealth does to a good man. There is the magnificent scene where she stands before her father on Gracechurch Street and tells him, with great feeling, that she is ashamed of the part of herself that ever wanted this life. That scene with Pa on the riverside, their little dinner together — it is one of the tenderest things in the whole novel. And it functions as an act of renunciation: she is choosing love over money before she has any reason to believe love will keep her comfortable.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The riverside dinner with Pa is extraordinary — Dickens writes it with such warmth that you almost forget it's also a scene of deliberate poverty. They eat a modest meal, they're treated with a little condescension by the waiter, and Bella is radiantly happy. Dickens is showing us what she actually values, without having her deliver a speech about it. That's the craft — the values are dramatised through pleasure, not declared through rhetoric. And then there is the confession scene — where she tells Rokesmith's mother, Mrs. Boffin, that she loves him and cannot help it. The phrase Dickens gives her is something close to: I love him, and I cannot help it, and I would rather be poor with him than rich without him. That is the arc completed — in a single breath, spoken to the wrong person, without any certainty of outcome.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to push back slightly on the idea that the arc is fully completed at that moment, though. Because the revelation that Rokesmith is Harmon — that she will be wealthy after all — does somewhat defuse the sacrifice she's made. Dickens wants it both ways: he wants Bella to have made the unselfish choice, but he also wants to reward her materially. Morally speaking, the novel can't quite eat its cake and have it too — though it tries extremely hard.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I wouldn't say Dickens is contradicting himself — I'd say he's following a very old narrative logic. The virtuous character who renounces worldly goods is precisely the character who, in the economy of Providence, receives them back transformed. It is the logic of the parable — of every parable, from the Prodigal Son onward. Bella doesn't receive the Harmon fortune because she has earned it by being good. She receives it because the story insists that goodness and happiness should not be permanently opposed. That is not a weakness in the moral imagination. That is the moral imagination at full stretch.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Edmund puts that beautifully, and I think it's also why the Boffin deception is so important to the whole design. The revelation that Mr. Boffin was merely pretending to be a miser — was in fact acting the part to cure Bella of her mercenary tendencies — has divided readers since the novel first appeared. Some find it contrived, even insulting to the integrity of Bella's transformation. But I think Dickens is doing something genuinely interesting: he's showing that it doesn't matter. Bella's change is real regardless of whether the stimulus that prompted it was genuine or manufactured. The will that exercised itself, to use Edmund's framework, was hers alone.
Host (host)
That feels like the crux of the whole debate, doesn't it — whether a character's transformation can be authentic if the conditions that produced it were staged. James, you look as though you're not quite persuaded.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Not entirely, no. My difficulty is with the scale of the deception — Rokesmith and the Boffins have been conducting an elaborate psychological experiment on a young woman without her consent. And while I accept Eleanor's point that her change of heart was genuinely hers, I find something uncomfortable about a man engineering the moral improvement of the woman he intends to marry. There is a paternalism built into the architecture of the plot that Dickens does not quite examine — and perhaps could not, given the limits of his imagination about women. Bella ends the novel happy, domestic, devoted. But we might ask: is that the fullest version of her?
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think that question imports rather too many modern assumptions into a Victorian novel. Dickens believed — as most of his contemporaries did — that domestic happiness was not a diminishment of a woman but a fulfilment of her. The question is whether Bella achieves that happiness freely — and the novel insists she does. She makes her choice against her own stated interests. That is not a small thing. In the moral vocabulary of the novel, it is everything.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I'd add one more thing to what Edmund says, which is about Bella's voice — the sheer vitality of it throughout. She is one of the most verbally alive characters in the novel — quick, self-mocking, capable of genuine tenderness and genuine viciousness in the same paragraph. Even when she's being her worst self, you feel the intelligence behind it — the woman who could be better and knows it. Dickens doesn't write her as a problem to be solved. He writes her as a person to be accompanied through her own reckoning. And that distinction — between a character who is a problem and a character who is a person — is what separates Dickens at his best from Dickens on a bad day.
Host (host)
A person to be accompanied through her own reckoning — I think that's one of the finest descriptions of what this novel does with Bella Wilfer that I've heard. We've touched on her voice, her will, the Boffin deception, the riverside dinner with Pa — and I think we've shown why she remains one of Dickens's most debated heroines. Coming up, we'll be turning to the other great transformed figure in this novel — a man, this time, and a very different kind of becoming — Eugene Wrayburn. Stay with us.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right that the soliloquy is extraordinary, but I want to press on the social mechanics for a moment. Harmon's disguise is not merely a literary device — it has very precise legal and economic contours. A man presumed dead cannot inherit. His estate passes, by old Harmon's eccentric will, to the Boffins. So what Dickens has done is place his protagonist in the one social position from which the entire money-world of the novel can be surveyed without participation. There's a deep irony in the fact that Rokesmith — the disguised Harmon — takes employment as secretary to the very couple who have inherited his fortune. He becomes, in effect, a domestic servant managing the wealth that should have been his. Dickens understood that the Victorian gentleman's identity was almost entirely constituted by property. Strip the property away, and what remains? That is precisely the test Harmon undergoes — voluntarily.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
James's reading is characteristically acute, but I think it risks making Harmon too much of an economist and too little of a man. What strikes me about the disguise plot is its essential moral dimension — what it reveals about Harmon's character. He conceals his identity, yes — but the reason he sustains the concealment is that he falls genuinely in love with Bella Wilfer. And he will not claim her under circumstances that might compel her — as the will's conditions originally might have done. That is an act of extraordinary moral fastidiousness. He insists on being loved as Rokesmith — the penniless secretary — before he can reveal himself as Harmon, the heir. Dickens gives us, in that soliloquy Eleanor quoted, a man reasoning his way back to moral agency. He decides — 'I will swear to lay my mind bare to you, and I will do it, so that you shall see' — the effort of transparent self-disclosure is the ethical core of his journey.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Edmund, I find that reading genuinely beautiful — and I don't want to undercut it — but I think the craft question and the moral question are actually the same question here. The reason the soliloquy works so brilliantly is precisely because Dickens makes the formal problem and the moral problem identical. How does one reconstruct a self? That is simultaneously how the narrative reconstructs its protagonist and how Harmon reconstructs his conscience. Structurally, consider how late in the novel the true revelation comes. Dickens withholds Harmon's identity not to tease the reader but to ask whether identity without social confirmation is even legible. We read three books watching a man who is not quite real — who exists in a kind of social parenthesis. And only when Bella chooses him — truly chooses him — does the parenthesis close.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'll concede to both of you that the moral and structural readings reinforce each other powerfully here. But I want to add one historical dimension that I think neither reading has yet touched. The Victorian sensation novel — and Our Mutual Friend consciously deploys its conventions — typically used disguise plots to dramatize anxieties about identity verification. In an era before photography was widespread, before any reliable system of official records, how did you know who someone was? The novel's answer — you know by character, by conduct, by love — is a deeply conservative one in some ways. But it is also a rebuke to a society that had come to identify persons entirely by property and legal standing. Inspector Bucket — in another Dickens novel — would have needed a dossier. Bella Wilfer needs only her heart.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
James's point about identity verification is well made, but I cannot leave the architecture of this plot without speaking about Boffin. Because the truly audacious structural move in this novel is not Harmon's disguise — it is Boffin's. The Golden Dustman feigning miserliness and cruelty in order to educate Bella — that is the disguise within the disguise. Dickens layers the concealment so that the reader, like Bella herself, is genuinely deceived. We believe Boffin has been corrupted by wealth — and Dickens has structured this with considerable craft — before the revelation undoes our certainty. I know there are readers — Eleanor may be among them — who find this double revelation too tidy, too reassuring. But I think the tidiness is the point. Dickens is insisting that goodness persists — that the Boffins of this world are not inevitably destroyed by the dust-heap money. That is not naivety — that is moral courage in a novelist.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Edmund, you've put your finger on something I've wrestled with for years — and I'll admit I am among the skeptics about the Boffin revelation. Not because goodness persisting is naïve — I believe in that — but because the craft execution of Boffin's pretence strains the novel's own internal logic. We are shown Boffin in scenes where no audience exists to perform for — and yet the performance continues. That is the architectural weakness — not the moral ambition, which is entirely admirable. What Dickens achieves brilliantly, however, is the way both disguise plots — Harmon's and Boffin's — converge on the same moral subject: Bella. The entire machinery of concealment exists to answer one question: can Bella Wilfer become capable of genuine love?

Goodness and Weakness in Mutual Dependence (discussion)

Host (host)
Welcome back to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked. We've been moving through a novel dense with plots and counter-plots, disguises and revelations. But today I want to slow down and sit with something quieter — the texture of goodness in this book, and the way it so often arrives entangled with weakness. Dickens is fascinated by characters who are genuinely kind but also genuinely fragile — and by the strange dependencies that form between them. Edmund draws a line between social commentary and moral drama, and I want to gently push on it. Because Bella's transformation only makes sense in social terms. She chooses love over money — but she does so having seen what money without love produces. She watches the Veneerings. She observes the Lammles. She sees exactly what a marriage conducted on mercenary terms looks like from the inside. And her great moment — when she tells Rokesmith she loves him and will be content with nothing — is not separate from that social education. It is the fruit of it. The individual moral choice and the social critique are the same act.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eugene Wrayburn and Lizzie Hexam are, to my mind, the novel's most daring choice in exactly this territory. A gentleman — idle, brilliant, self-contemptuous — falls genuinely in love with a woman who cannot read, who earns her bread on the river. The class distance between them is not softened or wished away. It is held in the novel's gaze with unflinching steadiness. And Eugene's near-death — his being beaten nearly to death by Headstone — is what finally burns away the idleness and forces the choice. He inherits a world that would never permit him to marry her. He chooses her anyway, from a sickbed, not knowing if he will survive. That is choice stripped of all comfort and convenience. It is about as pure a test as the novel can devise.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eugene Wrayburn begins the novel as something close to a figure of contempt — his own contempt, more than anyone else's. He has every advantage of birth and education and absolutely no purpose to which he will apply them. His friendship with Mortimer Lightwood is beautifully rendered — two men of talent who have agreed, by mutual unspoken consent, to waste themselves. And Dickens allows that Eugene is redeemed — genuinely redeemed — by love and by suffering. Not by a programme. Not by legislation or the Reform Act. By the ancient mechanisms of the human heart encountering something worth loving.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And yet — and I say this with real admiration for Edmund's reading — Dickens will not let Eugene off lightly. The society that shaped him — the Podsnaps and the Veneerings, who are simultaneously his world and his prison — doesn't disappear at the wedding. Mr. Twemlow's final word in the novel — that last beautiful, quiet act of moral courage — is necessary precisely because the Podsnaps will never be converted. He declares that Eugene and Lizzie's marriage is the affair of a gentleman — and the novel ends there, on that small, enormous word. ‘I say,’ resumes Twemlow, ‘if such feelings on the part of this gentleman, induced this gentleman to marry this lady, I think he is the greater gentleman for the action, and makes her the greater lady. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Twemlow is one of those characters Dickens keeps in the background for four hundred pages precisely so that moment lands with full force. He is a gentle, ineffectual, mildly ridiculous man who is always being confused for a dining table. And in that final Veneering dinner, when everyone else is performing their cynicism with great social fluency, Twemlow simply tells the truth. He has no power, no money, no influence. What he has is an uncorrupted sense of what the word gentleman ought to mean. That is not nothing. In this novel, it is very nearly everything.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I find myself in complete agreement with James on Twemlow, which is a rare and pleasurable thing. The novel, taken whole, is an argument that inheritance — of money, of class, of social position — is morally inert. What matters is what one does with it, or what one does instead of it, or what one sacrifices to refuse it. Bella refuses the mercenary self she might have become. Eugene refuses the idle self he had become. Twemlow refuses the cowardice that was always available to him. These are acts of will. They are, in the old and best sense of the word, moral.
