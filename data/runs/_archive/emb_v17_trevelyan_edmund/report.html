That single word — 'London' — is one of the great opening gestures in fiction. Dickens doesn't say 'It was a dark and stormy night' — he doesn't give you a sentence at all. He gives you a noun. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And notice the grammar of that passage, which is doing something extremely strange. Those opening sentences have no main verbs. 'London. Implacable November weather. Smoke lowering down.' They're noun phrases, fragments — as if the language itself can't quite form complete thoughts in this fog. The style enacts the content. The syntax is foggy. And then there's that extraordinary phrase — 'gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun.' "Gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun." 'The death of the sun' — that's not satire anymore, that's elegy. That's apocalypse. Dickens holds those two registers simultaneously — the comic grotesque and the genuinely terrible — and that tension is what makes the novel so vertiginous.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take the point about register — it's well observed — but I'd resist the impulse to reach immediately for allegory. This fog is, first and foremost, a literary atmosphere — magnificently rendered, but still an atmosphere. What Dickens is establishing is a moral climate — the sense that confusion and obscurity have settled over human affairs like weather. And then he locates that confusion precisely — in Chancery, in the law — and that is where Dickens becomes truly formidable. He writes — and this is the passage I return to again and again — "Never can there come fog too thick, never can there come mud and mire too deep, to assort with the groping and floundering condition which this High Court of Chancery, most pestilent of hoary sinners, holds this day in the sight of heaven and earth." 'Most pestilent of hoary sinners' — that is the language of the pulpit. That is Dickens as moral preacher. He is not merely describing an institution — he is delivering a judgement, in the oldest sense of that word.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Edmund is right that it's the language of the pulpit — but what strikes me performing it is how the rhetoric builds. Dickens does something almost musical in the great Chancery passage — that repeated 'which has its...' construction. Listen to it — it's an anaphora, like a terrible litany: "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." Every clause adds a new victim — the landowner, the lunatic, the dead, the beggar — the indictment keeps widening. And it ends — and this is the masterstroke — not with Dickens's own fury but with the warning of Chancery's own practitioners. 'Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here.' The lawyers themselves say this.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Now there I think Eleanor has put her finger on precisely the right question — and it is, at bottom, a moral one. The fog and the court are the given conditions of the novel. They cannot be reformed within the pages of a fiction. What can be done — what Dickens shows us across eight hundred pages — is that individual human beings may choose how to conduct themselves within those conditions. Esther Summerson does not wait for Chancery to become just. She becomes good. That distinction matters enormously.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Richard Carstone is, for me, among the most finely observed young men in all of English fiction. And Dickens is merciless with him — merciless in the way that genuine compassion sometimes requires. The diagnosis is delivered early, and it is exact. Esther observes that the educational system had allowed him to dash through his tasks with fair credit and distinction — "in a fitful, dazzling way that had confirmed his reliance on those very qualities in himself which it had been most desirable to direct and train." Fitful. Dazzling. Two words that contain the whole of Richard's tragedy. And then the great paradox — Dickens frames it as a classical inversion, almost Aristotelian in its precision. His gifts, Esther tells us, were like fire and water — "though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." There is an almost Johnsonian economy to that formulation — it belongs in a book of maxims.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is the structural cunning of it. Esther is narrating, which means we're getting this diagnosis filtered through someone who loves Richard — someone who cannot be entirely objective. And yet her judgment is absolute. Dickens uses Esther's warmth as a kind of credential — her affection makes the verdict harder to dispute. The dual-narrator structure is doing exactly what it was designed to do — Esther's intimacy here prevents the reader from dismissing this as authorial moralising. And the image of fire and water — Edmund is right that it has a proverbial weight. But it also does something more — it makes Richard's qualities sound magnificent, even as it condemns their misuse.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Precisely so — and that is what separates Dickens from the reformist pamphleteer he is sometimes reduced to being. He does not present Richard as a victim of Chancery alone. The suit is the occasion, not the cause. The cause is in Richard himself — in that fitful, dazzling reliance on native brilliance never disciplined into application. Any number of young men have faced Chancery and survived it. Richard is destroyed because he cannot resist the suit's terrible gravity — he throws himself into it as into a vocation. He tells us so, with terrible self-awareness — "I devote myself to it. I make it the object of my life." The dreadful irony is that this is perhaps the most committed Richard has ever been to anything.

That switch to the omniscient narrator is a masterstroke of structural economy. Esther cannot narrate Richard's corruption of Jarndyce's reputation without implicating herself — she loves both men. I would resist the temptation — charming though it is — to read Lady Dedlock purely as a victim of circumstance. Dickens gives her desolation — yes — but he grounds it in a choice she made: pride over love, concealment over truth. The Ghost's Walk — that ancestral punishment for a wife who defied her husband — is not merely gothic atmosphere. It is Dickens reminding us that moral consequences are real, that they accumulate through generations. Burke might have said — and I think Dickens would have agreed — that the sins of the fathers, and the mothers, are not so easily buried. Lady Dedlock ends in the mausoleum — and Chesney Wold ends in silence — not because the system destroyed her, but because she could not face the truth in time.
