Eleanor, let's start with you — and with the opening of the novel itself. Dickens doesn't begin with a character, or even a scene in the conventional sense. He begins with weather, with mud, with fog. What is he doing, structurally, in those first pages?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What he's doing is establishing the novel's entire method in miniature — before we meet a single soul. The fog isn't description — it's argument. Dickens is saying: this is what England looks like from the inside of a system that was never designed to let in light. And then he does something technically audacious — he lists. He writes: "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping." The repetition of 'fog' at the start of clause after clause — that's anaphora, but it works because it mimics the thing itself: pervasive, inescapable, returning. You can't escape it grammatically, just as the characters can't escape it socially.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it's worth being precise about what that fog is obscuring — because Dickens is not being merely atmospheric. The fog descends most heavily over the Court of Chancery, and that is not a coincidence or a metaphor chosen lightly. Chancery in the eighteen-fifties was a jurisdiction that had been accumulating unresolved suits for generations — literally generations. There were estates still in litigation that had entered the court before the people contesting them were born. Dickens knew this precisely, and he writes it with a kind of controlled fury. He writes: "Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. This scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means." 'Scarecrow of a suit' — three words that do the work of a law review article. It's grotesque, it's agricultural — something that was once planted to produce an outcome has become a hollow deterrent standing in a field of ruined expectations.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James is right that Dickens knew the specifics — but I think he also intuited something larger that his own politics sometimes prevented him from stating directly. Chancery is not a broken institution that happens to harm people. It is functioning exactly as institutions of its kind are designed to function — slowly, expensively, in the interests of those who can afford to keep a case alive. The fog isn't an accident of English weather. It's the natural atmosphere of a system that requires obscurity to survive. And Dickens almost shows us this — he comes right to the edge — when he writes about the Lord Chancellor himself, sitting in the fog. He writes: "On such an afternoon, if ever, the Lord High Chancellor ought to be sitting here — as here he is — with a foggy glory round his head." 'Foggy glory' — that's a savage irony dressed up as grandeur. The man who embodies the law is illuminated by the very thing that makes the law unintelligible.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And what makes that image even more remarkable is its structural position — it comes before we've been given a single clear face to look at. Dickens withholds character so that the system itself becomes the protagonist of these opening pages. Which is why the two narrators, when they do arrive, feel so significant. The third-person narrator is omniscient, present tense, angry — almost hallucinatory in his intensity. And then there's Esther — past tense, first person, cautious, self-deprecating — who enters the novel like someone apologising for taking up space. The contrast between those two voices isn't just a formal device — it's an argument about who gets to speak and from what position.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That observation about Esther is worth sitting with, because her self-deprecation is historically legible. She is a woman of uncertain birth, dependent on the charity of a guardian she doesn't fully understand, in a society where her legal standing is effectively nil. Her narrative modesty isn't a personality quirk — it is the only rational response to her position. One thinks of the passage where she introduces herself with a kind of preemptive apology. "I have a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write my portion of these pages, for I know I am not clever." That sentence is doing extraordinary work — it's a woman telling you she doesn't trust her own perception before she's had a single perception. And of course, she turns out to be the most perceptive observer in the novel.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that's the novel's sharpest move — the character the system has taught to be invisible is the one who sees everything. What Dickens grasps — perhaps without fully theorising it — is that marginality confers a kind of clarity. Esther observes people like Mr. Smallweed — calculating, malevolent, trapped in their own grotesquerie — with a precision that the powerful characters lack entirely. She describes one such figure — noting how he admits cooperation only under absolute compulsion: "It would be impossible to make an admission with more ill will and a worse grace than Mr. Smallweed displayed when he admitted this." The comedy is acid — Smallweed would have kept Bucket out of his confidence by any means available, and Esther sees it plainly. But the larger point is that Esther's clarity is a product of her exclusion — and the system that excluded her never knows it's being watched.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right to seize on that verb, and I want to push it one step further into the historical record. The Court of Chancery — the real institution Dickens had in his sights — was not primarily a place of dramatic confrontation. It was a place of waiting — of adjournments, of motions, of solicitors' bills accumulating over decades while the original parties aged and died. There were real cases — documented cases — that ran for forty, fifty, sixty years. Dickens knew this. He had worked as a law clerk at Ellis and Blackmore in the late 1820s, and what he saw — the performative proceduralism, the way the system served itself — never left him. So when George says "file a strong man's heart away," Dickens is speaking from experience — not metaphor alone.

Professor James Blackstone is a legal historian, and he brings something invaluable here: he knows what Chancery actually was — not as metaphor, but as lived institution. And Dr. Daniel Rosen reads the novel as an anatomy of class power — every smile, every benefaction, every delay in Bleak House is, for Daniel, a mechanism of extraction. Let's begin with the world Richard moves through — the satirical texture of the institutions that hem him in.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I find so striking about how Dickens constructs Richard's world is that the rot is never just in Chancery itself — it radiates outward into every institution he touches. And Dickens' primary technique for showing institutional rot is satire rendered at speed — the prose moves so fast it feels comic, but the accumulation is genuinely horrifying. There's a passage in chapter eleven — the inquest scene — where the beadle goes looking for witnesses, and Dickens just lets the bureaucratic absurdity unspool. "Is immediately referred to innumerable people who can tell nothing whatever." That's the whole of official inquiry in six words — referred to people who know nothing. And the sentence structure enacts it — no subject, no agent, pure passive voice — because there is no one actually doing anything. The beadle is imbecile not because he's stupid but because the system makes imbecility its output.
