What strikes me first — every time — is how the prose refuses to give you a subject. It's all fragments. London. Michaelmas term. Implacable November weather. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I don't dispute the power of that sentence — it's one of the finest in the novel. But I'd resist the move to make it a theorem. It is, and I want to read it in full — because no summary does it justice. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The passage Esther gives us in the early chapters is, I think, one of the finest pieces of quiet diagnostic writing in the whole novel. She is fond of Richard — genuinely fond — and yet she sees him with absolute clarity. He is delightful. He is quick, spirited, charming. And precisely these virtues are what will undo him. Dickens builds the diagnosis with great care, and it culminates in a sentence I consider one of the most perfectly constructed in the book. "They were good qualities, without which no high place can be meritoriously won, but like fire and water, though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." The balance of that sentence is not merely decorative — it is moral architecture. Fire and water. The most powerful natural forces. Indispensable and ruinous depending entirely on who commands whom. This is not a sociological observation about schooling. It is a statement about the hierarchy of the self — reason governing appetite, or appetite consuming reason.

Lady Dedlock's destruction is the most devastating demonstration of class violence in the entire novel. That image of the pursuer in his coffin-bed, still obdurate, still imperturbable — it is one of the great Gothic strokes of the novel. But I would resist the conclusion that it is solely a political nightmare. What Lady Dedlock fears most, I think, is the verdict of her husband's love — not the law, not society. And in that, the novel is profoundly moral — perhaps even Christian — in the oldest sense. The question Dickens poses is not whether institutions will punish her — they will — but whether human love can hold when all else has fallen away.
