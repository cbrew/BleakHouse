Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I think Caroline is right that the atmosphere is powerfully rendered — but I'd resist the temptation to read it as indictment. What Gaskell is doing in those passages is showing us Milton through Margaret's sensibility — a sensibility formed entirely in the South. The novel's great project, as I read it, is the education of that sensibility — not its vindication. And there is already, even here, a seed of self-correction in Margaret. Consider what she says about Thornton — and notice the candour of it. "Everything else revolted me, from its hardness; but he spoke about himself so simply—with so little of the pretence that makes the vulgarity of shop-people, and with such tender respect for his mother" She is pronouncing judgement on Thornton and simultaneously revising it — within the same sentence. That is character developing in real time — and it is the deepest thing the novel does.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right that this is Margaret's developing perception — but I think what she perceives is also historically accurate, and that matters. When Margaret describes the men she sees in the streets of Milton, she is not being melodramatic. The condition of the handloom weavers, the factory operatives in the late 1840s — the grinding poverty was a matter of parliamentary record. Listen to what she actually says — this is precise social observation dressed in novelistic language. "I see men here going about in the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care—who are not only sufferers but haters." "Not only sufferers but haters" — that is a political diagnosis, and a very acute one. The resentment of labour that had been dispossessed and degraded — that is the engine of what will eventually become the strike at the centre of this novel.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James will forgive me if I suggest that the sullen sense of injustice Margaret observes is not quite the same thing as injustice being committed. She is reading faces in a street — and she is doing so as a stranger, newly arrived, shaped by everything Helstone has made her. What Gaskell gives us here is not reportage but the phenomenology of displacement — the shock of encountering a world whose values one does not yet understand. And that, I submit, is the far more interesting subject for a novelist than any social programme.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I want to start where Gaskell herself seems to start — with the cost of love that is not returned in kind. There is a scene in chapter twenty-three that I find almost unbearably moving, and I suspect it is underread. Mrs. Thornton has just made a rare and almost uncharacteristic appeal to her son — a genuine outpouring. And he, preoccupied with his own feelings for Margaret, scarcely notices. Gaskell tells us she crushes down — that is the verb — her personal mortification at the little notice he has taken. She recovers, she says goodnight, she goes majestically out of the room. And then Gaskell gives us this: "But when she got into her own, she locked the door, and sate down to cry unwonted tears." Unwonted — unused, unfamiliar to herself. This woman who has armoured herself in pride and will for decades, weeping in a locked room because her son did not notice her love. That is not social history. That is tragedy.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to bring in Higgins here, because he is in many ways the most sophisticated moral teacher in the novel — and the least credentialed one. In chapter twenty-eight, he delivers what is, in effect, a theory of pedagogy — entirely in dialect, entirely from lived experience. He is speaking about Hamper — a mill owner who tried to correct his workers and failed entirely. And Higgins explains why, with a metaphor of such precision it would not disgrace a lecture hall. "Folk who sets up to doctor th' world wi' their truth, mun suit different for different minds; and be a bit tender i' th' way of giving it too, or the poor sick fools may spit it out i' their faces." Be tender in the way of giving it. That is a more humane theory of instruction than most official educational doctrine of the 1850s could muster. And Gaskell gives it to a factory hand, not a reformer or a clergyman.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
That passage is one of the great democratic moments in Victorian fiction, and I say that as someone not naturally disposed to democratic readings. What Higgins articulates is precisely what Burke understood — that legitimate authority depends on the manner of its exercise, not merely its content. I daresay there's great truth in yon Latin book on your shelves, Higgins says — but it's gibberish and not truth to me unless I know the meaning of the words. He is not rejecting knowledge. He is making a rigorous epistemological claim about the conditions under which knowledge becomes knowledge. That is a remarkable thing to put into the mouth of an illiterate mill worker in a mid-Victorian novel.

The social geometry of that scene is precise in a way we can underestimate. Tea-making in this period was the hostess's sovereign domain — it was one of the very few performances of domestic authority that a gentlewoman exercised in public.
