That conditional is doing extraordinary structural work, and I think Eliot knows it perfectly well. Philip cannot ask the real question — 'do you find me repellent?' — so he routes it through Tom. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a social dimension here that tends to get overlooked in favour of the emotional texture, and I think it matters quite a lot. Philip Wakem is the son of a lawyer — a man the Tulliver family regards as an enemy, a villain, almost. For Maggie to extend warmth to Philip is already a small act of transgression against her family's loyalties. And deformity in the Victorian period carried a freight of social meaning that we can easily underestimate now. It was read, not infrequently, as moral stigma — as if the body were confessing something the character was concealing. Philip knows this. That's what makes his confession to Maggie so unguarded and so brave. He tells her — and the words are worth pausing on: "I'm very fond of you, Maggie; I shall never forget you. And when I'm very unhappy, I shall always think of you, and wish I had a sister with dark eyes, just like yours." A man of Philip's social position committing himself emotionally to a girl who is, at this moment, below him in fortune and entirely outside the scope of approved affection. Eliot is already loading the novel's tragedy into the childhood scenes.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about the narrator's portrait of young Maggie is the structural audacity of the comedy. Eliot gives us a child who has deduced the meaning of 'poly' from 'polygamy' and 'polysyllable' — and that is both entirely logical and entirely wonderful. The passage sets it up with this formulation that I find almost perfectly calibrated. "in travelling over her small mind you would have found the most unexpected ignorance as well as unexpected knowledge." That word 'travelling' is doing so much work — it positions the reader as an explorer entering unmapped territory. And the symmetry of 'unexpected ignorance' and 'unexpected knowledge' — neither cancelled by the other — that is Eliot's whole thesis about Maggie, in miniature. She is not simply gifted and not simply naive — she is both, simultaneously, and the combination is what makes her both magnificent and vulnerable.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to hold onto that phrase 'small female of eight or nine' — because the irony in it has a social edge that I think we shouldn't smooth over. Eliot is mimicking the language of conduct literature — the improving literature aimed at producing the 'well-trained, well-informed young person.' A girl of Maggie's class and era was expected to absorb exactly as much as made her useful and marriageable — no more. The tragedy is that Maggie's mind, left to itself, ranges far beyond those boundaries — and the world she's growing up in has no provision for that. She's not deficient — she's simply educated by the wrong things, in the wrong order, with no guide.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Which is exactly what makes the gypsy episode so brilliant as a structural moment. Maggie has run away to the gypsies expecting them to recognise her as their queen — a mind that has read about the wide world but knows nothing of the actual world. What interests me about that passage is how Eliot uses the gypsy episode to expose Maggie's class-based blindness alongside her intellectual precocity. Tom's education at Stelling's is one of Eliot's most controlled and devastating structural ironies. Tom is being subjected to exactly the classical curriculum that was withheld from Maggie — and it is destroying him. And the narrator makes the case so precisely: Tom's pride, which had previously floated comfortably in a world he understood, has nowhere to rest. What breaks me, though, is the prayer. All this, you remember, happened in those dark ages when there were no schools of design; before schoolmasters were invariably men of scrupulous integrity, and before the clergy were all men of enlarged minds and varied culture. In those less favoured days, it is no fable that there were other clergymen besides Mr Stelling who had narrow intellects and large wants, and whose income, by a logical confusion to which Fortune, being a female as well as blindfold, is peculiarly liable, was proportioned not to their wants but to their intellect, with which income has clearly no inherent relation. The problem these gentlemen had to solve was to readjust the proportion between their wants and their income; and since wants are not easily starved to death, the simpler method appeared to be to raise their income. There was but one way of doing this; any of those low callings in which men are obliged to do good work at a low price were forbidden to clergymen; was it their fault if their only resource was to turn out very poor work at a high price? Besides, how should Mr Stelling be expected to know that education was a delicate and difficult business, any more than an animal endowed with a power of boring a hole through a rock should be expected to have wide views of excavation? Mr Stelling’s faculties had been early trained to boring in a straight line, and he had no faculty to spare.
