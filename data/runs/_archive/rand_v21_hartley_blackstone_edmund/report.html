James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is absolutely right about the fog, and I want to add a layer of historical texture to it — because the fog is not just a literary device. The Court of Chancery in the 1850s was genuinely, systemically, catastrophically broken. Cases had been running for generations — not years, generations — consuming the entirety of the estates they were meant to adjudicate. Dickens was not inventing Jarndyce and Jarndyce — he was barely exaggerating it. There was a real Chancery suit — the case of Jennens versus Jennens — which had run since the seventeenth century and still had not been settled when Dickens began to write. So when Dickens places Chancery inside the fog — naturalistic, atmospheric, simply there — he is being, in a very precise sense, accurate. The institution had become part of the weather.
