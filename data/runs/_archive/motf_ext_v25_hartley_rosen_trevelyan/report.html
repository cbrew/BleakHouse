What strikes me immediately — and it never stops striking me — is how early Eliot lets you feel the comedy of Maggie's situation. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to push on that a little, because what Eliot is also describing is the shape of a restricted education — and who gets restricted. Maggie reads the dictionary because she has 'so few books.' That's not a charming quirk — that's material deprivation dressed up as eccentricity. And then look at what happens to Tom when Maggie actually visits him at school. She asks Mr Stelling so many questions about the Roman Empire that Tom reaches a realisation the formal curriculum had never given him. "he had actually come to a dim understanding of the fact that there had once been people upon the earth who were so fortunate as to know Latin without learning it through the medium of the Eton Grammar" The Eton Grammar — the text, the method, the institution — is the medium through which learning must pass, and it deadens everything it touches. Maggie, excluded from that institution, has retained the capacity to ask the living question. Eliot is showing us that the system doesn't just fail Maggie — it fails Tom too, by teaching him to mistake the Grammar for the thing itself.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that same fold appears in the Tulliver passages — the narrator is almost always comic and tragic in the same breath. There's a moment early on that I find structurally extraordinary — Mr Tulliver's entanglement with Wakem begins as pure farce and ends as something much darker. Eliot sets up that he absolutely cannot borrow from a client of Wakem's — his whole will is against it. "Mr Tulliver had a destiny as well as Œdipus, and in this case he might plead, like Œdipus, that his deed was inflicted on him rather than committed by him." Now that is a breathtaking sentence. A provincial miller in the English Midlands is placed alongside Oedipus — not as parody, but as genuine tragic claim. And the phrase 'inflicted on him rather than committed by him' tells you that the whole machinery of fate and free will is at work in this novel from the start.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Eliot does in these early chapters of ruin is something I find almost technically breathtaking. She distributes blame across a whole system of causes, and she does it with this gorgeous, lawyerly precision — ironically appropriate given the subject matter. Take the passage about Mrs Tulliver's influence on her husband's decision to go to law. The narrator offers this extraordinary image: "it might even be comparable to that proverbial feather which has the credit or discredit of breaking the camel's back;" But then she immediately pivots — and this is the move that makes it brilliant. The blame, the narrator insists, ought to lie with all the previous feathers — the accumulated weight that made the back so fragile in the first place. It's a structural argument dressed up as a comic simile — Eliot's at her most devastating when she's being funny.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me is how precisely Eliot maps the ideological machinery underneath that personal fury. Tulliver doesn't simply dislike Wakem — he has constructed Wakem as the single figure who embodies all systemic injustice against him. The law, as Eliot shows it here, is not neutral — it is a mechanism that the clever and well-connected deploy against the vulnerable. And Tulliver, whose principle that 'water is water' is perfectly rational, simply cannot compete with a system that rewards the glib over the honest. But what Eliot is doing with even greater audacity, I think, is showing how this same ideological inheritance poisons the next generation. The passage about Tom's mind is one of the most forensic pieces of social psychology in Victorian fiction. She writes: "Let a prejudice be bequeathed, carried in the air, adopted by hearsay, caught in through the eye,—however it may come, these minds will give it a habitation;" The prejudice against Wakem is not Tom's own creation — it is a structural inheritance, transmitted like property.

Her ideas about the gypsies had undergone a rapid modification in the last five minutes. From having considered them very respectful companions, amenable to instruction, she had begun to think that they meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking; the suspicion crossed her that the fierce-eyed old man was in fact the Devil, who might drop that transparent disguise at any moment, and turn either into the grinning blacksmith, or else a fiery-eyed monster with dragon’s wings. It was no use trying to eat the stew, and yet the thing she most dreaded was to offend the gypsies, by betraying her extremely unfavourable opinion of them; and she wondered, with a keenness of interest that no theologian could have exceeded, whether, if the Devil were really present, he would know her thoughts.
