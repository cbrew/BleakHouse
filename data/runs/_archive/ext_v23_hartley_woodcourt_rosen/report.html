Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time is the grammar before that sentence — or rather, the lack of it. 'London. Michaelmas term lately over. Implacable November weather.' These are not sentences — they're fragments, dropped like stones. Dickens has stripped the verb out of the language, and in doing so he's stripped agency out of the world. Nothing acts. Everything simply — is. Mired. And then that gorgeous accumulation: 'Smoke lowering down from chimney-pots, making a soft black drizzle, with flakes of soot in it as big as full-grown snowflakes — gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun.' The sun is dead. London is in mourning. And Dickens says it almost as an aside. That parenthetical 'one might imagine' is doing enormous work — it's the narrator half-winking, half-despairing.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to stay with that mud for a moment, because it isn't decorative. Notice who moves through it — or rather, who can't. 'Foot passengers, jostling one another's umbrellas in a general infection of ill temper, and losing their foot-hold at street-corners.' These are working people — people on foot, not in carriages. Before we leave the atmosphere entirely, I want to raise something that's haunted me across every re-read. That triple cadence is doing so much structural work, and I don't think it's accidental. Dickens is encoding Richard's entire tragic arc in a single sentence — spoken, crucially, in Esther's voice, not Jarndyce's. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to sit with that for a moment, because there is something ideologically loaded in Jarndyce's counsel that we shouldn't let pass unmarked. He tells Richard: trust Providence and your own efforts. But Richard is a ward of Chancery. His inheritance — if it exists — is locked inside a system designed to consume it. Jarndyce's advice is sound, but it also functions as a way of placing the burden of survival entirely on the individual — on Richard's character — rather than on the institution that is already devouring him. Dickens, I think, is clear-eyed enough to show us both: the genuine wisdom of Jarndyce's words, and their inadequacy in the face of Chancery's structural malice.

Tulkinghorn is the novel's great study in institutional power made flesh — or rather, made shell. Dickens shows him standing apart at the death of a stranger — equidistant from the surgeon's professional interest, the old man's unction, and the woman's awe. He registers nothing — not patience, not impatience, not attention, not abstraction. Dickens then offers this extraordinary image — "As easily might the tone of a delicate musical instrument be inferred from its case, as the tone of Mr. Tulkinghorn from his case." That word case does double duty — the instrument's protective shell and the legal case that is Tulkinghorn's entire existence. He is not a person who practices law; he is the law's instrument — and we have no idea what music, if any, is inside.
