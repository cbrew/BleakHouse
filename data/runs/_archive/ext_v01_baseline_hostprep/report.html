Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Both of those readings thrill me, and I think they're both partly right — but what strikes me about that Nemo sentence is the grammatical audacity of it. 'A deserted infant' — Dickens reaches for the most helpless possible image of erasure, a creature who arrives with no history at all. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
This is the passage that I think people read once and then walk past, which is an extraordinary mistake. Jarndyce is explaining what Lady Dedlock's sister did — how she chose, in effect, to cease to exist to the man she loved. "she wrote him that from the date of that letter she died to him — as in literal truth she did." That parenthetical — 'as in literal truth she did' — is the pivot where Dickens collapses the metaphor into fact. We start with a figure of speech — dying to someone — and by the end of the clause we understand it is a precise description of what actually happened. The silence that follows in the novel — 'he never saw her, never heard of her from that hour, nor did any one' — that silence is the architectural proof that the metaphor was always reality. That's what I mean by silence as speech — the absence in the narrative is making an argument.

I don't want to seem cold-blooded about this — that scene is devastating, I feel it exactly as Caroline does. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And what I'd add — at the risk of bringing the temperature back down — is that Mrs. Rouncewell's speech is also a precise diagnosis of the class structure. She says: 'you go your own way always without help, and you are not familiar with your friends.' She is telling Lady Dedlock that her isolation is a product of her position — that the distance between them is structural, not personal. The class system has made Lady Dedlock incapable of ordinary human connection — and Mrs. Rouncewell, with her 'genuine simplicity,' is the one who names it.
