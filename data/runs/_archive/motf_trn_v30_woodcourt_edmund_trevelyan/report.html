Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The thing that strikes you immediately, when you read Eliot on young Maggie, is that she's working in two registers at once. There's the high tragic — and she signals it quite openly — and then there's this sudden comic deflation. The Lucy-in-the-mud passage is almost the manifesto for how the whole novel works. Listen to how she sets it up — she even reaches for a Greek phrase. "There were passions at war in Maggie at that moment to have made a tragedy, if tragedies were made by passion only; but the essential τι μέγεθος which was present in the passion was wanting to the action." And then the payoff — the utmost she can do is push poor little pink-and-white Lucy into the cow-trodden mud. The Greek phrase signals grandeur; the cow-trodden mud is the reality.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
One must observe that Eliot is doing something quite precise with that Greek tag — τι μέγεθος, the essential greatness. She is not merely making a joke at Maggie's expense. She is making a very serious point about the relationship between feeling and action — which is, if I may say so, the governing question of the entire novel. Maggie will spend the rest of her life with passions large enough for tragedy and a world that offers no adequate stage for them. That is not comic, ultimately.

And then the narrator does something I find quite extraordinary — she describes Maggie's mind with this wonderful double-edged image. There's a line I always come back to. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The gypsy episode is also, I think, a study in the limits of imagination as a guide to conduct. Maggie's fantasy collapses not gradually but in what the text calls 'the last five minutes.' One moment she expects to be received as a superior being; the next, consider what she suspects. "She had begun to think that they meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking." Gradual cooking. The precision of that adverb is either very funny or quietly horrifying — and Eliot, characteristically, insists it be both. And then she wonders whether the Devil, if present, would be able to read her thoughts — which is not a child's question but a theologian's.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Right, and I want to begin with Tom Tulliver, because Tom gets a rather bad press and I think he deserves a moment of sympathy — and comedy — before we get to the harder business. There's a passage early on, when Maggie has been fetched home from Tom's school and Tom is suddenly alone again. He misses her, which is touching enough — but George Eliot can't resist telling us exactly what her presence had contributed to his education. And what she had contributed, it turns out, is one single luminous idea. She'd badgered Mr Stelling with questions about the Roman Empire — bless her — and the result was this: "Tom had actually come to a dim understanding of the fact that there had once been people upon the earth who were so fortunate as to know Latin without learning it through the medium of the Eton Grammar." That's the whole of Tom's historical enlargement for the half-year — one dim understanding, and before it, nothing but an epitomised history of the Jews. George Eliot is devastating about Tom's intellectual life, but she does it with such affection that it never feels cruel.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think that's exactly right, and I'd add only this: the comedy here is inseparable from Eliot's moral seriousness about education. Mr Stelling's pedagogy — Latin grammar as the great instrument of mental discipline, regardless of aptitude or circumstance — is not incidental satire. It is central to why Tom becomes the man he becomes. A boy taught nothing useful, shamed for his natural gifts, emerges rigid and merciless in his judgements. Eliot traces the moral consequences of bad education as carefully as any novelist I know.

A Dodson would not be taxed with the omission of anything that was becoming, or that belonged to that eternal fitness of things which was plainly indicated in the practice of the most substantial parishioners, and in the family traditions,—such as obedience to parents, faithfulness to kindred, industry, rigid honesty, thrift, the thorough scouring of wooden and copper utensils, the hoarding of coins likely to disappear from the currency, the production of first-rate commodities for the market, and the general preference of whatever was home-made. The Dodsons were a very proud race, and their pride lay in the utter frustration of all desire to tax them with a breach of traditional duty or propriety. A wholesome pride in many respects, since it identified honour with perfect integrity, thoroughness of work, and faithfulness to admitted rules; and society owes some worthy qualities in many of her members to mothers of the Dodson class, who made their butter and their fromenty well, and would have felt disgraced to make it otherwise. To be honest and poor was never a Dodson motto, still less to seem rich though being poor; rather, the family badge was to be honest and rich, and not only rich, but richer than was supposed. To live respected, and have the proper bearers at your funeral, was an achievement of the ends of existence that would be entirely nullified if, on the reading of your will, you sank in the opinion of your fellow-men, either by turning out to be poorer than they expected, or by leaving your money in a capricious manner, without strict regard to degrees of kin. The right thing must always be done toward kindred. The right thing was to correct them severely, if they were other than a credit to the family, but still not to alienate from them the smallest rightful share in the family shoebuckles and other property. A conspicuous quality in the Dodson character was its genuineness; its vices and virtues alike were phases of a proud honest egoism, which had a hearty dislike to whatever made against its own credit and interest, and would be frankly hard of speech to inconvenient “kin,” but would never forsake or ignore them,—would not let them want bread, but only require them to eat it with bitter herbs.
