Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close, unhurried look at one of the great novels of the nineteenth century. Our Mutual Friend was Charles Dickens's last completed novel, published in 1865, and it is in many ways his most ambitious and unsettling. It opens with a body pulled from the Thames and a fortune built on dust — the refuse and ash of a city that throws nothing away. From that first image, the novel asks a question it never stops asking: what is money, and what does it do to everyone it touches? Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me first, coming back to this novel, is how precisely Dickens understands the difference between inherited wealth and earned virtue. The Boffins are, by any conventional Victorian measure, nobody — servants, functionally illiterate in the language of polite society. And yet Dickens insists that their moral straightness is not incidental — it is the structural argument of the whole book. There is a passage where the narrator describes how even the hard, wrathful old miser who worked them to the bone could not entirely resist what they were. And the narrator draws from that a law — almost a cosmic law. That is Dickens at his most polemical — and most serious. Evil exhausts itself in the person who commits it. Good outlasts its origin.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That line lands like a sermon — and I mean that as praise. Dickens knew exactly the effect of that short sentence after all those long qualifying clauses. You build and build — ten thousand weaknesses, ten thousand vanities — and then you get four words: but Good, never. It is a preacher's trick. Dickens learned it from the King James Bible and spent a career perfecting it. But what I find equally thrilling is that the novel immediately puts that virtue under extraordinary financial pressure. The moment the Boffins inherit, the world descends on them — and I mean descends, like a flock of very well-dressed birds of prey.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I want to press on is the structural irony here. Charity, in this novel, is not a mechanism for redistribution. It is a mechanism for extraction. Money flows upward — from the newly enriched Boffin toward the aristocracy and the institutions the aristocracy controls. And then there are the individual beggars — the passage is even more ruthless about them. Dickens gives us this devastating line about the documents they enclose — their proofs of misery. That comparison is savage and true — and it cuts in two directions at once. The beggars are deluded about the weight of their evidence, yes — but the system that requires such evidence from desperate people is itself the indictment.

The comedy of that catalogue is genuinely dazzling — the inspired beggars sitting by a fragment of candle, the nobly independent beggars who wouldn't dream of accepting money, except as a loan. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me first — as a performer — is how Dickens constructs Harmon's confession as a man literally cross-examining himself. He's in the witness box and the dock simultaneously. Listen to the rhythm when he catalogues his own anxieties on returning to England: 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife, mistrustful of my father's intention in thrusting that marriage on me, mistrustful that I was already growing avaricious.' That triple repetition of 'shrinking' and 'mistrustful' — it's practically incantatory. Dickens is writing anxiety as a speech impediment — the mind stuttering under its own weight. And then — gloriously — he wraps up with: 'Is that so? That is exactly so.'

Oliver's right that it's rhetorically astonishing — but I want to press on what Harmon is actually confessing to.
