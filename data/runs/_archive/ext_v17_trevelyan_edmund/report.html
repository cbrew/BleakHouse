Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right that there is grief there — genuine grief, not mere sentiment. But I'd resist the word indictment, slightly. What Dickens is doing in that passage is something older and more universal than social criticism. He is asking what becomes of a human soul when it passes through the world without being truly known. That is a theological question as much as a political one — and Dickens, for all his reforming energy, always keeps one foot in that older tradition. Notice the image of the child at the breast — the mother's face, the soft hand. Dickens writes: That is not sociology. That is elegy.

Oliver has put his finger on something I think is central to the whole novel — the way Dickens holds comedy and tragedy in the same hand. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I won't quarrel with the power of that passage — it is genuinely disturbing. But I am slightly wary of the word 'choice' used so cleanly. What Dickens captures there is something murkier than a political programme — it is institutional inertia, collective moral failure, the gradual accretion of neglect. And I think his genius is that poor Snagsby — a decent, timid, thoroughly ordinary man — feels as if he is descending into an infernal gulf. It is the individual conscience that carries the weight here, not the abstract system.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
It is a passage of very great beauty, and I think it is important not to over-explain it. The cloud passing over the sun is not a symbol to be decoded. It is an atmosphere to be felt. Dickens is giving us, I would argue, something closer to the Shakespearean premonitory image than to allegory. What matters is the human fact behind that image: that Mr. Jarndyce, this good man, had himself once contemplated the very union now unfolding. He tells Richard directly, with that characteristic mixture of warmth and rueful self-knowledge. That repetition — afar off, afar off — is the gesture of a man who has relinquished something with great dignity and very little fuss.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I'd push back just slightly on that — not on the moral reading, which is beautiful, but on the question of responsibility. Dickens is doing something quite deliberate architecturally: he gives us Mrs. Badger's diagnosis before Richard's self-diagnosis. The institution confirms what the character already is.
