Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Boythorn is one of those passages where Dickens is being funnier than he perhaps intends — and more radical than he knows. On the surface it's pure comic performance — this magnificent blusterer firing volleys at the aristocracy. But look at what the dispute actually is: it's a right of way. It's about who has the legal claim to walk across a piece of ground — and Sir Leicester's answer is that it's his ground because it belongs to the park of Chesney Wold. What Dickens shows us — through the comedy — is that the law doesn't adjudicate between equals. It recycles the privilege of those who already own the landscape. And what makes Boythorn delightful is that he refuses to accept that — he just meets legal action with legal action, and physical force with physical force. Listen to the rhythm when Dickens finally lets him reach his triumphant conclusion — it's worth reading aloud. "He brings actions for trespass; I bring actions for trespass. He brings actions for assault and battery; I defend them and continue to assault and batter. Ha, ha, ha!" That triple rhythm — he does this, I do that — it's the structure of a legal duel rendered as pure farce. And what Boythorn understands — instinctively — is that if Sir Leicester can use the law, so can he. The system only begins to feel neutral when both parties have equal resources to fight it.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me is that Dickens frames the whole confrontation through Boythorn's reported speech — we never hear Sir Leicester's side directly. Which means we get Boythorn's theatrical version of events, full of abandoned villains with one eye and execrable scoundrels. But I think that choice is pointed — Sir Leicester speaks through lawyers and agents and letters. He doesn't have to be present because the system speaks for him. Boythorn, by contrast, is physically everywhere — with his fire engine, his axe, his man traps. One character occupies the landscape in person; the other occupies it through property law. And Dickens — through the comedy — is showing us that those are not equivalent forms of power.

“Come!” said Mr. Jarndyce. “Now for our assumption. Now we lift our eyes up and look hopefully at the distance! Rick, the world is before you; and it is most probable that as you enter it, so it will receive you. Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner. Constancy in love is a good thing, but it means nothing, and is nothing, without constancy in every kind of effort. If you had the abilities of all the great men, past and present, you could do nothing well without sincerely meaning it and setting about it. If you entertain the supposition that any real success, in great things or in small, ever was or could be, ever will or can be, wrested from Fortune by fits and starts, leave that wrong idea here or leave your cousin Ada here.”
Mr. Jarndyce advises Richard to trust Providence and his own efforts equally, to combine constancy in love with diligent effort in all pursuits, and never to expect success without genuine commitment.

There is something almost Shakespearean in that — the truth spoken clearly, heard clearly, and still not acted upon. Bleak House does not end in triumph — but it does not end in pure despair either. What it leaves us with is something harder to name: a deep accounting of what is lost when systems devour people, and a quiet insistence that the alternative remains possible.
