Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The novel announces its thesis in the very first sustained description we get of old John Harmon — the Dust Contractor. Mortimer Lightwood tells the story at a fashionable dinner party, and it has the tone of an amusing anecdote — which is itself a form of violence. The comfortable classes are being entertained by the story of a man who built an empire out of what other people threw away. And Dickens gives us the inventory with this extraordinary, accumulating rhythm — listen to what he catalogues: "Coal-dust, vegetable-dust, bone-dust, crockery dust, rough dust and sifted dust,—all manner of Dust." That final capitalised Dust — it stops being a substance and becomes a verdict. The wealth of England, Dickens is telling us, is built on what we discard — on the labour and the waste of the poor made profitable for someone else.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The dust heap was a real and substantial institution in Victorian London — which is what makes Dickens's choice of it so pointed. Dust contractors collected the ash and refuse of the city — and within it they found coal remnants, metal, rags, bone, all of it sorted and sold on. A large dust heap was a genuinely valuable piece of capital — the fortune Dickens builds the entire novel around was, in the 1860s, entirely plausible. What's remarkable is how quickly Dickens moves from this comic anecdote to the darkest edge of what the dust represents. When Mortimer and Eugene travel down toward Gaffer Hexam's part of the river, Dickens gives us a description of urban poverty that is anything but comic. He describes the wheels rolling down through degraded neighbourhood after neighbourhood, and then he arrives at this: "accumulated scum of humanity seemed to be washed from higher grounds, like so much moral sewage, and to be pausing until its own weight forced it over the bank and sunk it in the river." That phrase — moral sewage — is Dickens ventriloquising the ideology of his own class, and then holding it up to the light. He gives the governing class its own language — and the language condemns them.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James has put his finger on something essential — that the narrator's perspective in Our Mutual Friend is not a comfortable one. Dickens doesn't let us settle into a safe ironic distance from that phrase — he makes us feel the weight of it. And the novel comes back to this — explicitly, polemically — when Dickens addresses the governing classes directly over the dust heaps. It is one of the most startling passages in Victorian fiction — a direct address to power, in the voice of a man who has run out of patience. He writes — to his lords and gentlemen and honourable boards — that when you have piled up a mountain of pretentious failure: "you must off with your honourable coats for the removal of it, and fall to the work with the power of all the queen's horses and all the queen's men, or it will come rushing down and bury us alive." Bury us alive — not them, not the poor, not the marginalised — us. Dickens understood that the catastrophe of social neglect doesn't respect class boundaries — the dust heap buries everyone eventually.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and what Dickens captures here is not sentiment or generalised outrage but something far more specific: the mechanics of extraction. The moment Boffin's wealth becomes publicly known, the apparatus descends on him with extraordinary efficiency. Dickens gives us the inventory — and it's almost a ledger, which is entirely the point. He writes: 'Fifty-seven churches to be erected with half-crowns, forty-two parsonage houses to be repaired with shillings, seven-and-twenty organs to be built with halfpence, twelve hundred children to be brought up on postage stamps.' The comedy is in the bathos — the grand ecclesiastical ambition paired with the postage stamp — but the critique is entirely serious. These institutions are not asking Boffin to give proportionally — they are identifying him as the man who will make up whatever deficiency exists. And then we get the Duke of Linseed — and this is Dickens at his most precise. 'Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet.' Six words — and the entire social performance is there: the physical weight of the thing, the pretension to intimacy, the heraldic seal as a form of psychological leverage.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And the postscript — that is the tell. Dickens tells us it's 'thoughtful in the postscript' — because the postscript is where the duke finally gets to the point. Three guineas. And Legacy Duty is perfectly chosen — because it frames the parasitism as a tax, as something systemic and inescapable, not personal.
