Perhaps it was because teaching came naturally to Mr Stelling, that he set about it with that uniformity of method and independence of circumstances which distinguish the actions of animals understood to be under the immediate teaching of nature. Mr Broderip’s amiable beaver, as that charming naturalist tells us, busied himself as earnestly in constructing a dam, in a room up three pair of stairs in London, as if he had been laying his foundation in a stream or lake in Upper Canada. It was “Binny’s” function to build; the absence of water or of possible progeny was an accident for which he was not accountable. With the same unerring instinct Mr Stelling set to work at his natural method of instilling the Eton Grammar and Euclid into the mind of Tom Tulliver. This, he considered, was the only basis of solid instruction; all other means of education were mere charlatanism, and could produce nothing better than smatterers. Fixed on this firm basis, a man might observe the display of various or special knowledge made by irregularly educated people with a pitying smile; all that sort of thing was very well, but it was impossible these people could form sound opinions. In holding this conviction Mr Stelling was not biassed, as some tutors have been, by the excessive accuracy or extent of his own scholarship; and as to his views about Euclid, no opinion could have been freer from personal partiality. Mr Stelling was very far from being led astray by enthusiasm, either religious or intellectual; on the other hand, he had no secret belief that everything was humbug. He thought religion was a very excellent thing, and Aristotle a great authority, and deaneries and prebends useful institutions, and Great Britain the providential bulwark of Protestantism, and faith in the unseen a great support to afflicted minds; he believed in all these things, as a Swiss hotel-keeper believes in the beauty of the scenery around him, and in the pleasure it gives to artistic visitors. And in the same way Mr Stelling believed in his method of education; he had no doubt that he was doing the very best thing for Mr Tulliver’s boy. Of course, when the miller talked of “mapping” and “summing” in a vague and diffident manner, Mr Stelling had set his mind at rest by an assurance that he understood what was wanted; for how was it possible the good man could form any reasonable judgment about the matter? Mr Stelling’s duty was to teach the lad in the only right way,—indeed he knew no other; he had not wasted his time in the acquirement of anything abnormal.

The Stelling passages are among the funniest things in the novel, and I think they're funny in a very specific way — the comedy is doing serious work. Eliot gives us this magnificent image — Mr Broderip's beaver, building his dam in a room up three flights of stairs in London, with no water and no possible progeny. The beaver's function, you see, is simply to build. The absence of water is merely an inconvenient accident. And then the transition — and it is a perfect transition — "With the same unerring instinct Mr Stelling set to work at his natural method of instilling the Eton Grammar and Euclid into the mind of Tom Tulliver." The word "instinct" is the blade — the same word that governs the beaver now governs the schoolmaster. Stelling is not a villain — he's something far more devastating in Eliot's world: he is a man of complete and comfortable certainty.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to identify the satirical precision — but I'd want us to hold on to something that could easily be missed. Eliot's narrator is not simply mocking Stelling. There is a rather chilling generosity in the observation that Stelling had no secret belief that everything was humbug. He believed in religion, in Aristotle, in deaneries — as, Eliot tells us, a Swiss hotel-keeper believes in the beauty of the scenery. That is a devastating simile, but it is a morally nuanced one. Stelling is not corrupt — he is merely incurious. And in Eliot's moral universe, incuriosity about those in your care is its own form of failure.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Edmund calls incuriosity, I would call structural inevitability. Stelling is not a one-off failure — he is the product of a system in which the content of education is entirely disconnected from the needs of the people receiving it. The Eton Grammar and Euclid are not chosen because they educate Tom Tulliver — they are chosen because they mark the class position of the man who teaches them. And Eliot is precise about this — Stelling's belief in his method was not biased by excessive accuracy or extent of his own scholarship. The man didn't even know the subject particularly well. He had the credential, the position, the institution behind him — and that was entirely sufficient. That is not incuriosity — that is class power operating exactly as it was designed to.

Whence Mr Stelling concluded that Tom’s brain, being peculiarly impervious to etymology and demonstrations, was peculiarly in need of being ploughed and harrowed by these patent implements; it was his favourite metaphor, that the classics and geometry constituted that culture of the mind which prepared it for the reception of any subsequent crop. I say nothing against Mr Stelling’s theory; if we are to have one regimen for all minds, his seems to me as good as any other. There is something in that passage which I think resists Daniel's reading, however — and it matters. Eliot's narrator does not say that Tom's practical intelligence is simply suppressed by the system. She says, with real care, that his perceptive powers were not at all deficient — indeed, they were quite as strong as those of the Rev. Mr Stelling. This is not simply a story of a good mind crushed by a bad system. It is a story of the genuine tragedy of wrong fit — a particular mind, a particular method, and no one with the imagination to bridge them.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I take Edmund's point — and I'd never reduce Tom to a simple victim. But I want to move us to the moment when Tom first encounters Philip Wakem — because something very uncomfortable happens there. Tom looks at Philip and sees — first and foremost — the hump. He has a vague notion that the deformity of Wakem's son had some relation to the lawyer's rascality. And then Eliot gives us his evidence base — and it is this: "There was a humpbacked tailor in the neighbourhood of Mr Jacobs's academy, who was considered a very unamiable character, and was much hooted after by public-spirited boys solely on the ground of his unsatisfactory moral qualities." Public-spirited boys. That is Eliot's irony at its most precise — the cruelty of children dressed up as civic virtue. Tom is not an individual monster — he has simply absorbed exactly what his world has taught him about bodies that don't conform.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
And then Philip disarms him entirely — with the Greeks. He tells Tom, with a quite naked enthusiasm, what he would have wished to be. "I should like to have been a Greek and fought the Persians, and then have come home and have written tragedies, or else have been listened to by everybody for my wisdom, like Socrates, and have died a grand death." The narrator adds, in parentheses, that Philip was not without a wish to impress the well-made barbarian with a sense of his mental superiority. "The well-made barbarian" — that parenthetical is worth pausing on. It is gently mocking of Philip's vanity — but it also tells us exactly how Philip sees the world. Mind against body — the crippled scholar against the healthy, uncomplicated boy. This is a rivalry being established — one that will carry enormous weight as the novel develops.
