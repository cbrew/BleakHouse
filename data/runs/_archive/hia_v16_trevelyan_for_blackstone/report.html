Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What I love about Esther's first arrival at Bleak House is that the house is allowed to make its case entirely through accumulation. There's no dramatic reveal, no single image Dickens points to and says: here, this is home. Instead you get this wonderful, slightly bewildered inventory — birds staring out of pictures at a real trout in a case, as brown and shining, Esther tells us, as if it had been served with gravy. A trout served with gravy — in a picture — in a sitting room — being stared at by surprised birds. The whole house is like that: benignly, cheerfully, absolutely peculiar. And then — after all that glorious clutter — comes the sentence that pulls it all together. "with its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw; and just wind enough without to sound a low accompaniment to everything we heard, were our first impressions of Bleak House" That phrase — hospitable jingle — is just perfect. It's the sound of a kitchen at work, heard from a comfortable distance, and it means: you are safe, and dinner is coming.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Oliver's right to land on that sentence — because structurally it's doing something quite audacious. Dickens has spent pages cataloguing the quirky, fragmented, slightly comic contents of these rooms. And then that final sentence arrives as a single, long, sweeping period — and it retrospectively transforms every detail that came before. The chaos wasn't chaos — it was warmth in disguise. And notice the word Esther uses about the movables — all those objects in the house: they agreed in nothing but their perfect neatness. That is the grammar of Jarndyce's generosity: variety held together by care. It's a domestic architecture that mirrors a moral one — and Dickens builds it entirely through Esther's patient, grateful observation.

This is a passage I could read aloud until the end of time. Esther is resting at her favourite spot, Charley is gathering violets nearby — it's an image of perfect, pastoral tranquillity. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The triple cadence — a woman's, a lady's, Lady Dedlock's — is one of the great grammatical moments in the novel. Each noun is a different kind of seeing: the first is biological, the second is social, and the third is personal — and loaded with consequence. Esther's eye moves from what the figure is to who it is — and the novel can never go back from that moment. And look at what Esther was doing just before: she had been picturing to herself the female shape that was said to haunt the Ghost's Walk. She was already imagining a woman haunting the grounds — and the real woman walked out of the trees. Dickens choreographs the unconscious — Esther doesn't know what she knows yet, but her imagination is already there.

The Smallweeds are Dickens's most savage joke — and I mean that as the highest compliment. This family has spent generations systematically destroying childhood. Story-books, fairy-tales, fictions, fables — all banished. And what do you get when you raise children with no imagination, no play, no levity whatsoever? Dickens has the answer, and it's magnificent — listen to this: Old monkeys with something depressing on their minds. That is Dickens at his most gleefully merciless — the satirist and the moralist fused into one perfectly absurd image.
