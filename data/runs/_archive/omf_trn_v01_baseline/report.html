James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Twemlow passage, yes — and it's worth pausing on the sheer audacity of what Dickens does there. He introduces a human being — a gentleman, a man of blood and standing — as, and I quote directly, an innocent piece of dinner-furniture. "There was an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James's, when not in use." That single sentence does an enormous amount of ideological work. Twemlow is not a person to the Veneerings — he is a mechanism, stored away when not required, retrieved when a dinner table needs expanding. And the joke deepens when Dickens spells out the logic: the further Twemlow was pulled out — the more guests added — the further he drifted from the centre and toward the sideboard. Social utility and social marginality are, in this world, the same thing.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me structurally is that Dickens doesn't just use the furniture metaphor once and move on — he extends it systematically, leaf by leaf. Sometimes Twemlow and half a dozen leaves, sometimes a dozen leaves, sometimes pulled out to his utmost extent of twenty leaves. That repetition — the accumulation of numbers — mimics the very social inflation it's describing. The prose enacts what it satirises. And the best quote is the one James mentioned — the line about being in frequent requisition and representing the dining-table in its normal state. "Being first cousin to Lord Snigsworth, he was in frequent requisition, and at many houses might be said to represent the dining-table in its normal state." The normal state of a table is absence — it exists to be used, not to exist in itself. Dickens has compressed an entire theory of how the Victorian aristocracy was instrumentalised by the newly rich into a single clause.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That's an important distinction — the satire in this novel is almost never about individuals being wicked. It's about systems — social, financial, institutional — that corrupt or diminish perfectly ordinary people. Which brings me to a passage I find genuinely harrowing — the moment John Harmon reads his own death notice. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What's remarkable is that Dickens steps out from behind the curtain entirely — which he almost never does — and addresses the reader directly about his own method. He actually names what he was doing and what he expected certain readers to get wrong. Here's the passage — and it's one of the most fascinating authorial intrusions in all of Victorian fiction: "I foresaw the likelihood that a class of readers and commentators would suppose that I was at great pains to conceal exactly what I was at great pains to suggest: namely, that Mr John Harmon was not slain, and that Mr John Rokesmith was he." He's drawing a distinction between concealment and suggestion — and that distinction is the whole game. The mystery isn't hidden; it's lit from the side, so that readers who look straight at it see shadow, and readers who look sideways see the shape. That's an incredibly sophisticated structural choice — and then he has the confidence to explain it to us afterward, trusting that explanation won't diminish the achievement.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
This passage is doing a lot of legal and social work, even as it sounds like a man fumbling for words at a kitchen table. Boffin is establishing, for Lightwood — who is effectively the family's solicitor — that he and Mrs Boffin consistently acted in John's interest against the old man's cruelty. It's testimony, as Caroline said — it has the rhetorical shape of sworn evidence, even in Boffin's vernacular. And then comes the moment that's really quite startling in its physicality: "I have seen him, after Mrs Boffin has given him her mind respecting the claims of the nat'ral affections, catch off Mrs Boffin's bonnet — she wore, in general, a black straw, perched as a matter of convenience on the top of her head — and send it spinning across the yard."
