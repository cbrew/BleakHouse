Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, every time I come back to this novel, is how precisely Dickens locates the moral centre in characters the plot seems designed to marginalise. Take the Boffins — a dustman and his wife, servants their whole lives, apparently minor figures in the inheritance plot. And yet Dickens gives us this extraordinary passage explaining why old John Harmon — the dead Jailer of Harmony Jail, as Dickens calls him — left them in his will. The explanation is almost paradoxical: he trusted them precisely because he had raged at them and could not break them. Dickens writes that — and I want to read this exactly — "he had perceived the powerlessness of all his wealth to buy them if he had addressed himself to the attempt." That word — powerlessness — applied to wealth, in a novel entirely about the power of wealth, is doing enormous work. Dickens is telling us, from very early on, that the novel's real currency is moral integrity — and it can't be minted.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor's right that the word powerlessness is startling — but I want to press on what the passage actually reveals about old Harmon's psychology. Dickens says he mistrusted all who bore any resemblance to himself. "sorely indeed he did mistrust all who bore any resemblance to himself" That is a devastating psychological observation, slipped in almost parenthetically. The rich man's paranoia about being robbed is, at its root, a projection — he knows what he himself would do if the positions were reversed. What the Boffins represent isn't simply virtue — they represent people who have not yet been deformed by the system that deformed him. And the tragedy, which the novel traces with extraordinary precision, is whether they can stay that way once the money arrives.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Dickens is doing there is performing a diagnosis with surgical precision. Twemlow is introduced as, and I want to be exact about the phrase here, 'an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors.' 'an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James's, when not in use.' Not a man fallen on hard times. A dining table. Stored away when not required. What makes it devastating is the logic that follows — the more Twemlow is 'pulled out,' the further he is from the centre of power. His aristocratic connection — his first cousinship to Lord Snigsworth — is the very thing that makes him useful and the very thing that ensures his marginality. The Veneerings need him precisely because they are new money performing old money, and he is the authenticating prop.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It is a different register, and that contrast is precisely the point. In the Veneering world, money transforms a man into furniture. In the Harmon world, the absence of money — or rather, the cruelty of those who hold it — transforms a child into an exile. Old Harmon won't hear of allowing a sixpence of coach-money for the seven-year-old boy he's sending away alone. That detail does more work than a paragraph of editorialising would. The father's wealth is measured in the coins he withholds from his son. And then Dickens gives us Mrs Boffin. The passage Boffin narrates is almost unbearable in its warmth. 'Mrs Boffin, then quite a young woman and pictur of a full-blown rose, stands him by her, kneels down at the fire, warms her two open hands, and falls to rubbing his cheeks.' The physicality of that — the two open hands, the kneeling, the warmth she is literally transferring to him — is everything the Veneering world has evacuated from human contact. And then comes the line that haunts the whole novel for me. 'I'd give the wide wide world, I would, to run away with him!' That repetition — the wide wide world — is Dickens writing in a folk register, almost a lullaby. And it breaks the heart precisely because she cannot run away with him.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That folk register is doing something architecturally important, I think. Boffin's narration throughout this novel is rendered in a speech that is rough, unpretentious, phonetically rendered — 'pictur' for picture, 'year old' rather than 'years old.' And yet what he describes has more moral grandeur than anything spoken in the Veneering drawing room. Dickens is making a structural argument: the closer to the dust heap, the closer to genuine human feeling. The Boffins are the hinge of that argument — good people to whom great wealth is suddenly given. Which is the novel's central test case.

What strikes me first about this passage is the sheer structural audacity of it — Dickens is essentially writing a satirical taxonomy.
