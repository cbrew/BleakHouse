Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, returning to Bleak House, is how confident Dickens is about the power of place. He does not hurry toward event. He builds a world first — and demands that we inhabit it. Now, Esther's account of arriving at Bleak House for the first time is, on its surface, a cheerful domestic inventory. Birds in frames staring at a trout, portraits of strangers distributed through the rooms, angels conveying a complacent gentleman to heaven with some difficulty. But underneath the comedy, Dickens is doing something precise and generous. He is establishing Bleak House as a sanctuary — a place of genuine warmth in a novel where warmth will be tested severely. Listen to how Esther closes her first impression of the house. "with its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw; and just wind enough without to sound a low accompaniment to everything we heard, were our first impressions of Bleak House." That phrase — a low accompaniment to everything we heard — is not decoration. It tells us that the house itself is musical — that it resonates.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Edmund is absolutely right about the musicality, and I'd go further — that passage is written to be read aloud. That accumulation of "with its" — three times, four times — it's an incantation. Dickens is casting a spell over the reader, over Esther, over the house itself. Dickens cannot resist that parenthesis: "with some difficulty." Three words that make the angels absurd and endearing simultaneously. He's a very funny writer who is also a very serious one — and the comedy here earns the warmth of that closing sentence.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What moves me in that passage is the detail about the rose-leaves and sweet lavender stored wherever there's a drawer. It's almost invisible — a small aside — but it tells you everything about Jarndyce as a host. Someone has thought about what a drawer smells like when you open it. That's care. And yet — and this is what Bleak House does almost from its first pages — that warmth is immediately shadowed. Because the novel gives us another house — Chesney Wold — and another woman, and a very different relationship to a building's past. There is a passage — the crippled lady, day after day, walking the terrace at Chesney Wold. "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" That is a curse. A house and a family placed under a sentence — and the novel knows it will deliver on that sentence.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What Caroline has done there is identify something central to this novel's method — the way it holds two registers at once. Warmth and dread. Bleak House — even the name — promises both at once. And the curse passage is remarkable for its repetition. "I will walk here" — three times. It has the structure of liturgy. Dickens understood that when we wish to bind the future, we reach for ritual language.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, surveying these passages together, is how Dickens orchestrates moral weight without ever quite preaching. Take the brickmaker's great speech — and it is a speech, a formal oration delivered from a floor of squalor. Dickens does not place the indictment in the mouth of a reformer or a narrator. He places it in the mouth of the man himself — brutal, articulate, and utterly beyond consolation. The passage reads, in part — "Is my daughter a-washin? Yes, she IS a-washin. Look at the water. Smell it! That's wot we drinks. How do you like it, and what do you think of gin instead!" The man pre-empts every question Mrs. Pardiggle might ask — he catalogues his own degradation before she can. That is not the voice of a victim seeking sympathy — it is the voice of a man who has dispensed with illusion.

Edmund, I have read that speech — literally read it, for the audiobook — and I will tell you, it is fiendishly difficult. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Caroline is precisely right, and it connects to something about Dickens's method that I think is misunderstood. People speak of Dickens as melodramatic — as a writer who excess is a governing principle. But these passages show the opposite truth: his restraint, when he deploys it, is absolute. The same precision governs his portrait of Tulkinghorn. The man is described not as wicked but as simply — absent. Dickens writes — "As easily might the tone of a delicate musical instrument be inferred from its case, as the tone of Mr. Tulkinghorn from his case." A shell. A case. No music audible at all. That is more frightening than any outright villain Dickens ever drew.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Tulkinghorn and Vholes belong to the same genus — but Vholes is in some ways the more insidious creation. Tulkinghorn at least doesn't pretend to serve his victims. Vholes wraps his predation in the language of professional duty — and Dickens is forensic about it. He says to Esther — "I HAVE carried it out; I do carry it out. But I will not smooth things over to any connexion of Mr. C.'s on any account." The capitalised I — Dickens means us to hear the emphasis, to hear the self-congratulation disguised as candour. He openly declares Richard's affairs are in a very bad way — and then cheerfully greets Richard the moment he walks in. The pivot is devastating in its comedy and its horror simultaneously.
