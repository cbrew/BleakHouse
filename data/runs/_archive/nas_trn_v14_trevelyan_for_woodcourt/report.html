And then there is the passage from Chapter Forty-Two — which is, I think, where Gaskell finally gives us the full cost of this man's feeling. James Blackstone (social_historian)
It is worth pausing on that word 'slave,' because Gaskell is not using it carelessly. The novel is saturated with the comparison between the condition of the southern drawing room and the condition of the northern mill — and Gaskell, who knew her Manchester, understood that the domestic sphere had its own forms of compulsion. Margaret at this point has been uprooted — from Helstone, from her aunt Shaw's London world — and deposited in Milton. And what Gaskell registers, in the very next chapter, is the physical toll of that displacement — not on Margaret but on her mother. The air in Milton is, quite literally, described as deprived of all revivifying principle. 'The air itself was so different, deprived of all revivifying principle as it seemed to be here; the domestic worries pressed so very closely, and in so new and sordid a form, upon all the women in the family.' 'Sordid' is the key word there. This is not picturesque poverty. This is the grinding, undignified domestic reality of a middle-class family suddenly without resources. And it falls, as Gaskell is unflinchingly honest about, on the women.

That critique lands with real force when you set it against the historical reality of 1850s Manchester. The dominant ideology among manufacturers was precisely what Thornton articulates — that individual self-improvement was the path, and that paternalism was either sentimental or dangerous.
