A Will, A Death, A Question (opening)

Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close reading of one of Dickens's most ambitious, most underrated, and in many ways most modern novels. This is a novel about money, identity, death, and resurrection — and Dickens builds it from the very first page with a corpse dragged from the Thames and a fortune nobody can quite claim. Joining me today are three readers who each bring something irreplaceable to this conversation. Dr. Eleanor Hartley is a novelist and creative writing teacher who reads Dickens the way a watchmaker reads a mechanism — she wants to know exactly how it ticks. Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian whose work asks who benefits and who suffers from the way society organises itself — and Dickens gives him enormous material to work with. And Ms. Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who has lived with this novel since she was a teenager — she reads it for what it feels like, what makes you stop mid-page and simply breathe. We begin where Dickens begins — with a will, a death, and a question that drives the whole machinery of the plot. Old John Harmon, a dust contractor of legendary miserliness, has died and left behind a fortune accumulated from London's filth — and a will with a condition attached that will pull every character in the novel into its orbit. Eleanor, let me start with you — what does Dickens actually do in these opening chapters that is so distinctive?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to the opening is how aggressively Dickens refuses to orient the reader. We begin on the river at night — Gaffer Hexam and his daughter Lizzie pulling something through the dark water — and we are given no context, no backstory, no reassuring narrative hand-holding. Dickens opens with a scene of scavenging that is frankly grotesque, and he trusts us to stay with the discomfort. And then — almost immediately — we cut to a dinner party in the Veneerings' ostentatious home, which is as polished and hollow as the riverbank was raw. That juxtaposition — the body in the river and the dinner table — is announced right at the start as the novel's governing structural principle. Dickens is telling us: everything in this world connects, and nothing connects comfortably.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd push that further, because what Dickens is doing structurally he is also doing politically. The Veneerings — that name is barely even a metaphor, it's a direct statement — they have made themselves out of money so new it hasn't dried. And the guests at that table — Twemlow, Podsnap, the Lammles — are all there to perform society for one another. What Dickens captures is a world in which social position is pure performance, pure surface — and he says so in the very word he chooses for the family name. Meanwhile, the actual production of wealth — the dustheaps, the river, the labour — is happening elsewhere, among people who don't get invited to those dinners. That's not accidental. That is the system working exactly as designed.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I keep thinking about is the will itself — because it's such a strange, almost cruel dramatic engine. Old Harmon has essentially bought his son a wife from beyond the grave. John Harmon junior must marry Bella Wilfer — a girl he has never met — or the fortune passes entirely to the Boffins. And then the young man who is supposed to come home and claim both the fortune and the bride — turns up dead in the Thames. Or does he? That question — that suspended, unverified death — is what gives the whole novel its particular atmosphere of instability. Nobody in this novel is entirely who they say they are, and it starts right here, with an identity that may or may not be a corpse.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline has put her finger on something technically brilliant — Dickens gives us a mystery plot, but the mystery isn't whodunit, it's who IS he. And the genius is that we the readers know fairly early that John Harmon is alive and moving through the novel in disguise as John Rokesmith. Dickens doesn't withhold the secret from us — he withholds it from the other characters. Which means the dramatic irony is incredibly productive — we watch Bella Wilfer gradually fall in love with the man she was meant to marry, not knowing he is that man. It's a masterclass in what you might call ironic intimacy — the reader is always two steps ahead, and that gap is where the emotion lives.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to come back to what that will actually represents as a social document. Old Harmon made his money from dust — from the refuse of the city — which is to say, from the labour of people sifting and sorting London's waste. And rather than dissolving that wealth, redistributing it, letting it do anything generous in the world — he locks it up in a condition of patriarchal control. The will is the legal instrument through which a dead miser continues to dominate the living. That's Dickens at his most clear-eyed about property and inheritance — institutions that launder power across generations and call it natural.
Host (host)
And that sense of wealth as something simultaneously filthy and irresistible runs right through the novel — we'll be returning to the dustheaps and everything they symbolise as the series continues. But before we go further into the novel's machinery, I want to ask each of you something more personal. This novel has a reputation for being daunting — long, densely plotted, with a cast of dozens. Caroline — for someone coming to it for the first time, what is the one thing you would tell them to hold onto in these opening chapters?
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
The river. The Thames in this novel is not scenery — it is a character, and it has moods and a memory and it takes things and gives them back changed. When Dickens describes Gaffer Hexam on the water in that first chapter — the way the body he's towing barely disturbs the surface — there's a terrible poetry to it. The river connects every level of this society — the Hexams at the bottom, the Veneerings at the top — and it doesn't care about any of them. If you hold onto the river, you hold onto the novel's heartbeat.

Eleanor, let's start with you — because I think Dickens announces this theme in an almost architectural way in the very opening pages.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
He does, and the audacity of it is still startling when you go back to it. The novel opens not with a character we're invited to sympathise with, but with Gaffer Hexam on the Thames, towing a corpse for its pocket contents. Dickens frames this as an economy in miniature — the dead are worth more to Gaffer than the living, because the dead can be robbed without complaint. What Dickens is building from page one is a world where value is extracted from bodies — literally. And then he widens the lens to the Veneerings' dinner party, and we realise the extraction is just as total up the social scale — it's merely better dressed. There's a passage early on where Dickens describes the Veneerings' dining table and their guests with this magnificent savagery. He writes of them: 'Mr and Mrs Veneering were bran-new people in a bran-new house in a bran-new quarter of London.' Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that surface is doing ideological work, which I think Dickens understood more clearly than he's sometimes given credit for. The Veneerings are not just comic grotesques — they are a diagnosis of how capital reproduces social legitimacy. You buy the house, you buy the plate, you buy the guests — and within one dinner party you have purchased the fiction of belonging. But the more radical architecture in the novel, to my mind, is the dust-mound inheritance itself. Old Harmon has built a fortune out of the city's refuse — literally the waste that polite society generates and then refuses to see. Dickens is making a pun, and it is one of the most precise puns in English literature: the Harmon fortune is refuse and it is what the respectable refuse to acknowledge. Dickens describes old Harmon as 'the Dust Contractor' and his wealth as accumulated from 'dust, ashes, and such like.' 'Dust, ashes, and such like' — that 'such like' is devastating in its vagueness, because it means everything the respectable world excretes and denies. The entire plot — who gets the money, who marries whom, who transforms themselves for the inheritance — is constructed on top of that mound of filth. Dickens is telling us, with perfect structural clarity, that Victorian society's foundations are not moral — they are excremental.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me, re-reading this, is how funny it is alongside how dark it is — Dickens holds those two things in an almost impossible balance. The Veneering chapters, for me, have always been the most dazzlingly comic sections of the novel. The device of the Analytical — the Veneerings' butler, who silently registers contempt for everything — is a small masterpiece of satire. He moves around the table dispensing wine and disapproval in equal measure, and Dickens never lets him speak — his silence is louder than any speech. But what gets me is the moment when Dickens describes the mirror above the Veneerings' sideboard — which reflects all their guests. He calls it 'the great looking-glass above the sideboard' and says it reflects 'the table and company, and the Analytical Chemist — .' Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline's point about the mirror goes to something I find technically fascinating — Dickens's use of the Veneerings as a satirical frame through which we keep viewing the novel's other plots. They are not protagonists. They are the context — the ambient moral weather of the world John Harmon returns to. And structurally, Dickens keeps returning to their dinner table the way a playwright returns to a single stage set — to show us time passing, society not changing. The genius of Bella Wilfer's arc is that she begins as someone who openly, honestly admits she wants money — which is, in this world, practically radical. She says something to the effect that she has made up her mind to be rich — she is mercenary and she knows it. 'I am mercenary, and I am always to be found when money's concerned' — she says it with a kind of fierce self-awareness that almost everyone else in the novel lacks. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Bella's honesty is important but I want to push back slightly, because I think the novel ultimately domesticates her. Her education is precisely a process of learning to not want money — or rather, to want it in the approved, feminine, subordinated way. John Rokesmith — who is of course Harmon in disguise — tests her precisely by making her live without wealth first. And she passes the test by becoming domestic, small, content — which the novel rewards by then giving her the fortune anyway. The architecture of greed in Our Mutual Friend is deeply gendered — men pursue wealth openly; women are educated out of the desire and then wealth descends upon them. Podsnap's great declaration that something is 'Not English' whenever it challenges his comfort is the male version of this — the refusal to see what the system is built on. He is the Analytical made loud — the same contempt, but expressed as power rather than silence.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That comparison between Podsnap and the Analytical is something I will be thinking about for a while. But I want to come back to the mounds, because I think we haven't quite honoured how strange and wonderful they are as objects in the novel. They loom over Boffin's Bower — they are literally present on the horizon. And Noddy Boffin, the Golden Dustman, carries the mounds with him as a kind of moral history — he is kind precisely because he has not forgotten what wealth is made of. There is that gorgeous passage where Dickens describes Boffin coming into his inheritance — with the mounds as his backdrop. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The Boffin arc is, I think, one of the most contentious structural decisions in the novel — and I find it defensible precisely because of what Caroline describes. When Boffin appears to succumb to avarice — collecting misers, becoming harsh, turning on Rokesmith — many readers feel betrayed. But Dickens is playing a long game: the apparent corruption is a performance, staged to educate Bella. The question is whether the performance is too convincing — whether Dickens has accidentally written genuine avarice. Silas Wegg circling the mounds with his wooden leg and his eyes full of scheming — that is a darker, truer portrait of what the mounds do to people. Wegg is the figure in whom greed is most fully, most grotesquely anatomised — every page he appears on, the prose gets sharper and more savage.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Wegg is essential to this discussion and I'm glad Eleanor raised him. He is a man with no capital — literally a street vendor of fruit and literature — who believes the mounds owe him something. His conviction that a will exists that would give him the fortune is not simply greed — it is the aspiration of the dispossessed, corrupted into paranoia. The system has taught Wegg that the only route out of poverty is a windfall — because there is no other route available to him. So his villainy — real, comic, grotesque as it is — is also the system's product. When Wegg recites poetry to Boffin for wages and then schemes to blackmail him, the distance between those two acts is not as great as it looks. Both are attempts by a man with nothing to extract something from a man with everything — which is, in miniature, the logic of the whole novel.

That is a remarkable formulation — Wegg as the novel's logic made explicit. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Lizzie Hexam. She is the character who, for me, most completely refuses the novel's economy — not because she is above desire, but because she is inside a different set of values entirely. She grew up on the river taking money from the dead alongside her father — and she loathes it. Her entire arc is a flight from that inheritance — not towards money, but towards something the novel can barely name. Eugene Wrayburn sees it in her — that's what draws him, and it's what finally changes him.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I'd add Eugene himself — but only after he almost dies. The near-drowning is structurally a rebirth — Dickens is quite literal about that — and what Eugene is reborn into is a self that doesn't need social approval to validate its choices. He marries Lizzie against everything his class demands — and the novel, unusually for Dickens, refuses to punish him for it. The deathbed scene where he insists on marrying her — where Mortimer watches — is one of the most quietly radical moments in Victorian fiction.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'll offer a more sceptical answer: I think the novel's true escapees are the ones it can barely integrate — Mr. Venus, for instance, in his small way. But more seriously: Riah, the Jewish money-lender who is forced to appear rapacious on behalf of Fledgeby — and knows it. Riah is the most structurally lucid character in the book because he is made to embody the greed of others — he is the face of a system that hides its actual face. He knows exactly what the architecture of greed is, because he is its instrument and its victim simultaneously.

The first thing I want to say is that Dickens never lets money be abstract in this novel. Every transaction has a texture — a smell, almost. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor is right that the structure is a ledger — and I'd press that point further. The Veneerings don't just have new money — they are new money, indistinguishable from the objects they own. What Dickens is diagnosing here is commodity fetishism decades before Marx named it — the way capital turns persons into things and things into persons. And the dust mounds — Harmon's dust mounds — are the novel's great emblem of this inversion. Waste becomes wealth. Filth becomes inheritance. The ground of the plot is literally the accumulated refuse of a society. Dickens gives Boffin a line early on that cuts to the heart of it — when he's asked about the dust, he says something to the effect that money is the root of all evil, and the root of all the dust. The joke is that Boffin can't separate virtue from finance — and neither, Dickens suggests, can his society.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What moves me about the dust mounds is how tactile they are — you feel their weight, their smell, their ugliness. And yet Boffin — the Golden Dustman — is one of the warmest presences in the book. Dickens gives us this wonderful comedy in which Boffin pretends to be corrupted by his sudden wealth — he hires Silas Wegg to read to him about misers. And the language of those scenes — Wegg performing Decline and Fall at that dusty bower — is gloriously absurd. There's a phrase I keep coming back to — when Boffin first takes possession of the house and Dickens writes of him as a man who has come into his property 'staring' at it. It's such a small verb but it carries everything — the bewilderment, the pride, the slight terror of sudden wealth. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd put it slightly differently — though I don't disagree with Eleanor at all. What Dickens knew is that money doesn't just buy things — it rewrites people's moral grammar. Look at the Veneerings — their entire social existence is fabricated from wealth so new it barely has time to cool. Dickens describes their dinner table as 'the Veneering establishment' — and the word 'establishment' does so much work. It's an institution before it's a home — a place for the transaction of social credit. And the guests at that table — Twemlow, Lady Tippins, all of them — they're not friends, they're instruments. What Dickens saw — and what we still haven't fully confronted — is that capitalism doesn't corrupt society from outside. It is society, when society is organised around the pursuit of money above everything else.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline's point about Betty Higden makes me think about craft again — because Dickens earns that moment technically. He doesn't sentimentalise her death — he surrounds it with the beauty of the English countryside, and that contrast is deliberate and brutal. The novel as a whole does that — it holds beauty and ugliness in the same frame, almost without comment. The river gives up the dead and the living in the same gesture — Gaffer Hexam's livelihood is also London's open sewer. Dickens knew that the novel form could hold that doubleness — could make you laugh at the Veneerings and weep for Betty Higden in the same afternoon's reading. That tonal range is what makes it not just a great Victorian novel but a living one.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And I'd add — the ending doesn't entirely redeem the world it has described. Harmon gets his money, Bella becomes genuinely good, Lizzie Hexam gets Eugene Wrayburn — happy endings, of a kind. But Twemlow's final speech — defending Lizzie's marriage against Lady Tippins's social snobbery — is remarkable precisely because it solves nothing. One decent man says a decent thing at a dinner table — and goes home alone. Dickens gives Twemlow the last moral word, and then quietly puts him back in his chambers in Duke Street, alone with his folding bedstead. That is a writer who knows that moral courage and social power are not the same thing.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Twemlow always gets me — he's so easy to underestimate, and then suddenly he's the spine of the whole novel.
