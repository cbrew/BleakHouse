Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, every time I return to this novel, is how Dickens constructs its atmosphere as an argument. The setting is not backdrop — it is moral proposition. Take the train passage in chapter sixty-one — this is Dickens doing something genuinely extraordinary with landscape. He writes — and this is the passage I want us to sit with — "the train rattled among the house-tops, and among the ragged sides of houses torn down to make way for it, and over the swarming streets, and under the fruitful earth, until it shot across the river: bursting over the quiet surface like a bomb-shell, and gone again as if it had exploded in the rush of smoke and steam and glare." A bomb-shell bursting over a quiet surface — that is not descriptive writing. That is a sentence with an argument inside it — about violence, about modernity tearing through something still and ancient. And then he extends it — the train becomes Father Time himself, indifferent to all the little lives running high or low beneath it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right that the writing is extraordinary — I would not quarrel with a syllable of that analysis. What moves me, though, is the moral dimension that runs beneath the spectacle. Father Time going to his end — straight, indifferent, unstoppable — is not merely a metaphor for a railway. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to add something to that picture of the train — because what Dickens describes is also an act of destruction. The ragged sides of houses torn down to make way for it — those are working people's homes, cleared for capital's convenience. But I want to bring in a passage that I think is the moral spine of the entire novel — this declaration about Good and Evil. Dickens is writing about the Boffins — two people ground down by a hard and sordid employer for their entire working lives. And he says — and the narrator means every syllable of this — "For, Evil often stops short at itself and dies with the doer of it; but Good, never." That is not sentiment — that is a theory of history. Goodness, in this novel, is the one thing that accumulates across generations — not money, not dust, not property.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That line is extraordinary — and note how Dickens earns it structurally. He piles up the qualifications first — ten thousand weaknesses, ten thousand vanities — and then cuts through them with that lapidary sentence. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
"The haggard head floated up the dark staircase" — that verb, "floated," is doing something uncanny. It dissociates Bradley from his own body — the head moves without a person apparently attached to it. And that dissociation connects to what Dickens does so brilliantly with John Harmon — who loses his very name under the same pressures. Harmon recounts his drugged attack and says — "I saw a figure like myself lying dressed in my clothes on a bed. What might have been, for anything I knew, a silence of days, weeks, months, years, was broken by a violent wrestling of men all over the room." A figure like himself — wearing his clothes, taking his place, becoming him. Identity in this novel is always available for theft — because in Dickens's London, identity is partly a financial instrument.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor's reading of the mirror scene is precisely right, and I want to add one thing: the moral imagination here is classical. What Bella is experiencing is the ancient distinction between knowing what is right and willing it — the Aristotelian problem of akrasia, weakness of will. She knows what she dislikes in Boffin's advice because she recognises its reflection in herself. That is not satire of a social system — that is a novelist depicting the drama of conscience with great precision. One might almost invoke Dr Johnson: 'The greatest part of our pleasures are illusions, and the only remedy is — clearer sight.' Bella in the mirror is achieving exactly that — however reluctantly.
