It feels like being lowered into cold water, very slowly, and being told this is perfectly normal. What Dickens does in those first paragraphs is extraordinary — there is almost no main verb. It's all noun phrases and fragments, accumulating like — well, like the mud he's describing. And then he does this. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And notice what gets submerged in that mud. It's not the aristocracy. It's not the lawyers in their wigs. It's the foot passengers — jostling, slipping, losing their footing at street corners. Dickens even uses a financial metaphor for the mud — it accumulates, he says, "at compound interest." The very language of capital is embedded in the description of urban squalor. That is not accidental. Dickens understood, perhaps more deeply than he consciously knew, that this filth is a product — produced by a specific economic arrangement. The fog doesn't arrive from nowhere. It is, in every sense, man-made.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That phrase — "compound interest" — is doing more work than it might seem. Dickens is writing in 1852 and '53, and the Court of Chancery — which we will meet very shortly — operated on something very like the compound-interest principle. Costs accrued on costs. Delays compounded delays. Estates were consumed by the very process meant to protect them. The mud of London and the mud of the law are the same substance, described in the same economic vocabulary, in the same opening paragraph. Dickens is not warming up. He has already made his argument.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's a deliberate shock, and I think it's a carefully constructed one. What Dickens gives Esther is warmth, quirkiness, hospitality — and the crucial detail of a generous master brightening everything. Her description accumulates with the same layering technique as the fog opening — but in the opposite emotional direction. She reads the house as a text — portraits displaced into wrong rooms, angels hauling a complacent gentleman to heaven — and makes it feel sheltering rather than threatening. And then she arrives at this — "with its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw; and just wind enough without to sound a low accompaniment to everything we heard, were our first impressions of Bleak House." That word — "accompaniment" — is Oliver's territory really, but I'll steal it: the house is described as having a soundtrack. Wind as musical underscoring. Dinner preparations as percussion. Dickens is writing a scene for the ear, again.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to be careful not to let the warmth do too much work on us here. The house is called Bleak House. That name is not ironic decoration — it is a structural warning. James Blackstone (social_historian)
It is a ghost story — and like all the best ones, it is also a story about what institutions do to individuals. The passage concerns an ancestral Lady Dedlock — crippled, silenced, walking the terrace of Chesney Wold day after day, never complaining. She never speaks to her husband after whatever wrong he has done her — not once — until the afternoon she falls on the pavement. He bends over her, and she looks at him — fixedly and coldly — and then speaks. "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" That is not a supernatural curse — or not only that. That is the compressed rage of a woman whose suffering the aristocratic order has refused to see, finally speaking — at the moment it is too late for her husband to do anything about it.
