Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about The Mill on the Floss — even before the great set pieces — is the narrator's voice. Eliot builds her world on this extraordinary tension between sympathy and irony — she loves her characters and she sees through them, simultaneously. And nowhere is that clearer than in how she handles Tulliver's fate. There's a moment early in the novel where Tulliver has sworn he will never do business with Wakem's clients. Within a fortnight, he has done exactly that — not, Eliot tells us, because his will was weak, but because external fact was stronger. And then she delivers this: "Mr Tulliver had a destiny as well as Œdipus, and in this case he might plead, like Œdipus, that his deed was inflicted on him rather than committed by him." That is an astonishing move. She invokes Greek tragedy — the grandest possible frame — for a provincial miller borrowing money from the wrong man. It is comic and tragic at once, which is precisely Tulliver's condition.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That Oedipus comparison is doing more than elevating a small man to tragic dignity, though — and I think Eliot knows it. The deeper point is about how market forces produce the appearance of destiny. Tulliver has no real choice because capital in St Ogg's has already consolidated around Wakem. The "external fact" that defeats him isn't fate in the cosmic sense — it's monopoly. What Eliot is showing us — and this runs through the whole novel — is a world in which the law and money and respectability all converge in the same hands. And those hands belong to Wakem.

And that double attitude is exactly what Eliot brings to bear on the children too — particularly Maggie. What interests me is what's producing that pain — because it doesn't arrive in a vacuum. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Absolutely — and I think Eleanor puts her finger on exactly the right word: pride. But pride, in this novel, is never simply personal vanity. It is the psychological armour of a man whose class position is already precarious, who feels the ground shifting under him and responds by standing more rigidly upright. What I want to bring in here is the narrator's extraordinary account of what actually drives Tulliver into the lawsuit — because it is not simply hot-headedness. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What moves me in that passage — and it is both funny and painful — is that Mrs Tulliver's influence works precisely because she is seen as a Dodson, not as herself. Eliot says he sees in her 'the representative of the Dodson family' — her individual voice is erased. She is always a category to him, never quite a person. And then there is the Wakem obsession — which Eliot renders in this glorious ventriloquism of Tulliver's paranoid interior. She sets it up and then gives us this — absolutely unforgettable. Caroline is absolutely right about the free indirect discourse — and I'd add that what makes it so devastating is that Eliot does not let Tulliver off the hook. She lets us feel the warmth of his conviction, his certainty that Wakem is a rascal — and then she quietly notes that his own lawyer Gore is probably not up to the fight. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And Eliot makes sure we understand what the ruin actually costs — not in abstract terms, but in the most material, intimate way possible. The scene where Mrs Tulliver goes through her linens is one of the most quietly devastating in the whole novel. She is not an intellectual woman — Eliot has been lightly satirical about her — but in this moment she becomes the novel's truest voice of loss. Listen to how Eliot renders her speech. "To think o' these cloths as I spun myself... and Job Haxey wove 'em, and brought the piece home on his back, as I remember standing at the door and seeing him come, before I ever thought o' marrying your father!" That memory of standing at the door — before marriage, before the mill, before the ruin — carries everything. These cloths are not just property — they are labour, time, identity, hope. When they are sold, it is not merely an economic event — it is the erasure of a life's making.

He was of a very firm, not to say obstinate, disposition, but there was no brute-like rebellion and recklessness in his nature; the human sensibilities predominated, and if it had occurred to him that he could enable himself to show some quickness at his lessons, and so acquire Mr Stelling’s approbation, by standing on one leg for an inconvenient length of time, or rapping his head moderately against the wall, or any voluntary action of that sort, he would certainly have tried it. But no; Tom had never heard that these measures would brighten the understanding, or strengthen the verbal memory; and he was not given to hypothesis and experiment. It did occur to him that he could perhaps get some help by praying for it; but as the prayers he said every evening were forms learned by heart, he rather shrank from the novelty and irregularity of introducing an extempore passage on a topic of petition for which he was not aware of any precedent. But one day, when he had broken down, for the fifth time, in the supines of the third conjugation, and Mr Stelling, convinced that this must be carelessness, since it transcended the bounds of possible stupidity, had lectured him very seriously, pointing out that if he failed to seize the present golden opportunity of learning supines, he would have to regret it when he became a man,—Tom, more miserable than usual, determined to try his sole resource; and that evening, after his usual form of prayer for his parents and “little sister” (he had begun to pray for Maggie when she was a baby), and that he might be able always to keep God’s commandments, he added, in the same low whisper, “and please to make me always remember my Latin.” He paused a little to consider how he should pray about Euclid—whether he should ask to see what it meant, or whether there was any other mental state which would be more applicable to the case. But at last he added: “And make Mr Stelling say I sha’n’t do Euclid any more. Amen.”
