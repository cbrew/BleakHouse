James Blackstone (social_historian)
And then the fog finds its natural home — the Court of Chancery. Dickens sets up the connection with a precision that looks like poetry but is actually jurisprudence. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That fog passage is one of the most precise pieces of social criticism in all of Victorian literature, and I want to insist on that word — precise. Chancery in 1852 was genuinely, historically, a court that could hold a case in perpetuity. Jarndyce and Jarndyce is satirical, yes — but only slightly exaggerated. There were real cases lasting generations, real estates devoured entirely by legal costs. And here is the secret that Dickens exposes: the court's purpose was never resolution. Its purpose was continuation — because continuation meant fees, and fees meant power, and power meant that the lawyers and the masters of the court lived very comfortably indeed.
