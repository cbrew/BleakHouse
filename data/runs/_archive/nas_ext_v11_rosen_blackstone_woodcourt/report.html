James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and I think it's one of the most quietly devastating passages in the book, precisely because it's so easy to overlook. Margaret is standing at a window, looking out at the church towers and beyond them into open sky — and what she finds there is not comfort. What she finds is something worse than an iron dome shutting her in. Gaskell gives her this remarkable thought: "those never-ending depths of space, in their still serenity, were more mocking to her than any material bounds could be — shutting in the cries of earth's sufferers, which now might ascend into that infinite splendour of vastness and be lost — lost for ever, before they reached His throne." That word "mocking" is doing enormous work. Infinity isn't consoling — it's indifferent, which is far more frightening than cruelty. And that phrase — "the cries of earth's sufferers" — isn't abstract theology. This is a novel set in Milton, among factory workers with damaged lungs. Gaskell is placing Margaret's private spiritual crisis inside a much larger, much more material one. And then her father enters, unheard, and touches her shoulder — and says nothing yet.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto James's phrase — "a much more material crisis" — because I think that's precisely what Gaskell is doing, whether consciously or not. Margaret's doubt isn't just personal. It's the doubt of a class — the educated, Anglican southern gentry — confronting a world in which the old consolations are no longer holding. And Gaskell embeds in the very same image — the cries of the sufferers rising and being lost — the condition of the mill workers whose suffering generates the wealth that her class depends on. Margaret can't see God. But what she also can't quite see yet — what the novel will force her to see — is the worker, the hand, the human being producing that suffering below the frame of her vision. The cosmos is mocking her. Milton will answer.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to start somewhere that might seem oblique — with Mrs. Shaw, in the opening chapters, and her little performance of maternal sacrifice. Because I think Gaskell is already, on the very first pages, dissecting the ideology of respectable feeling. She gives us this wonderful, acid sentence about Mrs. Shaw's motives for Edith's marriage. "Mrs. Shaw said that her only child should marry for love,—and sighed emphatically, as if love had not been her motive for marrying the General." In one clause, Gaskell demolishes the entire mythology of Victorian romantic sentiment among the propertied classes. Mrs. Shaw preaches love, but she married for establishment — and she knows it, and Gaskell knows we know it. This is the Southern ruling class in miniature: sentiment as the decorative cover for economic calculation.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That contrast you're drawing, Caroline, has a precise social meaning which the original readers would have felt very sharply. Belgravia was not merely a neighbourhood — it was a specific signifier of the rentier class, of money held in land and funds, not made by any kind of effort. And the General's 'establishment' — his rank and income — was the currency in which Mrs. Shaw's marriage had actually been transacted. The law of the period made this absolutely literal — a wife had no separate property until 1882. What Gaskell calls sentiment, the law called a contract of economic dependency. Which makes Margaret's financial proposal at the end of the novel — her own money, in her own name — genuinely revolutionary.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James has jumped ahead but I want to follow him there, because that final proposal scene is the crux of the whole novel for me. Margaret is holding Lennox's legal papers, framing what she's about to do as a business arrangement. And listen to the precision of what she says. "If you would take some money of mine, eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds, lying just at this moment unused in the bank, and bringing me in only two and a half per cent.—you could pay me much better interest, and might go on working Marlborough Mills." She specifies the sum to the pound. She names the interest rate. This is not a romantic gesture dressed up as business — it is a woman who understands capital, who has read Lennox's proposals, who is conducting a negotiation as a principal. The novel has travelled an enormous distance from Mrs. Shaw's sighing.

That's the honest thing about Gaskell's ending. It is not utopian. Two people find each other across a genuine chasm — of class, of region, of temperament. That is worth something. But the mill is still there. The workers are still there. Mrs. Thornton is still in her locked room. The novel earns its emotion by refusing to pretend that a marriage solves a social order.
