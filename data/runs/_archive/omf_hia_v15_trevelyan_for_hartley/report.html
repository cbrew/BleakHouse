James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me first, coming back to this novel, is how precisely Dickens maps the anxiety of inherited wealth. John Harmon returns from abroad to claim a fortune, and Dickens gives us his interior monologue in a passage that is almost forensic in its self-examination. Harmon interrogates himself — quite literally — in the second person. He says, in that strange, fractured soliloquy: 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife.' That repetition of 'shrinking' — twice — is not a slip. It is the sound of a man physically recoiling from what he is about to become. And notice Dickens gives him the instruction — 'Now, stop, and so far think it out, John Harmon' — as though the character must interrogate himself like a witness.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it's worth pausing on exactly what Harmon is afraid of when he uses the phrase "perpetuating the fate that seemed to have fallen on my father's riches." In law, in this period, a testator could bind his heirs to conditions — including matrimonial ones — and those conditions ran with the money. Old Harmon's will is a legal instrument of coercion masquerading as inheritance. Young John's fear isn't sentimental — it's structural. He has seen how money corrodes, and the scheme he hatches to dress as a common sailor and observe Bella directly is, in a way, his attempt to get outside the legal frame entirely. Dickens is very precise about how that scheme formed — a shipboard confidence, a shared history, a plan to move in Bella's neighbourhood in disguise. "the plot was made out of our getting common sailors' dresses, and throwing ourselves in Bella Wilfer's neighbourhood, and trying to put ourselves in her way" The gentry going native, you might say — if only briefly, and for entirely self-interested reasons.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to circle back to the charity passages, because they tell us something important about the world Bella is navigating. Boffin's tirade about patrons and patronesses is, on the surface, pure comic ranting — but the target is real. Victorian philanthropic culture was saturated with exactly this kind of title inflation — a mechanism for converting money into social prestige. Listen to how Dickens piles up the categories. "Patrons and Patronesses, and Vice-Patrons and Vice-Patronesses, and Deceased Patrons and Deceased Patronesses, and Ex-Vice-Patrons and Ex-Vice-Patronesses, what does it all mean in the books of the Charities that come pouring in on Rokesmith as he sits among 'em pretty well up to his neck!" The absurdity accumulates with each compound title until the whole edifice collapses under its own pomposity. And Rokesmith sits at the centre of it — up to his neck — which is Boffin's very precise image for a man being drowned by other people's vanity.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That letter is not entirely invented, you know. The practice of recruiting wealthy names as charity stewards — at a fee — was entirely standard in Victorian philanthropic circles. The steward's name appeared on the printed list, conferring respectability on the institution, while the institution extracted a tidy sum in exchange. What Dickens captures beautifully is the lithographed intimacy — the letter is "sealed with ducal coronet" and addressed to Nicodemus Boffin "in quite another hand," meaning someone else filled in the name. The personal address is a fiction. The warmth is manufactured. The whole apparatus of aristocratic condescension is mechanised. That is a sharper observation about Victorian charity than many historians have managed.
