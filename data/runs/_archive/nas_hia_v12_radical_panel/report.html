Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I return to this passage is how entirely it belongs to Margaret's consciousness. She isn't described watching the two men — she is recalled to a sense of the present by her mother's low-spoken remark, and then she simply looks up. And in that single unguarded moment of attention, Gaskell gives us a portrait of two men that takes your breath away. She describes her father first — slight, waving lines, eyelids with a peculiar languid beauty which was almost feminine. And then Gaskell turns, and she gives us this, which I find almost physically arresting. "The straight brows fell low over the clear, deep-set, earnest eyes, which without being unpleasantly sharp, seemed intent enough to penetrate into the very heart and core of what he was looking at." Intent enough to penetrate into the very heart and core — that's not a polite social gaze. That's a man who looks at the world as if he intends to understand it completely, or not at all.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Caroline is right to dwell on the faculty of vision here — though I think Gaskell is doing something even more precise than psychological portraiture. She is giving us, through Margaret's eye, a moral typology — two kinds of English manhood set side by side. Mr. Hale — the clergyman who has surrendered his living on a point of conscience — carries his softness in his very face. Every fluctuating emotion passes across those waving lines, Gaskell says. A man of feeling and scruple — admirable, but perhaps not quite equal to the world he has thrust his family into. Thornton, by contrast, is all resolution — the lines in his face few but firm, as if carved in marble. And yet — and this is where Gaskell's genius lives — she gives him that smile. That rare bright smile, she writes, changing the whole look from the severe and resolved expression of a man ready to do and dare everything, to the keen, honest enjoyment of the moment. He is not a statue. That is rather the whole point.

I want to come at this from a slightly different angle, because I think the passage is also about the politics of looking. Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
Margaret's own judgment of Thornton is more interesting to me than his of her — and it reveals her class instincts in operation. In the next chapter she's talking to her father about Thornton — and she makes this remarkable distinction. She disliked almost everything about him — but she lights up when she describes how he spoke about himself. "Everything else revolted me, from its hardness; but he spoke about himself so simply — with so little of the pretence that makes the vulgarity of shop-people, and with such tender respect for his mother." Now — she approves of him when he doesn't sound like a manufacturer. The vulgarity of shop-people — that's her phrase, unexamined, carrying a whole world of inherited prejudice. And yet she also charges him with failing in social responsibility — with never thinking it his duty to train his workers differently. So she is simultaneously snobbish and more radical than she knows — which is what makes her the right character to carry this novel.

And while all of this is happening — the social comedy, the mutual assessments — Gaskell is running another, darker current entirely. Margaret's mother is declining. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The Milton air itself becomes complicit in Mrs. Hale's suffering — Gaskell is quite explicit about that. The air itself was so different, deprived of all revivifying principle — that is the phrase she uses. Helstone had open air, a freshness that was almost moral in its connotations. Milton offers its inverse — a closed, pressing, sordid domestic world. Gaskell is not writing propaganda for the south — she is honest enough to show Helstone's failings later. But here, at this stage, the north is experienced by Mrs. Hale as an affliction — and her body registers what her pride will not admit.

There is something important in the fact that Mrs. Hale's secret is held between her and Dixon — the servant. Dixon would come out crying and cross — her sympathy expressed as irritability. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It's a passage I return to again and again, because it catches Gaskell doing something she doesn't always get credit for. She is not sentimentalising the North. She is anatomising two distinct modes of exploitation — the industrial and the agrarian — and finding both brutal. But what she gives the northern mill worker — through Higgins, through Bessy, through this extraordinary speech of Margaret's — is consciousness. The southern labourer is denied even that. Listen to how Margaret describes it — and this is verbatim from the text. "Those that have lived there all their lives, are used to soaking in the stagnant waters. They labour on from day to day, in the great solitude of steaming fields—never speaking or lifting up their poor, bent, downcast heads." Stagnant waters. Bent, downcast heads. That is not a picturesque landscape — that is a description of a class that has been ground so far down it cannot even look up. And this comes from Margaret — the novel's southern, genteel conscience — which tells you something about how far Gaskell has moved her.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I won't quarrel with the observation — the passage is remarkable. But I think Daniel rather smuggles in his conclusions with his vocabulary. 'Modes of exploitation' — it's a useful phrase, but it rather flattens what Gaskell is doing morally. What strikes me in that speech is not the political economy — it is the quality of moral imagination. Margaret is not delivering a manifesto. She is desperately trying to prevent a good man from making a ruinous decision out of despair. She cares about Higgins — as an individual, as a particular human being with particular intellectual gifts. That is Gaskell's genius — the moral particular, not the political general.
