Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, every time, is how Gaskell introduces Margaret not in the present but through the past — through memory layered on memory. We meet her in a nursery — already a room about childhood, about origins — and she's immediately transported nine years back. Gaskell gives us this marvellous phrase — she was brought, she writes, "all untamed from the forest." Untamed from the forest — to share the polished world of Harley Street. There's something almost fairy-tale in that — the wild child brought into civilization — and Gaskell plays it with enormous tenderness. And then comes this passage about the nursery itself, which I think is one of the finest pieces of comic-gothic in the novel. "She remembered the dark, dim look of the London nursery, presided over by an austere and ceremonious nurse, who was terribly particular about clean hands and torn frocks." That nurse is the whole world of Harley Street compressed into one figure — correct, cold, obsessed with surfaces. She appears for exactly one sentence and she tells you everything.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What Oliver says about surfaces is exactly right — and what moves me is what's underneath them. That little girl of nine, crying bitterly but more quietly so as not to wake Edith — that detail of the suppressed grief is heartbreaking. And then she hushes her sobs when her father comes in — not to distress him. At nine years old, Margaret is already protecting the people she loves from her own pain. That is Margaret's whole character, right there, in embryo. And it prepares us for a passage that comes later in these opening chapters — where the suppression becomes almost cosmic. When Margaret looks out at the night sky after her father's devastating announcement, Gaskell writes something extraordinary. "Those never-ending depths of space, in their still serenity, were more mocking to her than any material bounds could be — shutting in the cries of earth's sufferers, which now might ascend into that infinite splendour of vastness and be lost — lost for ever, before they reached His throne." That repetition — lost, lost for ever — is grief that has no container. It's the same grief as the nine-year-old's, but now it fills the universe.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Both of those passages are telling us something specific about displacement — and it's not only psychological. Margaret is uprooted at nine to serve the social requirements of a family above her in the hierarchy — the grander circumstances, Gaskell calls them, with pointed irony. Her wardrobe must be arranged to suit those circumstances before her father can even visit. The child's feelings are simply not part of the calculation. That pattern — individual suffering subordinated to social obligation — runs through the entire novel. What interests me about Caroline's passage is that when Margaret looks up at the void and finds no God, what she's actually confronting is the absence of any authority that cares. The cries of earth's sufferers — Gaskell's phrase is precise — might be lost before they reach anyone who can hear them. That's not a personal spiritual crisis — that's a crisis of legitimacy. And it's exactly the crisis the factory workers of Milton-Northern are living through every day of the novel.

There's one more passage that belongs in this conversation — and it's almost a comic coda to everything we've said. Thornton, having met Margaret for the first time, walks away muttering to himself. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Thank you — yes, and I think the complication starts very early in the novel, before the strike, before the riot. There's a passage in Chapter Eleven where Margaret reports back to her father after that first dinner at the Thorntons'. She's been repelled by Thornton's boasting about Milton, by his contempt for the improvident poor. But then she catches herself — because one moment broke through the hardness. She says — and Gaskell gives her extraordinary precision here — "Everything else revolted me, from its hardness; but he spoke about himself so simply — with so little of the pretence that makes the vulgarity of shop-people, and with such tender respect for his mother." That is a scalpel of a sentence. Gaskell knows that what makes Thornton legible as a human being is not his achievements but his origins — the shop-boy who didn't forget where he came from. And yet Margaret's critique is also devastating: he despises improvidence without once thinking it his duty to address it. That is the precise charge that the whole novel will spend its remaining chapters trying to answer.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And speaking of his mother — there's that extraordinary passage in Chapter Twelve about Mrs. Thornton's relationship with her two children. Gaskell dissects the way Mrs. Thornton loves — or rather, the way she cannot quite love — her daughter Fanny. She has unconscious contempt for weakness, the narrator tells us — and Fanny is weak in precisely those points where mother and son are strong. And yet the tenderness she reserves for Fanny — the "love" and "dear" — is really a form of shame. The real pride, the real love, is in the line Gaskell gives the narrator — quietly, as if almost reluctant to say it aloud. "Her heart gave thanks for him day and night; and she walked proudly among women for his sake." No "love," no "dear" — just that biblical cadence. This is where Gaskell shows us that the Thornton household is built on a kind of productive hardness — and that hardness has social consequences far beyond the family parlour.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What gets me every time is the physical detail at the end of that passage. Margaret covers her face with her hands — and when she takes them away, the palms are wet with scalding tears. Scalding. Not weeping — scalding. That word does everything. It tells us the shame is burning, physical, beyond her control. And Gaskell never quite lets Margaret escape that tension — between the woman who despises emotion and the woman who feels everything. The resolution in the final chapters softens it. It doesn't remove it.

“I, who hate scenes—I, who have despised people for showing emotion—who have thought them wanting in self-control—I went down and must needs throw myself into the melée, like a romantic fool! Did I do any good? They would have gone away without me, I dare say.” But this was overleaping the rational conclusion,—as in an instant her well-poised judgment felt. “No, perhaps they would not. I did some good. But what possessed me to defend that man as if he were a helpless child! Ah!” said she, clenching her hands together, “it is no wonder those people thought I was in love with him, after disgracing myself in that way. I in love—and with him too!” Her pale cheeks suddenly became one flame of fire; and she covered her face with her hands. When she took them away, her palms were wet with scalding tears.

Daniel Rosen (social_historian)
And I think that's ultimately what makes North and South a great novel rather than merely a good one. Gaskell doesn't tie things off. She shows us a world in the middle of its own transformation — industrial, social, personal — and she has the courage to leave the seams showing. The workers are still hungry. The South is still stagnant. Margaret is still, at some level, bewildered by herself. Love doesn't fix the machinery. It just makes it briefly, radiantly bearable.
