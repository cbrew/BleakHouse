The first thing to understand is that this is not description — it's incantation. Dickens doesn't tell you there is fog in London. He conjures it — repeats the word until it fills the room you're sitting in. When I first read this aloud in a studio, I genuinely felt the temperature drop. That parenthesis — "and dirty" — is Dickens winking at you from inside the fog. It's gravity and comedy in the same breath — which is the whole novel in miniature.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time is how the fog moves — it's not static, it's searching. It creeps into cabooses, it droops on gunwales, it cruelly pinches. That word — cruelly — arrives almost without warning and it changes everything. Because until that word, the fog is atmosphere — after it, the fog has agency, has intent, has malice. And the person it pinches is not a lord or a lawyer — it's a shivering little apprentice boy. Dickens places him there, at the end of that long catalogue, almost as a test: are you paying attention? That boy is Jo before we even know Jo exists.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Caroline is right, and I want to press on that point — because it isn't accidental. The fog touches everyone — the Greenwich pensioner, the skipper, the apprentice — but it touches them differently. The skipper is wrathful, in his close cabin — he has a cabin. The boy is on deck, shivering, with no shelter — the fog is merely inconvenient for one and lethal for the other. Dickens has encoded the class structure into his opening image before he's named a single character. And then — and this is what I find extraordinary — he pivots immediately to Chancery. Here is what that transition sounds like: "Never can there come fog too thick, never can there come mud and mire too deep, to assort with the groping and floundering condition which this High Court of Chancery, most pestilent of hoary sinners, holds this day in the sight of heaven and earth." The fog is not just weather — it is the institution made visible.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I keep returning to is the word "monied" — buried in the middle of that passage. "Which gives to monied might the means abundantly of wearying out the right." That is not a sentimental observation — it is a precise structural analysis of how law functions under capitalism. Justice is not merely delayed — it is rationed by wealth. Dickens understood this more clearly than many of his contemporaries who would have called themselves radicals. He was more radical than he knew — and the fog is the proof.

Host (host)
Welcome back to Bleak House Unpacked, the series where we take Dickens's great fog-bound novel apart, passage by passage, to see how it works and why it still matters. Today's segment is called The Machinery of Injustice: Jo's World. Jo — the crossing-sweeper of Tom-all-Alone's — is arguably the novel's moral centre of gravity. He cannot read, he cannot write, he has no name that anyone writes down. And yet, through him, Dickens delivers some of the most devastating social criticism in the English language. Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads Bleak House as an anatomy of class power — every institution, every courtroom, every act of charity, as a mechanism for keeping the poor exactly where they are. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What makes this passage so remarkable is that Dickens doesn't describe Jo from the outside. He performs the act of inhabiting Jo's consciousness — trying on a life of total exclusion — while simultaneously knowing he cannot fully get there. The whole passage is built on that subjunctive: 'It must be.' Not 'it is.' Not 'he feels.' Must be — the imagined, the inferred, the failure of imagination that is itself the point. And then Dickens does something almost unbearable — he lets Jo speak in the first person, briefly. 'It must be a strange state, not merely to be told that I am scarcely human, but to feel it of my own knowledge all my life.' That 'of my own knowledge' — that is the knife in the sentence. Jo doesn't need the court to tell him he's less than human. He has been taught it so thoroughly he has come to know it himself.
