Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
One must begin, I think, by acknowledging what Dickens is doing structurally in this novel — which is rather unlike anything he had attempted before. He divides the narration between two voices: an omniscient narrator of almost biblical authority, and Esther Summerson, who speaks in the first person with characteristic modesty and characteristic unreliability. The effect is that we are never quite in possession of the whole truth — which is, of course, precisely Dickens's point. The atmosphere of the novel — the fog, the mud, the perpetual twilight of Chancery — is not merely decorative. It is the objective correlative of moral obscurity. And I say that as someone who does not reach for that term lightly.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Edmund is absolutely right about the structural division, and what strikes me — as someone who's had to voice both narrators — is how different the prose actually sounds. The omniscient narrator is all present tense and cold fury, driving forward like weather. Esther is all past tense and careful qualification — she is forever apologising for herself. But she can carry the most extraordinary emotional weight precisely because she underplays. There is a passage in chapter forty-three where Esther describes the anguish of living near her mother — Lady Dedlock — while being unable to acknowledge her. She writes: "Knowing that my mere existence as a living creature was an unforeseen danger in her way, I could not always conquer that terror of myself which had seized me when I first knew the secret." Terror of herself. Not of discovery, not of scandal — of her own being. That is one of the most quietly devastating lines in the whole novel, and it arrives without fanfare in a chapter full of Skimpole's comic nonsense.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Oliver has put his finger on something important there, and I want to pick up on that sense of hidden peril — because it runs through the novel at every level. There is a passage I find almost unbearably concentrated — the old curse on the Dedlock family, attributed to a crippled ancestral lady. She has walked the terrace every day, in silence, in worsening pain, saying nothing — and then she falls. And from the pavement, repulsing her husband as he bends over her, she says: "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" Now, that is gothic machinery — and Dickens knows it is gothic machinery. But the reason it works is that it is completely credible as the speech of a woman who has been humiliated, who has borne her suffering in silence for years, and who chooses this one moment to speak. The pride of the house — that phrase lands differently once you've met the present Lady Dedlock and seen what pride has cost her.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James has put it very precisely — and I'd add that the curse functions not as superstition but as structural prophecy within the novel. Dickens is not asking us to believe in ghosts. He is asking us to believe that the sins and humiliations of one generation do not dissolve — that they accumulate, that they walk. The Smallweeds offer the same point in an entirely different register — comic rather than gothic, but equally relentless. Here is a family that has, over generations, systematically eliminated every trace of childhood from its own children. The narrator's description of them is one of Dickens's great comic-savage passages. "Hence the gratifying fact that it has had no child born to it and that the complete little men and women whom it has produced have been observed to bear a likeness to old monkeys with something depressing on their minds." The word 'gratifying' is doing all the work there — the narrator adopting the Smallweed family's own language to condemn them. Irony as moral instrument — Johnson would have approved.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Old monkeys with something depressing on their minds — I mean, you cannot improve on that. And the marvellous thing is that when you meet Grandfather Smallweed in person, Dickens delivers exactly on the promise of that description. He is simultaneously terrifying and hilarious — which is very hard to do, and Dickens makes it look effortless. But I want to follow James's thread back to Esther — specifically to the end of chapter fifty-nine, when she arrives at the gate. This is, to my mind, one of the most technically brilliant passages in all of Dickens — the way he withholds understanding from Esther, and therefore from us. She sees a woman lying by the iron gate and keeps insisting that this woman was to guide them to her mother. "She lay there, and they stopped me! I saw but did not comprehend the solemn and compassionate look in Mr. Woodcourt's face." She saw but did not comprehend. Because the woman lying at the gate is her mother — and Dickens makes us know it before Esther does. That gap between what we know and what she can bear to know — that is where the tragedy lives.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Oliver has described that scene with real precision, and I think it's worth noting what makes it structurally so devastating. Lady Dedlock has died not in her house, not in her bed, but in the street — at the gate of a paupers' burial ground. She has, in a very literal sense, walked down the social scale from everything that pride and rank could offer to its absolute opposite. The curse from chapter seven has been fulfilled — the pride of the house humbled — but in a form far more terrible than any gothic haunting. That, I think, is what distinguishes Dickens from lesser melodramatists: his punishments are always social as well as personal.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Yes, and it is one of those passages that rewards very careful attention, because its comedy conceals something genuinely unsettling. Dickens gives us Lady Dedlock in all her icy magnificence — and then proceeds to demonstrate, with surgical precision, that she is managed entirely by the people she condescends to. The passage turns on a beautiful paradox. She supposes herself inscrutable — and the text shows us that every dim little star revolving about her knows her completely. The image Dickens reaches for is wonderfully unexpected. "who, in hooking one, hook all and bear them off as Lemuel Gulliver bore away the stately fleet of the majestic Lilliput" The great lady and her entire circle — borne off like a toy fleet by people she has never thought to take seriously. What interests me morally is this: Dickens is not merely satirising snobbery. He is diagnosing what happens to a character who has entirely abdicated the work of self-knowledge.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The passage is accurate as social history, which makes it even more pointed. The librarian, the mercer, the jeweller — these were genuinely the gatekeepers of fashionable consumption in mid-Victorian England. The aristocracy believed they set taste; in practice, taste was manufactured for them and sold back at considerable profit. What Dickens captures is the complete inversion of the apparent social order — who actually holds the power, and who merely believes they do. That question — of apparent versus real power — runs through everything Dickens builds around the Dedlocks.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
And yet — and this is where I part company slightly from a purely sociological reading — Dickens never lets us forget that Lady Dedlock is also a human being under terrible pressure of her own making.
