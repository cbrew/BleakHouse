Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The opening of Bleak House is, I think, the single most technically daring paragraph in Victorian prose fiction. Dickens dispenses with a narrator, dispenses with a character, and gives us instead a condition — the fog as the first fact of the novel's world. What I find consistently underappreciated is the comic dimension running beneath the gothic atmosphere. That is a Dickensian type, and it recurs throughout this novel with the insistence of a moral argument.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Caroline's point about Esther's restraint connects to something I think is easily overlooked in the historical record — the sheer cost of sensitivity in a world built on indifference. The contagion plot — Jo carrying disease, Allan Woodcourt encountering it — is not metaphor alone. Dickens understood that disease moved along the lines of poverty, and he rendered that with documentary precision. There is the moment when Woodcourt — that most decent of men — shrinks back from Jo in involuntary horror. "Allan shrinks back from him with a sudden horror." That is not a failure of character — it is a physiological fact rendered in five words. And the indictment is structural: Woodcourt shrinks because the system has produced a child from whom any decent person would instinctively recoil. Dickens does not let the decent man off the hook — and he does not let us off it either.
