James Blackstone (social_historian)
Thank you — yes, and I want to start with a passage that I think is one of the most economical pieces of social and psychological observation in the entire novel. It concerns Tom — Tom Tulliver, the son of the ruined miller — and his walk into St Ogg's on a morning when he still believes he can repair the family's fortunes. Eliot gives us his state of mind not through interior monologue but through a sustained geographical metaphor — and it is, I think, perfect. The narrator tells us: "Two hours ago, as Tom was walking to St Ogg's, he saw the distant future before him as he might have seen a tempting stretch of smooth sandy beach beyond a belt of flinty shingles; he was on the grassy bank then, and thought the shingles might soon be passed." "But now his feet were on the sharp stones; the belt of shingles had widened, and the stretch of sand had dwindled into narrowness." Two hours: that is the entire span of a young man's economic education. Morning optimism; afternoon reality — and the shore itself has changed, not the boy's vision. This is what I'd call the novel's governing truth about class and aspiration: the horizon retreats at precisely the rate at which you advance.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage — and James has identified the geography beautifully — is the shift of tense and agency buried inside it. Tom doesn't think — the shingles widened themselves; the sand dwindled. The world acts on him; he receives the action. That's a profound structural choice — it's one of Eliot's great gifts, encoding a character's powerlessness in the grammar itself rather than announcing it. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund, I wouldn't entirely separate the moral from the social — this novel rather insists they are the same terrain. Eliot can be very funny about exactly this, which is something readers sometimes miss. There is a marvellous comic aside later in the novel — when Tom is lodging in modest rooms, still nurturing enormous plans — and the narrator turns directly to the reader. She writes of his throwing the bell-rope to the far end of the room — not very far either — and adds: "a touch of human experience which I flatter myself will come home to the bosoms of not a few substantial or distinguished men who were once at an early stage of their rise in the world, and were cherishing very large hopes in very small lodgings." The irony is perfectly pitched — 'substantial or distinguished men' who have since forgotten, or prefer not to recall, that the belt of shingles was once very wide indeed. That is the social historian's quarry — the way ambition rewrites its own origin story.

And the structure of that waking is extraordinary — dream layered upon false waking layered upon the real, until she is left with nothing but the bare fact. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The novel's final accounting of all this — what Maggie's suffering costs and what remains after it — is done with a restraint I find genuinely admirable. Consider what Eliot says of Maggie when she has returned and Tom has rejected her — when the last human anchor has been cut. "The idea of ever recovering happiness never glimmered in her mind for a moment; it seemed as if every sensitive fibre in her were too entirely preoccupied by pain ever to vibrate again to another influence." Not tragic self-pity — the sentence is clinical in its accuracy. 'Every sensitive fibre' — there is nothing melodramatic here; this is an honest phenomenology of grief, rendered with the precision of someone who has been there. That is what makes Eliot, for me, the supreme moralist of the English novel.

The feeblest member of a family—the one who has the least character—is often the merest epitome of the family habits and traditions; and Mrs Tulliver was a thorough Dodson, though a mild one, as small-beer, so long as it is anything, is only describable as very weak ale: and though she had groaned a little in her youth under the yoke of her elder sisters, and still shed occasional tears at their sisterly reproaches, it was not in Mrs Tulliver to be an innovator on the family ideas. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately is that Eliot builds the Dodson world with the precision of a satirist but the patience of a portraitist. She catalogues their rituals — bleaching the linen, making cowslip wine, curing the hams — and the accumulation is both comic and, quietly, devastating. Because what she is constructing is a world in which tradition is identity, and identity is tradition — and there is no gap between them for anything like Maggie to breathe. The detail that lodges in me every time I read it is this one — she writes of a female Dodson visiting other households, "a female Dodson, when in 'strange houses,' always ate dry bread with her tea, and declined any sort of preserves, having no confidence in the butter." That phrase — having no confidence in the butter — is doing so much work it almost buckles under the weight. It is snobbery, but it is also a kind of epistemological rigidity — the Dodsons cannot trust anything outside their own system of knowing. And Mrs Tulliver is a thorough Dodson — Eliot's image of her as small-beer is perfect: not enough character to rebel, just enough to preserve the type.
