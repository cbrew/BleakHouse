James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me, coming back to those opening chapters, is how precisely Dickens understood what Chancery actually was — and how long it could hold a person. People tend to read the fog as pure metaphor — oppression made visible — but there's something more surgical in the satire. Dickens catches the system in its holiday, which is almost funnier than catching it at work. There's a passage in Chapter Nineteen where he describes the Long Vacation — the courts shut, the lawyers scattered across the Continent. He writes: 'The good ships Law and Equity, those teak-built, copper-bottomed, iron-fastened, brazen-faced, and not by any means fast-sailing clippers are laid up in ordinary.' That list — teak-built, copper-bottomed, iron-fastened — is pure mock-heroic. Each adjective adds to the ship's apparent solidity, and then 'brazen-faced' turns the whole thing on its head. The institution is not slow because it is careful — it is slow because it is shameless.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James is right to savour the rhetoric — that list is genuinely funny — but I want to resist the idea that the satire is simply aimed at an institution. Dickens is also lampooning the people who feed the institution — who keep it alive through their credulity, their hope, their inability to let go. The Flying Dutchman image makes this perfectly clear — 'a crew of ghostly clients imploring all whom they may encounter to peruse their papers.' They are haunting their own lawsuit. The law has not merely failed them — they have become it. That is a moral observation as much as a social one, and it is where Dickens's real power lies.

What gets me about that passage — every time — is the rhythm of it. Teak-built, copper-bottomed, iron-fastened — it's practically iambic. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That curse is doing more historical work than it might appear to. The Dedlock family pride that she invokes is inseparable from the legal and social structures that protected aristocratic property — entails, settlements, the whole apparatus. And Chancery is part of that apparatus — the court of equity that was supposed to be more humane than common law, and became instead its most elaborate trap. The line that completes the curse is worth hearing in full: 'And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!' That word 'disgrace' is precisely calibrated — not merely ruin, but social shame, the undoing of a reputation. Which is, of course, exactly what the novel will deliver.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I think that passage reveals something James's institutional reading, fine as it is, might underplay. The woman's silence before the curse is the point — 'she never spoke to any one of being crippled or of being in pain.' That restraint is a form of pride in itself — not the Dedlock pride she condemns, but something more austere and more admirable. That is not a conservative evasion — it is a moral seriousness that political readings too easily flatten.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I take Edmund's point, but I don't think you can have it one way or the other — the suffering and the system are interlocked in Dickens. There's a passage about the Smallweed family that makes the same argument from pure comedy — and it's almost more devastating for that. Dickens describes how the Smallweed line has, across generations, 'discarded all amusements, discountenanced all story-books, fairy-tales, fictions, and fables, and banished all levities whatsoever.' And then the punchline — and it really is a punchline — arrives: 'Hence the gratifying fact that it has had no child born to it and that the complete little men and women whom it has produced have been observed to bear a likeness to old monkeys with something depressing on their minds.' 'Gratifying' — that mock-approving word — is doing everything. The system has produced children who are not children — and Dickens frames it as an achievement.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Smallweeds are the novel's most concentrated example of what commercial culture does to human development. Going out early in life, marrying late — that formula is repeated across three generations in the space of one paragraph, almost like a legal precedent being cited. Each generation reproduces not just its habits but its very physiology — the 'lean and anxious' mind passed down like a bequest. And yet this family is central to the Jarndyce case — which is to say, the law and the degradation of childhood are not separate themes in this novel.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Thank you. What strikes me first about the Lady Dedlock passage is its extraordinary satirical precision — Dickens at his most Swiftian. Lady Dedlock believes herself to be — Dickens's phrase is quite magnificent here — an inscrutable Being, quite out of the reach and ken of ordinary mortals. And yet every tradesman, every dressmaker, every librarian has taken her exact moral measure. The comparison Dickens reaches for is, I think, one of his great comic images. He tells us that these deferential people lead her and her whole troop after them — — who, in hooking one, hook all and bear them off as Lemuel Gulliver bore away the stately fleet of the majestic Lilliput. The joke is perfect — the powerful are the tiny ships, and the tradesmen are the giant. But there is something beneath the comedy — a moral point about how vanity makes one manipulable, how the pretence of power invites the reality of dependence.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Sladdery figure is historically precise — the fashionable circulating library was a genuine institution of social leverage in this period. Librarians, jewellers, mercers — these were the conduits through which taste was manufactured and sold back to the aristocracy as their own discovery. Dickens's satirical target is quite precise: a class that believes its preferences are natural, when in fact those preferences are engineered. Which is exactly what Chancery does to Richard — it engineers his ruin while appearing merely to receive his hope.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Mrs. Badger's observation is not merely domestic — it maps onto a real Victorian anxiety about young men of middling means and no fixed profession. The mid-century was full of Richards — men whose expectations exceeded their capacities, who drifted between callings looking for the one that asked nothing difficult of them. What Chancery does — with devastating efficiency — is give Richard a structure that looks like purpose while actively rewarding idleness and magical thinking. He doesn't need medicine, or law, or the army — he only needs the suit to come good. And the suit never comes good.
