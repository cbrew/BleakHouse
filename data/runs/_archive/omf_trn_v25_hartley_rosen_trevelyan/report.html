Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The thing that gets me every time I open this novel is the audacity of it. Dickens, at the height of his fame, publishing in monthly parts, builds an elaborate mystery around a man who may or may not be drowned — and then, mid-novel, breaks the fourth wall entirely. He turns to the reader — and I mean directly turns — and says, in effect: yes, you've guessed it, and that was the point. Here is exactly what he writes — and I love this passage enormously. "I foresaw the likelihood that a class of readers and commentators would suppose that I was at great pains to conceal exactly what I was at great pains to suggest: namely, that Mr John Harmon was not slain, and that Mr John Rokesmith was he." He is not apologising — he is educating his audience in how fiction works. The mystery was never the secret — the mystery was the atmosphere, the dread, the pleasure of deferral.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And notice how he frames his own confidence — "thinking it worth while, in the interests of art, to hint to an audience that an artist may perhaps be trusted to know what he is about." That phrase — "in the interests of art" — is doing something quite precise. He's distinguishing between the reader's momentary suspense and the novel's deeper design — and insisting the design takes precedence. For a writer working in serial parts, under enormous commercial pressure, that is a genuinely radical statement of artistic authority. The Harmon mystery isn't a puzzle to be solved — it's a structural engine that keeps the novel's central question alive: who is John Harmon, really, beneath any name?

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to stay with that question — who is John Harmon beneath any name — because what this novel understands is that names and money are the same system. John Harmon inherits a fortune made from dust — from the refuse of London — only if he marries a woman he has never met, on his father's posthumous instruction. His death, real or faked, is in a sense the only freedom available to him — the only way out of being property. And then look at what Mrs Boffin — the warm-hearted woman who inherits in his place — immediately wants to do with that money. "Now that he is past all benefit of the money, and it's come to us, I should like to find some orphan child, and take the boy and adopt him and give him John's name, and provide for him." She wants to restore the name — fill it with a living body, a child, so the money can do some human good. It's a tender impulse — but it also reveals how thoroughly this world thinks in terms of names as property rights, legacies to be transferred rather than lives to be lived.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Dickens does in that passage is something structurally quite brilliant — he builds a catalogue, and the catalogue itself becomes the argument. It begins with this wonderful rhetorical exclamation — 'Oh the varieties of dust for ocular use, offered in exchange for the gold dust of the Golden Dustman!' That single sentence sets the whole engine running — dust in exchange for gold dust. Charity is reframed as a transaction, and not even an honest one — the charities are offering worthless dust back for something real. Then Dickens gives us the numbers — fifty-seven churches, forty-two parsonage houses, seven-and-twenty organs — and the accumulation is almost musical. It tips into absurdity on purpose — twelve hundred children to be brought up on postage stamps. The bathos of that final item is a comic technique, but it's also a moral one — it reduces the children to a denomination. And then comes the centrepiece — the Duke of Linseed's letter, read aloud in full. 'Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet.' That description is doing so much work in so few words — the physical weight of the letter, the aristocratic seal, the pretence of intimacy. And then the sting in the postscript — the steward's fee is three guineas.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I'd add is that Dickens isn't merely satirising bad manners — he's exposing the structural relationship between wealth and philanthropy. Charity in this passage is never about the poor. It's about the reproduction of aristocratic prestige — the duke lends his coronet to the enterprise, and Boffin's money launders the whole thing into respectability. That's why the noble earls and the viscount 'combine' — there's a corporate logic to it. They are offering Boffin the chance to buy social legitimacy — to attach his name to theirs. And when they point out that 'if Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, should wish to present two or more purses,' it would be perfectly acceptable — that's not generosity, that's an upsell.

Daniel's point about prestige leads directly into the individual beggars passage, because there the machinery shifts from institutional to personal — and it becomes much darker. Dickens uses the same cataloguing technique, but now the irony has genuine tragedy underneath it. He sets up the key comparison early — the beggars claim to have documents, but as he puts it: 'they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf.' The image is grotesque and funny — minced veal to a calf — but it also speaks to the distance between aspiration and reality. These people are constructing official-sounding claims out of nothing because they have nothing — and the novel knows that.

These are the corporate beggars. But there are, besides, the individual beggars; and how does the heart of the Secretary fail him when he has to cope with them! And they must be coped with to some extent, because they all enclose documents (they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf), the non-return of which would be their ruin. That is to say, they are utterly ruined now, but they would be more utterly ruined then. Among these correspondents are several daughters of general officers, long accustomed to every luxury of life (except spelling), who little thought, when their gallant fathers waged war in the Peninsula, that they would ever have to appeal to those whom Providence, in its inscrutable wisdom, has blessed with untold gold, and from among whom they select the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, for a maiden effort in this wise, understanding that he has such a heart as never was. The Secretary learns, too, that confidence between man and wife would seem to obtain but rarely when virtue is in distress, so numerous are the wives who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted husbands, who would never permit it; while, on the other hand, so numerous are the husbands who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted wives, who would instantly go out of their senses if they had the least suspicion of the circumstance. There are the inspired beggars, too. These were sitting, only yesterday evening, musing over a fragment of candle which must soon go out and leave them in the dark for the rest of their nights, when surely some Angel whispered the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, to their souls, imparting rays of hope, nay confidence, to which they had long been strangers! Akin to these are the suggestively-befriended beggars. They were partaking of a cold potato and water by the flickering and gloomy light of a lucifer-match, in their lodgings (rent considerably in arrear, and heartless landlady threatening expulsion ‘like a dog’ into the streets), when a gifted friend happening to look in, said, ‘Write immediately to Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire,’ and would take no denial. Mrs Boffin, then quite a young woman and pictur of a full-blown rose, stands him by her, kneels down at the fire, warms her two open hands, and falls to rubbing his cheeks; but seeing the tears come into the child’s eyes, the tears come fast into her own, and she holds him round the neck, like as if she was protecting him, and cries to me, “I’d give the wide wide world, I would, to run away with him!” I don’t say but what it cut me, and but what it at the same time heightened my feelings of admiration for Mrs Boffin. The poor child clings to her for awhile, as she clings to him, and then, when the old man calls, he says “I must go! I went aboard with him (I gave him first what little treat I thought he’d like), and I left him when he had fallen asleep in his berth, and I came back to Mrs Boffin. But tell her what I would of how I had left him, it all went for nothing, for, according to her thoughts, he never changed that look that he had looked up at us two. But it did one piece of good. Mrs Boffin and me had no child of our own, and had sometimes wished that how we had one. But not now. “We might both of us die,” says Mrs Boffin, “and other eyes might see that lonely look in our child.” So of a night, when it was very cold, or when the wind roared, or the rain dripped heavy, she would wake sobbing, and call out in a fluster, “Don’t you see the poor child’s face?
