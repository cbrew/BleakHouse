Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Oliver is pointing to is something Eliot does structurally throughout the novel — she seeds the ending in the beginning. The intelligence Maggie has — that restless curiosity that electrifies Tom's dull lesson — is the same force that will later put her fatally outside the boundaries her world has drawn. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I return to the childhood chapters is how ruthlessly honest George Eliot is about what jealousy does to a child. There's a passage with Maggie, Tom, and their cousin Lucy that I find almost shocking in its directness. "Maggie would have thought a little while ago that she could never be cross with pretty little Lucy, any more than she could be cruel to a little white mouse; but then, Tom had always been quite indifferent to Lucy before, and it had been left to Maggie to pet and make much of her." That qualification — "but then" — is doing so much work. It's not excusing Maggie. It's explaining her, which is a different and more demanding thing. And then — the little white mouse. That image lands so precisely, because Lucy is small and soft and innocent, and the cruelty Maggie imagines is against exactly that innocence. Eliot never lets us look away from what Maggie is feeling. That's a form of deep respect — for the child, and for us.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I keep coming back to is the way this childhood scene rhymes structurally with everything that happens to Maggie later. Her hunger is always turned inward, or sideways — she can never strike at the thing she actually wants. And then the novel does something extraordinary with the father — because Mr Tulliver is the character who focusses that love and that helplessness most piercingly. There's a moment when Maggie sees her father broken that Eliot frames with one of the most concentrated sentences in the book. "All we have hoped or delighted in, all we can dread or endure, falls away from our regard as insignificant; is lost, like a trivial memory, in that simple, primitive love which knits us to the beings who have been nearest to us, in their times of helplessness or of anguish." Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Eliot actually prepares us for that defeat much earlier, with a remarkable passage about Mr Tulliver and fate. He's trying to arrange a loan, and he's determined it shall be from no client of his enemy Wakem's. And of course it turns out the only person available is exactly Wakem's client. The narrator's comment is astonishing. "Mr Tulliver had a destiny as well as Œdipus, and in this case he might plead, like Œdipus, that his deed was inflicted on him rather than committed by him." The comedy of scale is deliberate — a miller from the English Midlands placed alongside Oedipus. But Eliot is perfectly serious. She is insisting that the ordinary man's undoing has exactly the structure of tragedy. Not the grandeur — the structure. The thing inflicted rather than chosen. And that is the world Maggie is born into — a world where forces stronger than will shape what happens to you.
