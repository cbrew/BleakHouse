The great pleasure of Mr Tulliver — and there is enormous pleasure in him — is that Eliot gives you the full portrait before he's done a single thing. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is the economic specificity underneath the psychological comedy. Mr Tulliver is about to call in a debt from his own sister's family — people who can barely survive the demand. And Eliot gives us the mechanism of self-deception with forensic precision: he reframes cruelty to family as a "spirited step." The novel is relentless about this — the way respectable people in financial distress dress up their predations as principle. Mr Tulliver genuinely believes he is being decisive and honourable — and that belief is exactly what makes him dangerous to his own family.

What I love about this passage — and I mean love with the devotion of someone who has read it aloud more than once — is the precision of the comic escalation. But I want to press on what the gypsy fantasy itself reveals. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And this incompatibility — this inability of people formed by different circumstances to truly fuse — runs through the whole novel. There's a passage I keep returning to about Tom and Philip — about the limits of feeling as a bond between people. The metallurgical metaphor is cold and precise — and deliberately so. I want to press on one thing, though — because I think the novel is asking a harder question than it's sometimes given credit for.
