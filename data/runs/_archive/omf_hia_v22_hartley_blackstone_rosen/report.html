James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a passage early in the novel that I return to whenever I want to explain what Dickens is doing with class in this book. It's the first time we see the interior of the Bower — the Boffins' home, built on a fortune made from dust and waste. Dickens describes it through the eyes of Silas Wegg, a street-vendor Boffin has hired to read to him — a man who fancies himself a social observer. And the description Dickens gives us is one of the most compressed pieces of social satire in the novel. "It was the queerest of rooms, fitted and furnished more like a luxurious amateur tap-room than anything else within the ken of Silas Wegg." A luxurious tap-room — that phrase does a great deal of work. The Boffins have money — real money, inherited from old Harmon — but they have no framework for spending it that fits any recognised class position. So you get drawing-room furniture — garish, expensive — placed next to tavern settles and a region of sand and sawdust on the floor.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that room is the precision of its comedy — Dickens is doing something very deliberate with spatial organisation. The carpet stops at Mrs Boffin's footstool — that's the border between aspiration and reality, and it runs right through the middle of the room. On the genteel side: stuffed birds, waxen fruits under glass — hollow ornament. On the sawdust side: a cold joint, a large pie, things that are actually useful — compensatory shelves, Dickens calls them. The word 'compensatory' is extraordinary — it suggests the Boffins know, on some level, that fashion is a fraud, and they've quietly kept hold of the real. The books, meanwhile — the eight volumes Wegg is meant to read aloud — are ranged flat like a galvanic battery. A galvanic battery! They're not there to be read — they're there to deliver a charge, to improve the Boffins by some vaguely understood electrical process of culture.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Both of you are right to linger on that room — but I want to push on what it means that we're seeing it through Wegg's eyes. Wegg is a man with nothing — a street-seller, a ballad-vendor — who has appointed himself a connoisseur of other people's social failures. The Boffins' awkward wealth irritates him — not because he wants justice, but because he wants leverage. And Dickens is absolutely clear-eyed about what that resentment is — it's not solidarity, it's envy transformed into grievance. There's a later passage that crystallises this, where Dickens uses the taxidermic specimens in Wegg's orbit to comment on his jealousy. "The yard or two of smile on the part of the alligator might have been invested with the meaning, 'All about this was quite familiar knowledge down in the depths of the slime, ages ago.'" The alligator has seen it all before — the transformation of a benefactor into an injurer by a man who can't tolerate gratitude. That's Wegg's whole story in a sentence — and Dickens encodes it in a stuffed reptile's grin.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
If we're talking about what the novel's central obsession actually is — and it is money, inescapably — then there's another passage I want to bring in, because it captures the stakes in a way that still feels shocking. John Harmon — the nominal heir to the dust-mound fortune — narrates his own near-murder. He discovers a placard announcing that John Harmon has been found dead and mutilated in the river — and that the corpse is not his but Radfoot's, a man killed wearing John's clothes. The logic of it is almost too bleak to follow — here is what Dickens gives us: "I perceived that Radfoot had been murdered by some unknown hands for the money for which he would have murdered me, and that probably we had both been shot into the river from the same dark place into the same dark tide." The same dark place, the same dark tide — two men, both nearly killed for an inheritance that comes from a heap of other people's waste. The money originates in dust — and it kills, or nearly kills, everyone it touches before the novel is done.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The repetition of 'same dark place into the same dark tide' — that's not accidental. Dickens is using the river as a great leveller — it doesn't distinguish between the man who kills for money and the man who was to be killed for it. They both end up in the same water, subject to the same current. And structurally, John narrating his own near-death creates this vertiginous doubling — he is simultaneously the survivor and, in the eyes of the world, the corpse. It's a narrative device that lets Dickens explore identity under erasure — what are you when the world believes you are dead?

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And I'd add — the murder logic here is remarkably pure. Radfoot kills — or attempts to kill — for money he doesn't have any legitimate claim to, having tricked Harmon out of his identity. Then unknown hands kill Radfoot for the same money. The money passes upward — out of the river, into the Boffins' possession — while the men who fought over it disappear into the current. That is a diagram of how capital works — the wealth accumulates at the top while the violence of its accumulation is absorbed by those at the bottom. Dickens didn't frame it in those terms, but the novel's structure makes the argument all the same.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me about that authorial intervention is how it sits alongside a very serious legal and material question — the question of who actually inherits old Harmon's fortune. Dickens is playing with identity as an aesthetic puzzle, but the novel's plot depends on identity as a legal category — and the consequences of that are anything but playful. The Dutch bottle passage lays this out with real precision. Harmon explains to Wegg — and through Wegg, to us: "That Dutch bottle, scoundrel, contained the latest will of the many wills made by my unhappy self-tormenting father. That will gives everything absolutely to my noble benefactor and yours, Mr Boffin, excluding and reviling me, and my sister (then already dead of a broken heart), by name." "Self-tormenting father" — that is a very precise legal and psychological portrait in three words. The old man has made many wills — a form of power exercised from the grave, or rather, exercised in the anticipation of the grave. And the final will does not merely disinherit John and his sister — it reviles them by name. There is a particular Victorian cruelty to that — the testator reaching across death to wound his children one last time, with the authority of a legal document.

And yet Boffin buries the will rather than destroy it — and the novel frames this as noble. I think we need to sit with that for a moment, because it's a genuinely complicated gesture. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Which is what makes the Wegg scenes — the scenes where the real Harmon finally reveals himself and settles accounts — so charged. When Harmon grabs Wegg by the cravat, the novel is giving us something we've been denied throughout — physical authority exercised by the man who was robbed of it.
