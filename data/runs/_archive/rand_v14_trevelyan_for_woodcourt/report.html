Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The first thing to say about Bleak House is that it doesn't ease you in. It drops you into fog — literal, symbolic, legal fog — with a present-tense narration that was genuinely radical for 1852. That opening chapter is Dickens announcing: the rules have changed. But what I find equally fascinating is how quickly the novel introduces its cast of moral hypocrites — and how merciless the comedy is. Take Mrs. Pardiggle — a charity worker who treats the poor like administrative problems to be processed. "Well, my friends," she says — and then Esther adds the killer observation: "her voice had not a friendly sound, I thought; it was much too business-like and systematic." James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it's worth pausing on just how precisely Dickens calibrated that portrait. The philanthropic busybody was a well-recognized type in the 1850s — earnest, middle-class, utterly convinced of her own virtue. The brickmaker's family that Mrs. Pardiggle descends upon had no recourse, no polite way to refuse entry. That power imbalance is the hidden architecture beneath the comedy. The novel is full of people exercising authority over those who cannot push back — and Dickens makes sure we feel the weight of that. Look at Sir Leicester Dedlock — a man whose entire sense of self is constructed from rank and condescension. When he dismisses Lawrence Boythorn — a man who dares to disagree with him — the language is magnificently throttled with contempt. "A man of a very ill-regulated mind. Sir Leicester has no argument — only repetition, as if volume and syntax could substitute for reason. It is a perfect portrait of aristocratic intellectual vacancy dressed in the clothes of authority.

And that's the thing about the language in this novel — every character has an idiom that is entirely their own.
