Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That word — performs — is exactly right, and it matters. The Court of Chancery in this novel is not just corrupt — it is comfortable. That is a much more damning charge than mere corruption, and Dickens earns it with a single scene. When Esther visits the court in Chapter Twenty-Four, she gives us this: "some awake and some asleep, and one talking, and nobody paying much attention to what he said." Nobody paying attention to what he said. This is a court of law — a place where people's entire lives, their inheritances, their futures, are supposedly being adjudicated. And the men inside it are dozing, reading newspapers, whispering in groups — extremely comfortable. Comfort is the instrument of class power here — it signals that this institution serves those inside it, not those who come before it in desperation.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel is right to press on that comfort — and the historical record makes it more grotesque, not less. The real Court of Chancery in the mid-nineteenth century had a backlog that stretched across decades. Cases from the seventeen-nineties were still technically live when Dickens was writing. The Lord Chancellor leaning back in his — and I want to quote Esther's precise phrase — his "very easy chair." "The Lord Chancellor leaned back in his very easy chair with his elbow on the cushioned arm and his forehead resting on his hand." Cushioned arm. While people outside that room — people like Richard Carstone — are slowly destroyed by the waiting. Dickens did not need to exaggerate the Court of Chancery. He merely had to describe it accurately, and the description was already an indictment.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That line is doing something quite radical, and I'd push Oliver's reading a step further. Esther — a middle-class narrator with middle-class sympathies — is confessing that she cannot actually see the world of the poor clearly. Which means the novel, at the level of its own narration, is acknowledging the limits of the social gaze it operates with. And yet Dickens also gives us moments that rupture that limitation — like Jo, the crossing-sweeper, addressed directly by the narrator. The omniscient narrator turns to Jo and says — tenderly, not pityingly — "There is something like a distant ray of light in thy muttered reason for this: 'He wos wery good to me, he wos!'" The misspelling — 'wos wery good' — is not condescension. It is the closest the novel can get to transcribing a voice the written word normally erases.

Jo's legal status in this novel is historically precise in a way that should make readers furious. Daniel, let's start with you — what is Dickens actually doing when he puts an inquest at the heart of this chapter?
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What he's doing is staging a performance of justice — a ritual that has the form of inquiry but not the substance of it. The inquest exists, officially, to establish facts about a death. What Dickens shows us is that its actual function is to filter out the testimony that would be most revealing. Look at what Jo is asked to establish before he's even allowed to speak substantively. "Name, Jo. Nothing else that he knows on. Don't know that everybody has two names." Dickens compresses an entire social biography into that fragment. One name, no education, no family — and therefore, in the law's eyes, no credibility. The system doesn't fail Jo accidentally — it is calibrated to produce exactly this outcome.

And it's worth being precise about what that calibration looked like in practice. At the time Dickens is writing, a witness could be excluded from giving evidence at a coroner's inquest on grounds of religious ignorance — of not understanding the nature of an oath.
