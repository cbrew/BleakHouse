Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Yes — and it's a room that rewards the most careful attention, because Dickens is doing something almost architectural with it. This is the Bower — the Boffin residence — and Silas Wegg has just been admitted for the first time. Dickens gives us the room through Wegg's eyes, which is already a kind of joke, because Wegg is an unreliable judge of almost everything. But even through those eyes, the room declares itself immediately. Dickens writes: "It was the queerest of rooms, fitted and furnished more like a luxurious amateur tap-room than anything else within the ken of Silas Wegg." A luxurious amateur tap-room — that collision of adjectives alone is a small essay on the Boffins' position in the world. They have money — new, sudden, enormous money — but they have no framework for what money is supposed to look like.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one mustn't be too quick to read that room as a class indictment. What strikes me is the warmth of the description — the steaming kettle, the cat on the hearth, the cold joint plainly discernible on the shelf. These are not the signs of vulgarity being mocked — they are the signs of genuine comfort, of a household that feeds people and keeps them warm. Dickens understood — as Dr Johnson understood — that there is a great deal to be said for a house where the provisions are in plain sight. The Boffins are good people before they are anything else — and the room, with all its glorious incongruity, reflects that goodness.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And then there is the other end of this novel's emotional range — the darkness that stalks those same margins. The scene involving Bradley Headstone lurking outside Wrayburn and Lightwood's chambers at night is one of the most genuinely frightening passages in Dickens. The narrator describes his approach with a phrase that is almost grotesque in its physicality: "The haggard head floated up the dark staircase, and softly descended nearer to the floor outside the outer door of the chambers." A haggard head that floats — Dickens has separated Bradley from his own body, turned him into something barely human. This is what obsession produces in this novel — not romantic longing but a kind of derangement that erases the self.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Yes — and one must linger here, because this passage is Dickens at his most formally brilliant. Twemlow is introduced not as a man but as a piece of dinner-furniture — kept, when not in use, over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street. That phrase — 'when not in use' — is doing enormous moral work. It tells us that this man's existence is entirely defined by his utility to others. And then Dickens gives us the sentence that locks the whole thing in place. 'Being first cousin to Lord Snigsworth, he was in frequent requisition, and at many houses might be said to represent the dining-table in its normal state.' He represents the table in its normal state — that is his identity, his social function, his entire claim to existence in this world. And the further the table is extended — the grander the dinner — the further poor Twemlow finds himself from the centre, nearer to the sideboard or the window-curtains. The grander the occasion, the more marginal the man.

Caroline is exactly right, and I want to push the point a little further. The Veneerings don't use Twemlow cruelly — they use him thoughtlessly, which is worse. He is a resource — an instrument of social credit — and they deploy him with the same casual efficiency they would bring to arranging the silverware. That is precisely the logic of a society organised around exchange value rather than human worth. And Dickens knows it — he encodes it in the very grammar of the metaphor. The man is not like a table — he simply is one, grammatically, for several extended paragraphs. Dickens doesn't reach for analogy — he performs the dehumanisation in the prose itself.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I would grant all of that, Daniel, and still want to insist on the moral dimension. Because Twemlow — and this is why he endures as a character — is not destroyed by the system. He retains a kind of innocent dignity precisely because he has never been corrupted by ambition or greed. Compare him to Silas Wegg — a man who also occupies a marginal position, but who responds to that position with a consuming hunger for what others possess. Dickens gives Wegg and his confederate Venus the most extravagant scheme — a plan to extort Boffin of half his fortune. And the language Wegg uses is almost parliamentary in its pomposity — listen to this: 'He considered it plain that such price was stateable in a single expressive word, and that the word was, Halves!' And if Boffin resists, Wegg proposes that he should be 'kept in a state of abject moral bondage and slavery until the time when they should see fit to permit him to purchase his freedom at the price of half his possessions.' The comedy is magnificent — but the moral ugliness beneath it is real.

The opening of this novel is one of the most audacious pieces of scene-setting in all of Victorian fiction. Dickens doesn't begin with a drawing room, or a courtroom, or a deathbed — he begins on a river at night, with a man fishing corpses. But what strikes me — what has always struck me — is the peculiar ritual Gaffer performs with the money he takes from the body. Dickens gives us three verbs in sequence, and they have the rhythm of an incantation. 'He chinked it once, and he blew upon it once, and he spat upon it once,—for luck, he hoarsely said—before he put it in his pocket.' Chink, blow, spit — these are the gestures of a man who believes in something older than the law, older than the market. And yet the money itself is, of course, entirely modern — taken from a dead man's pocket, washed clean in the same river that delivered him. That is Dickens's extraordinary compression: superstition and commerce, the archaic and the Victorian, inseparable in a single gesture.

What I keep returning to, at the end, is Jenny Wren. Because here is a child in genuine, relentless pain — and Dickens gives her this vision. "'Who is this in pain! Who is this in pain!' When I told them who it was, they answered, 'Come and play with us!'" The children come down in long bright slanting rows. They ask who is in pain. And the answer to pain is an invitation to play. I don't think that's sentimental. I think it's one of the most serious things in the novel. The imagination — the capacity to dream something different from the life you've been given — is Jenny's form of survival. And Dickens honours it completely.

All in white dresses, and with something shining on the borders, and on their heads, that I have never been able to imitate with my work, though I know it so well. They used to come down in long bright slanting rows, and say all together, “Who is this in pain! Then it was all delicious ease and rest till they laid me down, and said, all together, “Have patience, and we will come again.” Whenever they came back, I used to know they were coming before I saw the long bright rows, by hearing them ask, all together a long way off, “Who is this in pain! I won't argue with that reading of Jenny. Dickens does honour her. What moves me is that she has to construct an entire private cosmos of shining children because the real world offers her nothing.
