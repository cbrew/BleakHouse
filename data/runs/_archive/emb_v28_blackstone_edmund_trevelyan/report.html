Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What one hears, above everything else, is the audacity of the thing. Dickens opens not with a character, not with an event, but with a single word: London. A full stop. Then nothing but weather and moral implication for a page and a half. And then he introduces his Megalosaurus — that extraordinary image. "It would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." He is telling us, from the very first page, that London has reverted to something primordial. That civilisation — law, commerce, the whole institutional edifice — is a thin crust over something ancient and chaotic. That, to my mind, is not a political statement. It is a metaphysical one.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And what I'd add — speaking as someone who has stood in a recording booth with those sentences — is that the grammar is doing something extraordinary. There are no main verbs for the first half-dozen sentences. Just nouns. Fragments. "London. Michaelmas term lately over. Implacable November weather." It reads like a telegram from the edge of the world. There's no subject in those opening sentences because the fog is the subject — the fog is the governing force. When I perform it aloud, I let those fragments land separately, each one like a footstep into deeper mud. That's not literary analysis — that's just what the prose demands of the human voice.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to hold onto Edmund's word — primordial — because it captures something important about Dickens' strategy. This fog is not decorative. It is doing specific institutional work. Look at where Dickens places us next: inside the Court of Chancery itself. And what a portrait it is. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard." That anaphora — 'which has its... which has its' — is the rhetorical structure of an indictment. The real Chancery by 1852 had cases pending that had been active since the reign of George the Third. Dickens was not exaggerating. He was, if anything, exercising restraint.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James is quite right about the legal specifics, and I shan't contest them. But I would gently resist the idea that the novel's power is exhausted by its accuracy as social critique. The fog serves a deeper purpose than exposing Chancery, important as that exposure is. It is the atmosphere in which character is formed and tested. The fog obscures — and what it obscures most dangerously is one's own moral bearing. That, I submit, is why Jarndyce and Jarndyce can destroy Richard Carstone as surely as it destroys any estate. The institution corrupts from within, not merely by consuming money, but by corroding the will.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me — and Edmund, I think you're circling this — is that Dickens actually makes the fog funny as well as dreadful. There's that wonderful passage near Temple Bar — which, incidentally, was a genuine civic eyesore, a stone gate blocking Fleet Street — where Dickens goes superlative mad. Listen to the architecture of this sentence. "The raw afternoon is rawest, and the dense fog is densest, and the muddy streets are muddiest near that leaden-headed old obstruction, appropriate ornament for the threshold of a leaden-headed old corporation, Temple Bar." Rawest. Densest. Muddiest. He stacks the superlatives like a comedian stacks a punchline. And then 'leaden-headed old obstruction' — repeated — 'leaden-headed old corporation.' The joke is in the echo. The institution is literally the same thing as the lump of masonry blocking the road. Dickens the moralist and Dickens the music-hall comedian are the same man. Always.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Oliver's point about Temple Bar is worth dwelling on, because the geography here is precise. Lincoln's Inn Hall was the actual seat of the Chancery court. Dickens is not inventing an institution — he is describing one that every reader in 1852 would have known personally, or feared. And that passage I read earlier ends with a warning I find almost unbearably poignant. "Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here." That is Dickens quoting the practitioners themselves — the honourable men inside the system, advising suitors to stay away. When the people who run an institution tell you to avoid it, you are dealing with something that has ceased, in any meaningful sense, to function. That is not satire. That is reporting.

James's word — reporting — opens something I want to pursue. Because Dickens doesn't just set his scene in the fog and leave us there. Very early in the novel, he poses a question — a rhetorical, almost cosmic question — about connection across the social divide. He asks: what connexion can there be between the grand house in Lincolnshire, the fashionable town house — and Jo, the crossing-sweeper, the outlaw with a broom? Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me first, every single time I read that passage, is the hesitation Dickens builds into the sentence itself. Richard says he wants to study the law — and then he stalls, mid-sentence, before naming what he really means. Listen to how Dickens writes it — and those dashes are doing everything. "I should have my eye on the — hum! — the forbidden ground — and should be able to study it, and master it." That "hum!" — that tiny, embarrassed noise — is one of the most damning stage directions Dickens ever inserted into reported speech. Richard knows, even as he speaks, that he's rationalising. The suit is the forbidden ground — forbidden because everyone who loves him has warned him away from it — and he's walking straight towards it, eyes wide open.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver is right to land on that word — and I'd add that "master" is equally telling. Richard does not say he wishes to understand the suit, or to seek justice through it. He wishes to master it — as a man masters a horse he intends to ride to his own purposes. This is the characteristic Dickensian irony: the very confidence that makes Richard appealing is what makes him vulnerable. He believes his will is stronger than the institution. It is a form of pride — not the monstrous kind, but the ordinary, entirely human kind that Burke would recognise as the besetting sin of the young. And one notices, too, the cheerful, almost comical energy at the end — "peg away at Blackstone and all those fellows with the most tremendous ardour." "Peg away" and "tremendous ardour" in the same breath — it is the language of a schoolboy planning to conquer mathematics. Dickens gives us, with perfect economy, a young man who is entirely charming and entirely doomed.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to pause on what Richard is actually proposing here, because the institutional reality is rather grimmer than the rhetoric suggests. The Court of Chancery in the 1840s was not a system that rewarded scrutiny from suitors. It was a system that rewarded patience — or rather, the financial endurance to sustain patience across decades. Richard's fantasy of "studying" his own suit from inside a solicitor's office reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of how the machine operated. Jarndyce v. Jarndyce was not a case with moving parts a young clerk could monitor — it was a geological formation. It had been accumulating for so long that the original point of dispute had been buried under generations of procedural sediment. Richard thinks proximity is power. Jarndyce already knows, with the knowledge of bitter experience, that proximity is poison.
