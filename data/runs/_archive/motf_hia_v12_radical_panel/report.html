Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Caroline has put her finger on something essential, and I think it runs deeper than comedy. What Eliot is doing in these early chapters is establishing the moral texture of two very different minds. Tom is not unintelligent — he is simply shaped by a particular kind of formation that has no place for what Maggie offers. And this matters enormously for what follows — because the tragedy of the novel is not that Tom is cruel, exactly, but that he is limited in a way he cannot perceive. The father, Mr. Tulliver, has a different kind of limitation — and Eliot renders it with devastating brevity. He says to Maggie — simply, with no self-pity: "Ay, my little wench, but I shall never live twice o'er." Seven words — or thereabouts — and an entire life's defeat is contained in them. That is what the great novelists can do that no philosopher or historian quite manages.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund is right about that sentence — it is devastating. But I want to press on what it means that Tulliver will never live twice o'er — because that foreclosure is not simply the tragedy of one man's temperament. Tulliver is destroyed by debt and the law — by mechanisms that exist to protect property, not people. And the inheritance of that destruction falls on his children — on Tom, who must sacrifice his boyhood to restore the family's honour, and on Maggie, who must sacrifice everything else. Eliot traces that trajectory through to its end — and the end is the flood. Listen to how she renders Maggie's awakening on the water: "She was driven out upon the flood,—that awful visitation of God which her father used to talk of; which had made the nightmare of her childish dreams." "And with that thought there rushed in the vision of the old home, and Tom, and her mother,—they had all listened together." The flood is not a metaphor tacked onto the ending — it has been present in the family's imagination from the beginning. Eliot has made catastrophe feel inevitable without making it feel mechanical — and that is the novel's great structural achievement.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That is precisely where Eliot's moral imagination is at its most acute. Maggie does not simply feel sorry for herself — she understands the specific harm she has done to specific people. And that clarity of conscience — that refusal to look away — is what makes her a tragic figure in the proper sense. Dr. Johnson said that the business of a poet is to examine the general nature of things — but Eliot does something rarer. She makes the general feel absolutely particular — makes you feel you know exactly what it is to be this person, in this moment, on this water.

Daniel, let's start with the structural question — why does Mr Tulliver go to law, and who or what is actually pushing him there?
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Eliot does in these chapters is extraordinarily precise — she names the forces converging on one man and shows us exactly how they lock together. On one side, there's Mrs Tulliver's domestic pleading — which the narrator treats with magnificent irony. The passage is quite explicit that her influence works not through her own personality but through what she represents to him. The novel puts it like this — and it's worth reading carefully: "it might even be comparable to that proverbial feather which has the credit or discredit of breaking the camel's back; though, on a strictly impartial view, the blame ought rather to lie with the previous weight of feathers which had already placed the back in such imminent peril." That is not a comic metaphor — it is a structural argument. The back was already overloaded before Mrs Tulliver opened her mouth. And that prior weight — debt, competition, the encroachment on his water rights — that is the material history of a small proprietor being squeezed out.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel is right that the novel is analytically precise — but I think he underweights the moral dimension of Mr Tulliver's own choices. Eliot is at considerable pains to show us that Mr Tulliver is not simply a victim — he is a man of enormous pride, and that pride is operative. When she tells us that he saw in his wife the representative of the Dodson family, and that his guiding principle was to show that a male Tulliver was far more than equal to four female Dodsons — one hears the authentic note of masculine vanity taking on the character of principle. And then there is the matter of his theory of justice. Eliot gives us his philosophy with crystalline economy: "Law was a sort of cock-fight, in which it was the business of injured honesty to get a game bird with the best pluck and the strongest spurs." There is something almost Shakespearean in that sentence — the honest man who will hire a rascal to defend his honesty. The self-contradiction at the heart of his character is laid bare, and it is entirely his own doing — not society's.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me — reading it again this week — is the sheer affection Eliot maintains for this foolish, infuriating man even while she dismantles him. The portrait of his hatred for Wakem is almost slapstick — but there's real menace underneath. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Caroline's point about free indirect discourse is crucial — but I want to follow the money a step further. It's not incidental that in the same paragraph we learn Tulliver has recently been obliged to carry his own business to Wakem's office — because he's borrowing five hundred pounds. The man he most despises is already his creditor in practice, even before the ruin. And then there is the education chapter — which is Eliot at her most polemical, and I think her most honest. She describes a system in which clergymen ran schools as income supplements — men of narrow intellect and large wants, as she puts it — and fathers had no way to distinguish competence from its counterfeit. She says it plainly: "any of those low callings in which men are obliged to do good work at a low price were forbidden to clergymen; was it their fault if their only resource was to turn out very poor work at a high price?" The irony is savage — but it is also diagnostic. Tulliver's ruin is not just personal misfortune — it is the predictable output of institutions that extract value while performing service.

All this, you remember, happened in those dark ages when there were no schools of design; before schoolmasters were invariably men of scrupulous integrity, and before the clergy were all men of enlarged minds and varied culture. In those less favoured days, it is no fable that there were other clergymen besides Mr Stelling who had narrow intellects and large wants, and whose income, by a logical confusion to which Fortune, being a female as well as blindfold, is peculiarly liable, was proportioned not to their wants but to their intellect, with which income has clearly no inherent relation. The problem these gentlemen had to solve was to readjust the proportion between their wants and their income; and since wants are not easily starved to death, the simpler method appeared to be to raise their income. There was but one way of doing this; any of those low callings in which men are obliged to do good work at a low price were forbidden to clergymen; was it their fault if their only resource was to turn out very poor work at a high price? Besides, how should Mr Stelling be expected to know that education was a delicate and difficult business, any more than an animal endowed with a power of boring a hole through a rock should be expected to have wide views of excavation? Mr Stelling’s faculties had been early trained to boring in a straight line, and he had no faculty to spare. But among Tom’s contemporaries, whose fathers cast their sons on clerical instruction to find them ignorant after many days, there were many far less lucky than Tom Tulliver. Education was almost entirely a matter of luck—usually of ill-luck—in those distant days. The state of mind in which you take a billiard-cue or a dice-box in your hand is one of sober certainty compared with that of old-fashioned fathers, like Mr Tulliver, when they selected a school or a tutor for their sons. Excellent men, who had been forced all their lives to spell on an impromptu-phonetic system, and having carried on a successful business in spite of this disadvantage, had acquired money enough to give their sons a better start in life than they had had themselves, must necessarily take their chance as to the conscience and the competence of the schoolmaster whose circular fell in their way, and appeared to promise so much more than they would ever have thought of asking for, including the return of linen, fork, and spoon. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me first about the Maggie passages in childhood is how Eliot holds two registers at once — comic and devastating. There's a moment when Maggie's feelings about little Lucy turn, and Eliot describes it with this perfectly calibrated irony. "Maggie would have thought a little while ago that she could never be cross with pretty little Lucy, any more than she could be cruel to a little white mouse." That comparison — a little white mouse — is so precisely chosen. It's funny, but only just — because what follows is Maggie actively wanting to slap Lucy, to make her cry, to vex Tom. Eliot is tracing the logic of childhood emotion with total fidelity — and that fidelity is both funny and slightly horrifying.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Caroline is right that Eliot holds two registers — but I'd say the comedy is in the service of something morally precise. What Eliot is showing us is the moment at which natural affection can be corrupted — not by wickedness, but by wounded pride. The humour licenses us to observe this without flinching — because the child is funny, we can see clearly what she's becoming. And it is Tom's preference for Lucy — Tom's casual, careless power over Maggie — that produces the corruption. The comedy, in other words, is the vehicle for a moral anatomy.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to push on something Edmund said — because Tom's power over Maggie isn't incidental, it's structural. Eliot tells us that it was Maggie who had always been left to pet and make much of Lucy — that was her assigned role. "Tom had always been quite indifferent to Lucy before, and it had been left to Maggie to pet and make much of her." That word "left" is doing enormous work. Maggie has been assigned to the emotional labour — the petting, the making much of — while Tom simply takes or ignores as he pleases. The comedy is real, but it's comedy that exposes a gender arrangement Eliot never endorses.

Daniel's reading illuminates something — but I keep returning to the pure verbal comedy of the Tom scene a few chapters on. Tom dressing himself up as a fierce warrior — with burnt cork eyebrows and a red handkerchief turban — is one of the funniest passages in the novel. And what makes it funny is Eliot's account of the effort involved. "Philip had once told him of a man who had a horseshoe frown, and Tom had tried with all his frowning might to make a horseshoe on his forehead." "All his frowning might" — that phrase is a small comic masterpiece. Eliot understands that children are entirely serious about their play — and that seriousness is what makes it so delightful.

Nothing but long meditation and preconcerted arrangement of effects could have enabled Tom to present so striking a figure as he did to Maggie when she looked up. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The Tom scene is marvellous, and it illustrates why Eliot's comic method is fundamentally generous. She mocks Tom's self-deception — the amiable blue-gray eyes and the round pink cheeks that refused to look formidable — but she does not diminish him. The comedy is in the gap between Tom's aspiration and his nature — and that gap is universally human. I rather think Dr. Johnson would have approved: laughter at human vanity, without cruelty.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And then immediately the comedy reasserts itself — through Aunt Glegg, of all people. Mrs Tulliver reports Glegg's offer of shelter to Maggie, and the voice Eliot gives her is magnificent in its comedy. "I won't throw ill words at her; there's them out o' th' family 'ull be ready enough to do that." That is Glegg's idea of kindness — and Eliot renders it without condescension. The comedy here is doing something genuinely radical: it makes us see that even the most constrained, provincial character has her own form of loyalty. Glegg's comedy is the comedy of constraint — but constraint, Eliot insists, does not abolish the capacity for decency.

And then Philip's letter arrives — and the ironic comedy disappears entirely into something almost unbearably tender. Philip writes to Maggie and gives her what almost no one else in the novel gives her: belief. And then he describes what she has meant to him, and the language rises into something quite extraordinary. "You have been to my affections what light, what colour is to my eyes, what music is to the inward ear, you have raised a dim unrest into a vivid consciousness." "A dim unrest into a vivid consciousness" — that is the most beautiful description of falling in love I know in Victorian fiction. And it matters that it's Philip who says it — the character the novel has treated with the most irony, and the most compassion.

“Maggie,—I believe in you; I know you never meant to deceive me; I know you tried to keep faith to me and to all. I believed this before I had any other evidence of it than your own nature. The night after I last parted from you I suffered torments. I had seen what convinced me that you were not free; that there was another whose presence had a power over you which mine never possessed; but through all the suggestions—almost murderous suggestions—of rage and jealousy, my mind made its way to believe in your truthfulness. I was sure that you meant to cleave to me, as you had said; that you had rejected him; that you struggled to renounce him, for Lucy’s sake and for mine. But I could see no issue that was not fatal for you; and that dread shut out the very thought of resignation. I foresaw that he would not relinquish you, and I believed then, as I believe now, that the strong attraction which drew you together proceeded only from one side of your characters, and belonged to that partial, divided action of our nature which makes half the tragedy of the human lot. I have felt the vibration of chords in your nature that I have continually felt the want of in his. But perhaps I am wrong; perhaps I feel about you as the artist does about the scene over which his soul has brooded with love; he would tremble to see it confided to other hands; he would never believe that it could bear for another all the meaning and the beauty it bears for him.     “I dared not trust myself to see you that morning; I was filled with selfish passion; I was shattered by a night of conscious delirium. I told you long ago that I had never been resigned even to the mediocrity of my powers; how could I be resigned to the loss of the one thing which had ever come to me on earth with the promise of such deep joy as would give a new and blessed meaning to the foregoing pain,—the promise of another self that would lift my aching affection into the divine rapture of an ever-springing, ever-satisfied want?     “But the miseries of that night had prepared me for what came before the next. It was no surprise to me. I was certain that he had prevailed on you to sacrifice everything to him, and I waited with equal certainty to hear of your marriage. I measured your love and his by my own. But I was wrong, Maggie. There is something stronger in you than your love for him.     “I will not tell you what I went through in that interval. But even in its utmost agony—even in those terrible throes that love must suffer before it can be disembodied of selfish desire—my love for you sufficed to withhold me from suicide, without the aid of any other motive. In the midst of my egoism, I yet could not bear to come like a death-shadow across the feast of your joy. I could not bear to forsake the world in which you still lived and might need me; it was part of the faith I had vowed to you,—to wait and endure. Maggie, that is a proof of what I write now to assure you of,—that no anguish I have had to bear on your account has been too heavy a price to pay for the new life into which I have entered in loving you. I want you to put aside all grief because of the grief you have caused me. I was nurtured in the sense of privation; I never expected happiness; and in knowing you, in loving you, I have had, and still have, what reconciles me to life. You have been to my affections what light, what colour is to my eyes, what music is to the inward ear, you have raised a dim unrest into a vivid consciousness. The new life I have found in caring for your joy and sorrow more than for what is directly my own, has transformed the spirit of rebellious murmuring into that willing endurance which is the birth of strong sympathy. I think nothing but such complete and intense love could have initiated me into that enlarged life which grows and grows by appropriating the life of others; for before, I was always dragged back from it by ever-present painful self-consciousness. I even think sometimes that this gift of transferred life which has come to me in loving you, may be a new power to me.     “Then, dear one, in spite of all, you have been the blessing of my life. Let no self-reproach weigh on you because of me. It is I who should rather reproach myself for having urged my feelings upon you, and hurried you into words that you have felt as fetters. You meant to be true to those words; you have been true. I can measure your sacrifice by what I have known in only one half-hour of your presence with me, when I dreamed that you might love me best. But, Maggie, I have no just claim on you for more than affectionate remembrance.     “For some time I have shrunk from writing to you, because I have shrunk even from the appearance of wishing to thrust myself before you, and so repeating my original error. Philip's letter is the still centre of the whole novel's moral argument, I think. He has suffered the most — and yet his letter is an act of complete self-transcendence. I won't quarrel with Edmund about Philip's letter — it is genuinely great writing. But I notice that Philip's transcendence, his resignation, his generous release of Maggie — it costs him nothing structurally. What strikes me, returning to this novel again and again, is how carefully George Eliot frames the entire thing as an act of memory. The novel does not begin in the past — it begins with a dreamer in a chair, waking to find her arms have gone numb.
