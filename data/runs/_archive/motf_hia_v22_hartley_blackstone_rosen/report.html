Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately about these early chapters is how Eliot refuses to make Maggie's interiority simple or sentimental. There is a passage — about Maggie pushing Lucy into the mud — that I think is one of the most technically audacious things in the novel. Eliot writes: 'There were passions at war in Maggie at that moment to have made a tragedy, if tragedies were made by passion only; but the essential τι μέγεθος which was present in the passion was wanting to the action.' She drops a phrase of Aristotelian Greek into the middle of a scene about a small girl and a muddy puddle. That is an almost reckless structural gamble — and it pays off completely. Because the joke is also entirely serious: Maggie has the interior life of a tragic heroine trapped in the body and circumstances of a provincial nine-year-old. The form enacts what the story is about.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right that the gap between inner scale and outer circumstance is the nerve of the whole novel — but I want to press on what that gap actually meant in social terms. The narrator gives us a wonderfully precise account of Maggie's education — or rather, the absence of one. The text tells us she had 'only been to school a year at St Ogg's, and had so few books that she sometimes read the dictionary.' And the consequence, Eliot notes, is that 'in travelling over her small mind you would have found the most unexpected ignorance as well as unexpected knowledge.' Now, that is not a quirk of Maggie's personality — it is the entirely predictable outcome of what mid-Victorian England offered to clever girls from middling families. The education system — or the lack of one — sorted people by class and by sex, and Maggie falls on the wrong side of both divisions. She teaches herself etymology from a dictionary because nothing else is provided.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And what does that self-taught intelligence produce, when the world gives it no proper channel? It produces fantasy — the fantasy of escape. Eliot sets it up with exact irony: 'Maggie's intentions, as usual, were on a larger scale than Tom imagined.' And then comes the plan — she will run away to the gypsies, who will recognise her intellectual superiority and pay her due respect. This is not mere childish whimsy — it is a precise index of what society has denied her. The gypsies represent an imagined counter-society where her gifts would be valued — because the actual society around her refuses to value them. The fantasy is the critique.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And then Eliot does something magnificent — she lets the fantasy collapse into pure gothic horror. The narrator tells us that Maggie's ideas about the gypsies have 'undergone a rapid modification in the last five minutes.' From dignified companions, they have become something considerably worse: 'she had begun to think that they meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking.' 'Gradual cooking' — it is a phrase of such specific, deadpan horror that it still makes me laugh and wince simultaneously. Eliot is tracking, with forensic precision, the speed at which a child's imagination moves from utopia to catastrophe. And both the utopia and the catastrophe tell us exactly the same thing about Maggie — that she is incapable of thinking small.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to return to Eleanor's point about the Lucy-in-the-mud passage, because I think there's a class dimension there that is easy to miss. Eliot describes Lucy as 'poor little pink-and-white Lucy' — and that 'pink-and-white' is doing a great deal of social work. The full line runs: 'the utmost Maggie could do, with a fierce thrust of her small brown arm, was to push poor little pink-and-white Lucy into the cow-trodden mud.' Brown arm against pink-and-white skin — that is the contrast Eliot wants us to feel. Maggie is wild, dark, unruly; Lucy is the image of femininity that the culture rewards and protects. And when Maggie's passion finally finds an outlet, it is against the embodiment of everything she is told she should be. The mud is social, not merely physical.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Eliot does in the schoolroom chapters is structurally quite brilliant — she gives us Tom's suffering first, in full, before she lets Maggie anywhere near the Latin Grammar. Tom's humiliation is real and genuinely painful to read. He has this great unquestioned pride, and Stelling's schoolroom simply grinds it down, lesson by lesson. And Eliot captures his desperation in the most quietly devastating way — through prayer. Tom, having broken down for the fifth time on the supines of the third conjugation, that evening adds to his prayers: "and please to make me always remember my Latin." That whispered petition is heartbreaking — and it's also very funny, in the way only true tenderness can be funny. Eliot earns Maggie's triumphant relationship with learning precisely because she has first made Tom's failure so vivid and so human.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's worth pausing on just how specifically Victorian that scene is, in institutional terms. Mr Stelling is running what was effectively a private tutoring establishment — a clergyman supplementing his income, with no formal qualification to teach and no accountability whatsoever. The curriculum — Latin, Euclid — was designed for the professions and the universities, utterly disconnected from the commercial life Tom's father actually needs him to enter. And Eliot is scathing about this, though as ever she smuggles her criticism inside tenderness. Stelling is convinced Tom's failures must be carelessness — because the alternative, that the system is simply wrong for this particular boy, is unthinkable to him. What I find quietly devastating is that both children are trapped by the same set of assumptions — just in opposite directions. Tom is forced through an education that doesn't fit him, and Maggie is denied the education that would fit her perfectly.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that bond gets tested in the most physical and dramatic way in the scene with the sword. Tom counts aloud — one, two, three — swings the sword and it falls on his foot, and he faints. Maggie's response is extraordinary in its contradiction — she believes him dead, and shakes him by the collar as if to force him back to life. And then — when Tom opens his eyes — Eliot writes this: "She couldn't sorrow yet that he had hurt his foot; it seemed as if all happiness lay in his being alive." That sentence is doing something very particular about the hierarchy of griefs. Injury is a small catastrophe; death would have been absolute. And it tells us something essential about Maggie's love — it is unconditional in the most literal sense: it requires only that he exist.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That quality of love — requiring only that the other exist — is what makes Philip Wakem's question to Maggie so quietly shattering. Philip is the clever, deformed, sensitive boy who reads Philoctetes — the lame hero — and then turns to Maggie and asks, almost in passing: "Maggie, if you had had a brother like me, do you think you should have loved him as well as Tom?" That question carries every anxiety Philip has about his own body, his own place in the world, all compressed into a hypothetical about fraternal love. And Maggie, being Maggie, doesn't flinch or offer easy comfort. She works through it honestly — and then offers to kiss him as she kisses Tom, saying: "Should you like me to kiss you, as I do Tom? I will, if you like." That offer — guileless, practical, entirely without condescension — is Maggie's intellect and her love operating as the same faculty. She has worked out, through reasoning, how to demonstrate what she feels. For Eliot, that is not naivety — that is the most radical form of generosity.

The religion of the Dodsons consisted in revering whatever was customary and respectable; it was necessary to be baptised, else one could not be buried in the church-yard, and to take the sacrament before death, as a security against more dimly understood perils; but it was of equal necessity to have the proper pall-bearers and well-cured hams at one’s funeral, and to leave an unimpeachable will. A wholesome pride in many respects, since it identified honour with perfect integrity, thoroughness of work, and faithfulness to admitted rules; and society owes some worthy qualities in many of her members to mothers of the Dodson class, who made their butter and their fromenty well, and would have felt disgraced to make it otherwise. To be honest and poor was never a Dodson motto, still less to seem rich though being poor; rather, the family badge was to be honest and rich, and not only rich, but richer than was supposed. To live respected, and have the proper bearers at your funeral, was an achievement of the ends of existence that would be entirely nullified if, on the reading of your will, you sank in the opinion of your fellow-men, either by turning out to be poorer than they expected, or by leaving your money in a capricious manner, without strict regard to degrees of kin.
