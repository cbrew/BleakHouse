Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
You know, in forty-odd years of reading novels aloud, I have never encountered an opening quite like this one. Most novels open with a person, or a place, or a problem. Dickens opens with a condition. Just listen to the way it begins — and I do mean listen, because this passage was written for the voice. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That parenthesis — "and dirty" — is almost insolent in its precision. He could have said filthy, or foul, or polluted — but "dirty" has the bluntness of a fact that everyone knows and no one says. And then he keeps going — the fog creeps into cabooses, it droops on gunwales, it cruelly pinches the toes of a shivering apprentice boy. Cruelly. He gives the fog agency — and malice.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Oliver is pointing at is something technically extraordinary — Dickens is using anaphora here, the repetition of "fog," as a structural device, not just a rhetorical one. Each new "fog" is a new clause, a new victim, a new place the system reaches. The fog is not a description — it's an argument. And notice the social sweep of it — from the great ships down to the apprentice boy on deck, from the marshes of Essex to the heights of Kent. Nobody escapes — though the poor boy shivering on deck suffers it rather more acutely than the wrathful skipper in his close cabin. Dickens makes that distinction quietly, almost in passing — but it is absolutely precise.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor is right that it's an argument — and I want to press on what that argument is. This is the opening of a novel that is, at its core, about a legal system — the Court of Chancery — that has been grinding people to nothing for generations. The fog is Dickens's metaphor for that system — pervasive, indiscriminate in one sense, yet landing hardest on those who cannot escape it. The pensioners, the apprentice boy — they are in the fog and they cannot go below decks. And look at this extraordinary image at the end of the passage — people on bridges peeping over the parapets into a nether sky of fog. "Chance people on the bridges peeping over the parapets into a nether sky of fog, with fog all round them, as if they were up in a balloon and hanging in the misty clouds." They are suspended in it — unable to see above or below, unable to orient themselves. That is precisely what Chancery does to the people caught inside it — for decades, sometimes for entire lifetimes.

And Dickens doesn't let the fog be merely metaphorical — he keeps insisting on its physical reality. "Fog cruelly pinching the toes and fingers" — that's not a symbol, that's a sensation. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is the contrast Dickens builds around it — because just before Jo testifies, we get Mrs. Piper. And Mrs. Piper is comic — brilliantly, mercilessly comic. Her testimony is a cascade of parentheses and digressions, gossip dressed up as evidence. But what she's actually doing is performing the community's hostility to the stranger — the man who kept himself apart. She insists on calling the dead man "the plaintive" — and she reports gravely that he "was reported to have sold himself." "the plaintive—so Mrs. Piper insists on calling the deceased—was reported to have sold himself." She doesn't know what it means, she can't say where she heard it, but it damns him — and that is precisely the point. The inquest is not seeking truth — it is processing a nuisance, and Mrs. Piper is its perfect instrument.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me most, when I look at how Dickens builds this, is that Chancery never really appears directly — it operates through what it does to people. And nowhere is that more devastating than in Richard Carstone. There's a moment in chapter twenty-three where Richard tries to explain himself to Esther — his inconstancy, his inability to settle — and he reaches for a metaphor that is one of the most brilliant pieces of craft in the whole novel. He says: "If you lived in an unfinished house, you couldn't settle down in it; if you were condemned to leave everything you undertook unfinished, you would find it hard to apply yourself to anything." And then he completes it — and this is the line that breaks your heart — "I was born into this unfinished contention with all its chances and changes, and it has gone on unsettling me ever since." Born into it — that's the key phrase. Chancery is not something Richard stumbled into — it preceded his consciousness, it preceded his capacity to resist.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And what that metaphor exposes, brilliantly, is that the system produces its own justification for the damage it causes. Richard internalises the logic of Chancery — he blames his own inconstancy, his own weakness — when what he is describing is structural dispossession. The suit has consumed the inheritance, but Richard frames it as a failure of character — his character. That is a precise description of how ideology functions — the victim narrates their own victimisation as personal inadequacy. And earlier in the same chapter, when Richard admits the financial damage — "I should have begun to be steady and systematic by this time, and shouldn't be in debt" — you hear the grinding sound of that mechanism. "I should have begun to be steady and systematic by this time, and shouldn't be in debt, and—" That trailing dash — the sentence that cannot finish itself — is Dickens enacting in syntax what Chancery does in life.
