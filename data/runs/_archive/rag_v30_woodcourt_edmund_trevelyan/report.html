Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The very first thing Dickens does in this novel — before character, before plot — is establish a world saturated in obstruction. The fog, the mud, the court of Chancery — they are not separate phenomena. They are one atmosphere. And it is into this atmosphere that Dickens introduces, quite brilliantly, the figure of Jarndyce himself — who describes the ruin that Chancery leaves in its wake. He gives us a street — a real London street — consumed by the suit. "It is a street of perishing blind houses, with their eyes stoned out, without a pane of glass, without so much as a window-frame, with the bare blank shutters tumbling from their hinges and falling asunder, the iron rails peeling away in flakes of rust, the chimneys sinking in, the stone steps to every door — and every door might be death's door — turning stagnant green." Every door might be death's door. That parenthetical lands like a quiet toll of a bell. This is not mere social commentary — this is the gothic imagination at full power.

What strikes me every time I read that passage aloud is how physical it is — you can hear the shutters coming off their hinges. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I take Oliver's point about the voice entirely — though I'd add that the cumulative syntax also has a precise moral weight. Jarndyce ends that passage with a line I find almost unbearable in its simplicity. "These are the Great Seal's impressions, my dear, all over England — the children know them!" That is where Dickens' moral imagination operates at its most devastating — not in the polemical passages, but in the small, quiet observations.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I love about both those passages is how different they are in texture — and yet they're both about the same invisible force. Jarndyce's street is all surface — visible decay, things falling apart that you can touch and smell. Tulkinghorn's mystery is precisely the opposite — nothing is visible at all. But Dickens also gives us something else entirely — the interior of Bleak House itself. And this is what I keep coming back to on every re-reading: the warmth of that house is not sentimental. It's earned — it's specific. Esther describes her first impressions with this extraordinary trailing sentence — let me read it. "with its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw; and just wind enough without to sound a low accompaniment to everything we heard, were our first impressions of Bleak House." That phrase — hospitable jingle — is almost ridiculous, and yet it's completely perfect. You hear the warmth before you see it.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
And yet one must not overlook what Dickens places in deliberate counterpoint to all of that warmth. There is another world in this novel — the world of Chesney Wold, of the Dedlock interest, of Sir Leicester himself. And Dickens describes that world with a comic precision that is, frankly, surgical. "He has a general opinion that the world might get on without hills but would be done up without Dedlocks." Done up. It is gloriously vulgar, in the very best sense — a slang phrase intruding into baronial self-importance. Dickens' comic gift is not separate from his moral imagination — it is the instrument of it.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Thank you. It is worth pausing on the world that surrounds Richard before we consider the man himself — because Dickens is quite deliberate in the contrast he establishes. There is, on one side, a portrait of England as rooted permanence — Chesney Wold, the ancient house, Sir Leicester's immovable self-satisfaction. Dickens gives us this passage, which I confess I find rather beautiful despite its satirical intent. "Where the ivy and the moss have had time to mature, and the gnarled and warted elms and the umbrageous oaks stand deep in the fern and leaves of a hundred years." The language is doing two things simultaneously, which is Dickens at his most sophisticated. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Caroline is absolutely right, and I think this is where I must respectfully part company with those who read Richard simply as a victim of Chancery. Chancery is the occasion of Richard's ruin, but not its cause. The cause is a weakness of will — a constitutional inability to commit to anything that does not promise a miraculous resolution. Burke would have recognised it immediately: the man who mistakes expectation for inheritance, and hope for plan. Dickens shows us the full irony in the Dedlock passages, where we see what genuine — if comically inflated — rootedness looks like by contrast. Sir Leicester is absurd, yes — Dickens does not let him entirely off — but he is at least coherent. Richard is incoherent because he has substituted the lawsuit for a self.
