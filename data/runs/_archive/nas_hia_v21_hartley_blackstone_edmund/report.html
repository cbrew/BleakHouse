Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, whenever I return to this novel, is how precisely Gaskell engineers Margaret's inner life before she ever sets foot in Milton. There is a chapter — the conversation with Bessy Higgins — where Margaret describes Helstone, the home she has lost. And Gaskell gives her this extraordinary passage of sensory longing — listen to what she does with it. "There are great trees standing all about it, with their branches stretching long and level, and making a deep shade of rest even at noonday." A shade of rest at noonday — that is not description, that is longing rendered as landscape. Gaskell is building what I can only call a grammar of loss — so that when Milton's smoke arrives, we feel the full weight of what has been replaced. And then — and this is the structural move that undoes me every time — she cuts straight to Bessy's nineteen years of suffering in the fluff and cotton-dust of the mills. Margaret is also nineteen, and Gaskell lets that single fact carry an entire moral argument without spelling it out.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is quite right about the structural elegance, and I should not wish to quarrel with that reading. But I think one must resist the temptation to make that juxtaposition of the two nineteen-year-olds into a theorem about inequality. What Gaskell actually writes is rather more austere and more moving than a social argument. She writes that Margaret "could not speak for a moment or two for the emotion she was trying to keep down." Trying to keep down — that suppression is the signature of Margaret's entire character. She is not a creature of programme; she is a creature of tremendous feeling held, with great effort, in check. That is what makes her genuinely tragic in the classical sense — not her social position, but her self-governance.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund will forgive me if I insist that the social position and the self-governance are not quite so separable as he suggests. Consider the scene in the garden at Harley Street — the proposal scene — before any of this. Margaret has refused Henry Lennox, and Gaskell tells us precisely what that refusal costs her emotionally, in a society that offered women so very few alternatives. Her father's appearance in that garden — calmly eating a pear, entirely oblivious — is described as something almost hallucinatory. "It was like the story of the eastern king, who dipped his head into a basin of water, at the magician's command, and ere he instantly took it out went through the experience of a lifetime." An entire life changed — a proposal made and refused — in the space between one social obligation and the next. And then comes this: Margaret felt "stunned, and unable to recover her self-possession enough to join in the trivial conversation that ensued between her father and Mr. Lennox." Trivial conversation — while she is in ruins. That is the specific texture of constraint imposed on women of Margaret's class in this period — and Gaskell renders it with the precision of someone who knew it from the inside.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That eastern king image — I am glad James raised it, because it is doing something technically quite remarkable. Gaskell reaches all the way out to Oriental legend to describe the compression of emotional time — and it works, because the unreality of the image matches the unreality of Margaret's experience. She has gone through a lifetime of feeling in fifteen minutes, and now she must make small talk with her father about a pear. That pear — peeled in what Gaskell calls "one long strip of silver-paper thinness" — is one of those details that makes you trust a novelist completely. Mr. Hale is so entirely himself — so unmoved, so placid — that his presence throws Margaret's inner devastation into absolute relief.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
And this is precisely where I believe the novel announces its great subject — not industry, not class, though those will follow. The great subject is pride — a particular English, Protestant, quietly devastating form of pride. We are told that Margaret's "beautiful lip curled in a slight disdain" — and Gaskell is not criticising her for it. She is drawing a portrait of a young woman whose pride is both her greatest strength and the thing most likely to destroy her happiness. That is a moral problem — a problem of character — and it will take the whole of Milton and everything Milton brings to begin to resolve it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And Milton announces itself through Mrs. Thornton — which is rather wonderful, because Mrs. Thornton is in many ways Milton's most articulate self-advocate. Her speech to Margaret and Mrs. Hale about the town's manufactures is a remarkable piece of civic rhetoric — delivered, of course, with barely concealed contempt for people who do not appreciate what they are looking at. She speaks of Milton as a town "which has risen to eminence in the country, from the character and progress of its peculiar business." Character — she uses that word for an industrial town, and she means it absolutely. Yes — and it's a scene that rewards very close attention, because what looks like a simple meeting in a sitting-room is actually a masterclass in point-of-view engineering. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me is that the asymmetry of consciousness Gaskell establishes here is the engine of the entire novel. Margaret feels 'no awkwardness' — she has 'too much the habits of society for that.' That is not coldness — it is the product of a particular formation, a particular upbringing in which the management of social surfaces is second nature. Thornton, by contrast, is thrown entirely off his axis — 'surprised and discomfited,' as Gaskell puts it — by encountering a femininity he has no framework to categorise. This is not primarily about class antagonism — it is about two differently shaped moral and social educations meeting, unprepared, in a sitting-room. Gaskell's narrative frame holds both simultaneously — and neither is satirised.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That's the key — legibility. And then Gaskell does something formally very interesting when she moves the frame away from the drawing rooms entirely — into the Higgins household. I would resist the suggestion that this is a programmatic exercise in representing classes — Higgins is not a spokesman, he is a man. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And then Gaskell does something structurally daring — she puts her most powerful narrative frame around private suffering. The scene where Margaret confides in Bessy is one of the great moments of the novel — because it explodes the apparent social symmetry entirely. Margaret says — and I want to read this at length because every clause counts: 'Have I not care? Do I not know anxiety, though I go about well-dressed, and have food enough?' 'Oh, Bessy, God is just, and our lots are well portioned out by Him, although none but He knows the bitterness of our souls.' The frame here is so carefully set: a dying mother, a brother who is a fugitive, a father who cannot know — all held inside a single outburst to one other person. Gaskell has constructed a pressure chamber — and it's Bessy, a cotton worker, who is the only safe vessel for the truth.

I would add only this — that the novel's most quietly remarkable reconciliation is not between North and South at all, but between two women. Mrs. Thornton is perhaps the most unbending character Gaskell created — and yet, at the moment of Margaret's departure, something shifts. Gaskell writes, with characteristic restraint — and I ask you to attend to the precise movement of the sentence. "There was some softening in her heart towards Margaret, now that she was going away out of her sight. She remembered her spirit, as shown at various times and places, even more than the patience with which she had endured long and wearing cares." Even more than the patience — she remembers the spirit. Mrs. Thornton, who has resented Margaret's very existence, finds herself paying tribute to the quality she recognises in herself — defiant endurance. There is no ideology in that moment. There is only the recognition of one strong nature by another.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that moral attention, I'd argue, is what gives the novel its continuing force. North and South is not a comfortable book. It does not pretend that the conditions it describes have simple remedies. Margaret's final position — inheritor of property that comes partly from the industrial world she once indicted — is left open, unresolved, deliberate. Gaskell trusts her readers to feel the discomfort. That, in the end, is the mark of a serious novelist.
