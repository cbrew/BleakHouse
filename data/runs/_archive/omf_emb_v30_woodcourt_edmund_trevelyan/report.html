Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one must be careful not to flatten the comedy into polemic — which is a temptation with Dickens, and rather spoils the fun. The old Dust Contractor is, first and foremost, a comic monster — a splendid creation of grotesque greed. But I grant you that Dickens doesn't leave it there. Later in the novel, the narrator addresses the governing classes directly — and the tone shifts from satire to something closer to warning. The passage runs, if I may: 'My lords and gentlemen and honourable boards, when you in the course of your dust-shovelling and cinder-raking have piled up a mountain of pretentious failure, you must off with your honourable coats for the removal of it, and fall to the work with the power of all the queen's horses and all the queen's men, or it will come rushing down and bury us alive.' Now that is not the voice of a social theorist — that is the voice of a moralist who fears for the whole edifice. The dust heap is a warning, not merely a symbol.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The novel opens its thirty-eighth chapter with a question so compressed it takes your breath away. Dickens gives us precisely three sentences — and in the middle one, he poses what is really the moral axis of the entire book. The question is this: Was the Golden Dustman passing through the furnace of proof and coming out dross? Furnace of proof — that is an alchemical image, of course, but also a moral one. Gold goes into the furnace to be tested; dross is what remains when the base metal is all that was ever there. Dickens is asking whether Boffin — and by implication Bella — will survive their own testing.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Precisely — and that is what separates Bella from the merely satirised. She has the moral intelligence to see her own fault clearly. What she lacks — at this stage — is what Johnson would have called the power of resolution acting upon conviction. She knows better; she has not yet chosen better. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
And Bella, having survived that test — the spectacle of Mr Boffin's apparent corruption — then asks for a harder one. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
It sounds like a conjuror warming up — short declarative sentences, each one a flick of the wrist. Dickens has invented a device — the mirror — so he can introduce his entire social world in a single sustained passage without pausing for breath. And the repetition — 'Reflects Veneering', 'Reflects Mrs Veneering', 'Reflects Podsnap' — that anaphora is pure performance. It's a roll-call, a parade, and a prosecution all at once. The description of Veneering himself is the centrepiece, and it has to be read slowly to land properly. "Reflects Veneering; forty, wavy-haired, dark, tending to corpulence, sly, mysterious, filmy — a kind of sufficiently well-looking veiled-prophet, not prophesying." 'Not prophesying.' Two words. The whole man in two words. He has all the trappings of a prophet — the veil, the mystery — and absolutely nothing to say.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And then he turns immediately to Mrs Podsnap, and the comic register shifts completely. The description of Podsnap himself is already wonderful — those 'two little light-coloured wiry wings' of hair looking 'as like his hairbrushes as his hair.' But then Mrs Podsnap — 'fine woman for Professor Owen, quantity of bone, neck and nostrils like a rocking-horse.' 'Professor Owen' is the reference to Richard Owen, the anatomist — the man who coined the word 'dinosaur.' Dickens is saying, politely, that this woman belongs in a natural history museum. What I love is that it never tips into cruelty — it remains entirely delighted with its own ingenuity.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take the point about delight, but I would distinguish between two kinds of comic portrait in this passage. Twemlow is given something approaching pathos — 'cheeks drawn in as if he had made a great effort to retire into himself some years ago, and had got so far and had never got any farther.' Twemlow is a victim. The moral weight differs enormously.

Left alone at his stall as the other ambled off, Mr Wegg subsided into his screen, produced a small pocket-handkerchief of a penitentially-scrubbing character, and took himself by the nose with a thoughtful aspect. Also, while he still grasped that feature, he directed several thoughtful looks down the street, after the retiring figure of Mr Boffin. But, profound gravity sat enthroned on Wegg’s countenance. For, while he considered within himself that this was an old fellow of rare simplicity, that this was an opportunity to be improved, and that here might be money to be got beyond present calculation, still he compromised himself by no admission that his new engagement was at all out of his way, or involved the least element of the ridiculous. Mr Wegg would even have picked a handsome quarrel with any one who should have challenged his deep acquaintance with those aforesaid eight volumes of Decline and Fall. His gravity was unusual, portentous, and immeasurable, not because he admitted any doubt of himself but because he perceived it necessary to forestall any doubt of himself in others. And herein he ranged with that very numerous class of impostors, who are quite as determined to keep up appearances to themselves, as to their neighbours.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
And Dickens presses even further when he enlists the taxidermic specimens to witness Wegg's moral deterioration. The passage in Mr Venus's shop where the preserved creatures observe Wegg's scheming is, I confess, one of the strangest things Dickens ever wrote. The alligator's smile carries the weight of geological time — the cynicism of the deep past. Human treachery is so old, so reliably recurring, that even a preserved reptile has seen it all before. The comedy is still there, but it has a very dark bottom to it.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That 'yard or two of smile' is the most brilliantly calibrated physical description in the chapter. You feel the length of the jaw. But I want to talk about the Lammles and the opera box, because that is Dickens at his most technically astonishing. The Lammles are engineering a romance between Fledgeby and Georgiana Podsnap — and they do it by speaking entirely on the young couple's behalf. The lovers never actually speak to each other. The Lammles conduct the entire courtship as a ventriloquist act. Dickens captures the result in one sentence of perfect construction. "Through this device the two young people conversed at great length and committed themselves to a variety of delicate sentiments, without having once opened their lips, save to say yes or no, and even that not to one another." 'Not to one another.' The cruelest, funniest three words in the scene. A courtship conducted entirely by proxy. It is the most efficient satire of performance in social life that I know.

Miss Woodcourt identifies the crucial point. The grotesque and the pathetic are not in competition in this novel — they are the same instrument played in two registers. Dickens learned from Hogarth — that you may make a man ridiculous and pitiable in the same stroke of the pen. The looking-glass passage makes us laugh at these people. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And then there's the moment where Dickens himself appears — steps out of the fiction entirely, looks us in the eye, and says: I knew. He's addressing — with barely concealed exasperation — readers who thought the Harmon mystery was bungled. And he writes this extraordinary sentence: 'I foresaw the likelihood that a class of readers and commentators would suppose that I was at great pains to conceal exactly what I was at great pains to suggest: namely, that Mr John Harmon was not slain, and that Mr John Rokesmith was he.' The symmetry of that — 'at great pains to conceal exactly what I was at great pains to suggest' — is itself a demonstration of the artistry he's defending. He's not just arguing for himself. He's arguing for patience with art — for the idea that an artist may be trusted to know what he is about. Reading that, I always feel a little chastened — and then deeply persuaded.

When I devised this story, I foresaw the likelihood that a class of readers and commentators would suppose that I was at great pains to conceal exactly what I was at great pains to suggest: namely, that Mr John Harmon was not slain, and that Mr John Rokesmith was he. Pleasing myself with the idea that the supposition might in part arise out of some ingenuity in the story, and thinking it worth while, in the interests of art, to hint to an audience that an artist (of whatever denomination) may perhaps be trusted to know what he is about in his vocation, if they will concede him a little patience, I was not alarmed by the anticipation.
