Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And it's worth pausing on how Dickens earns that rage structurally — because pure anger is cheap in a novel. The reason those opening paragraphs hit so hard is the dual narration that begins almost immediately. We have the anonymous, present-tense narrator — cold, godlike, merciless in his irony. And then Esther Summerson — warm, personal, past tense, unreliable in her modesty. These two voices are doing completely different kinds of work, and Dickens switches between them without apology. The third-person narrator can see the fog for what it is — a systemic evil — and say so with full satirical venom. Esther can only experience consequences — she lives inside the fog, unable to name it. Together they create a kind of stereoscopic vision of the same nightmare — and that is an astonishing technical achievement for 1852.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And historically, Esther's position is extremely precise. She is a woman, she is illegitimate, she has no legal standing of her own — she exists entirely at the mercy of institutions and patrons. Thank you — and it's worth being quite precise about this, because Dickens is precise, even when he's being savage. Richard's arc is one of the most formally controlled things in the novel — and I think it's underappreciated as a structural achievement.
