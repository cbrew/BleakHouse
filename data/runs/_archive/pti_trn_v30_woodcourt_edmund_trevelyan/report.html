Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to pause there, though I'd frame it slightly differently. What Forster is describing is not a cultural failing but a particular kind of human gift — the capacity for immediate, uncalculating trust. Fielding recognises it and, crucially, he values it. The novel's tragedy — and it is a tragedy — is not that the British fail to understand India, but that individual goodwill turns out to be insufficient against the weight of institution and habit. Fielding himself tells us what he believes education is for — and it is not a small ambition. "I believe in teaching people to be individuals, and to understand other individuals. What moves me is how immediately the prose trusts the reader. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to begin with a passage that many readers skim past, because it appears comic — almost whimsical. Forster introduces two missionaries — old Mr. Graysford and young Mr. Sorley — who live beyond the slaughterhouses and never come up to the club. They believe in an infinitely inclusive divine hospitality — mansions for all, even the monkeys. But as the logic descends to jackals, then wasps, then bacteria — it collapses. And Forster delivers the sentence that ought to stand as the novel's epigraph. "We must exclude someone from our gathering, or we shall be left with nothing." That is the architecture of the whole novel in a single sentence. Every attempt at connection in this book runs aground on precisely that reef.
