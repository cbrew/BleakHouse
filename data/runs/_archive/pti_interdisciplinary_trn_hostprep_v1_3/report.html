Elena Volkov
The three-part structure of A Passage to India is a failed sonata, and these three passages are the evidence: Forster builds the formal architecture that promises resolution across three hundred pages, then at the structural moment of recapitulation substitutes consolation for resolution, arriving not in the home key of liberal Enlightenment understanding but in the relative minor of aesthetic solace—what a Beethoven listener would call a deceptive cadence executed at novel length.
Key points:
  • The three passages represent three distinct and mutually contradictory theories of what language is for: language as the instrument of mutual understanding (Fielding, c11:p67), language as exorcism of illusion (c26:p29), and literature as refuge from facts (Aziz, c30:p12), and Forster refuses to resolve the contradiction.
  • The grammatical structure of Fielding's creed encodes colonial hierarchy before the plot reveals it: the dependency structure places Fielding as agent of 'teaching' and 'people' as object, while the parallel infinitives 'to be' and 'to understand' conceal a deep semantic asymmetry between ontological state and relational cognitive act.
  • The three-part structure Mosque-Caves-Temple is not a sonata but a failed sonata: Forster builds the form that promises resolution (exposition, development, recapitulation) and substitutes consolation for resolution at the structural moment where resolution was expected, arriving on the relative minor rather than the home key.
  • Aziz's retreat to literature as solace is not a Romantic or Arnoldian affirmation of culture but a survival strategy under institutional duress, structurally analogous to Soviet composers writing 'drawer music' when the public institutional space had been made uninhabitable.

The 'India a nation!' passage in c37:p27 is not politically ambiguous — it is syntactically attributable to Fielding, not the narrator, and that attribution changes everything. Fielding's private contempt for Indian nationalism is so shaped by the nineteenth-century European categories he claims to have transcended that he can only express his love for Aziz by performing the very ideology that makes their friendship impossible. 'You and I shall be friends' comes after Fielding has mentally ranked India with Guatemala. The friendship is real, the disenchantment is real, and they coexist in the same syntactic structure — that is what Forster understood about liberalism.
Key points:
  • The segment title 'Fielding's Quiet Disenchantment' is misleading because the narrator's voice and Fielding's voice occupy the same grammatical space in these passages — whose disenchantment is being expressed is a measurable syntactic puzzle, not a rhetorical one.
  • The resumptive pronoun structure in c37:p27 ('She, whose only peer was the Holy Roman Empire, she shall rank…') is a syntactic feature of spoken, interactive register, constituting the grammatical fingerprint of free indirect discourse — Forster has imported Fielding's speaking voice into narrative prose.
  • Forster uses lexical repetition in c36:p71 ('whatever had happened had happened,' 'God is love') to formally enact the unlocatable cycling quality of the Mau ceremony, while the political irony of c37:p27 is lexically denser because disgust is specific and transcendence is vague.
  • The engagement sentence in c8:p119 — 'He assented, and they became engaged to be married in consequence' — frames the engagement as a logical outcome rather than an emotional event; the presupposition trigger 'in consequence' does the affective work of resignation without stating it.

With so emotional a people it was apt to come at once or never, and he and Aziz, having heard only good of each other, could afford to dispense with preliminaries.

And that's why the intimacy between Aziz and Fielding in that early scene is so painfully beautiful and so compromised simultaneously.
