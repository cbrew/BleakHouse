Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What I love about the opening of Tulliver's story — and I mean this quite seriously — is that Eliot gives us a man in the grip of a magnificently comfortable delusion. He carries a two-thousand-pound mortgage on his freehold and has somehow arranged, internally, not to feel it. Eliot's diagnosis of this is quite surgical. "He had been always used to hear pleasant jokes about his advantages as a man who worked his own mill, and owned a pretty bit of land; and these jokes naturally kept up his sense that he was a man of considerable substance." The jokes do the mortgage's work for it, in reverse. They gave — and I adore this phrase — "a pleasant flavour to his glass on a market-day." There's an entire social world in that sentence — the market-day glass, the good opinion of neighbours, the comfortable fiction of substance.

They gave a pleasant flavour to his glass on a market-day, and if it had not been for the recurrence of half-yearly payments, Mr Tulliver would really have forgotten that there was a mortgage of two thousand pounds on his very desirable freehold. That was not altogether his own fault, since one of the thousand pounds was his sister’s fortune, which he had to pay on her marriage; and a man who has neighbours that will go to law with him is not likely to pay off his mortgages, especially if he enjoys the good opinion of acquaintances who want to borrow a hundred pounds on security too lofty to be represented by parchment. Our friend Mr Tulliver had a good-natured fibre in him, and did not like to give harsh refusals even to his sister, who had not only come in to the world in that superfluous way characteristic of sisters, creating a necessity for mortgages, but had quite thrown herself away in marriage, and had crowned her mistakes by having an eighth baby. On this point Mr Tulliver was conscious of being a little weak; but he apologised to himself by saying that poor Gritty had been a good-looking wench before she married Moss; he would sometimes say this even with a slight tremulousness in his voice. But this morning he was in a mood more becoming a man of business, and in the course of his ride along the Basset lanes, with their deep ruts,—lying so far away from a market-town that the labour of drawing produce and manure was enough to take away the best part of the profits on such poor land as that parish was made of,—he got up a due amount of irritation against Moss as a man without capital, who, if murrain and blight were abroad, was sure to have his share of them, and who, the more you tried to help him out of the mud, would sink the further in. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver puts his finger on something essential. The tragedy of Mr Tulliver is not that he is a foolish man, but that he is a man of limited self-knowledge in a situation that demands rather more. And yet — one does not despise him. Eliot sees to it that we feel his goodness even as we watch him blunder. There is that moment — and it strikes me as the passage that unlocks the whole novel's emotional design — when he rides away from his sister's farm and then, quite suddenly, stops. "He turned his horse, and rode slowly back, giving vent to the climax of feeling which had determined this movement by saying aloud, as he struck his horse, 'Poor little wench! she'll have nobody but Tom, belike, when I'm gone.'" "Poor little wench." It is said aloud — to himself, to no one — which is how we know it is the truest thing he feels.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund's right to land on that moment — and I think it matters enormously what kind of world Tulliver is worrying about leaving Maggie in. Because the world Eliot describes around that ride to Basset is one of systemic, grinding failure — not individual misfortune. Look at what she catalogues: poor soil, poor roads, a non-resident landlord, a non-resident vicar, and — the detail that kills me — "rather less than half a curate, also poor." The institution of the Church, the institution of landownership — both absent, both derelict in their duties. And into that vacuum steps Dickison's public house — the only social institution left standing. Now — Tulliver's fear for Maggie is real, and Edmund rightly calls it tender. But the structural fact beneath the tenderness is that a daughter in this world has no independent standing whatsoever. She will have nobody but Tom — because the law and the custom of the time ensure that she can have nobody but Tom.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I would not wholly condemn Tom — Eliot doesn't, and she is wiser than we are in these matters. He has, after all, genuinely worked — genuinely sacrificed — to restore the family honour after the ruin. The tragedy is that his virtues and his cruelties flow from precisely the same source. He is, in the Aristotelian sense, a man undone by the excess of a genuine quality. That is what makes this novel — at its best — genuinely tragic, rather than merely pathetic.
