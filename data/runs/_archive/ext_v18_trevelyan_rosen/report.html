Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me every time I come back to the opening of this novel is how much Dickens asks of the atmosphere before he asks anything of a character. He builds the world first — its textures, its smells, its light — and then he lets people walk into it. And when Esther finally arrives at Bleak House itself, the writing becomes this extraordinary act of noticing. She counts the pictures, the stiff courtly chairs, the oval engravings of the months — ladies haymaking in short waists and large hats — the whole eccentric, quaint, beloved disorder of it. And then Dickens gathers all of it into a single sentence of almost overwhelming warmth. "With its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw; and just wind enough without to sound a low accompaniment to everything we heard, were our first impressions of Bleak House." That phrase — "a low accompaniment to everything we heard" — that is a writer composing in sound. It is almost musical notation dressed as prose.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver is absolutely right that it is overwhelming — and I think that is deliberate in a way that goes beyond aesthetics. Dickens is showing us what a house can be — warmth, neatness, the smell of lavender in every drawer — precisely because he is about to show us what the system denies to almost everyone else in this novel. Think about Jo — the crossing-sweeper who appears only a few chapters later. Jo has no room, no oval engraving of the months, no rose-leaves stored in a drawer. Jo exists in the negative space of everything Bleak House represents. And when the narrator turns to address him directly — and it is one of the most extraordinary moments in the novel — the language is almost unbearably tender. "There is something like a distant ray of light in thy muttered reason for this: 'He wos wery good to me, he wos!'" A boy who has received one act of human kindness in his entire life — and it is enough, just barely enough, to keep him out of outer darkness. That is Dickens's indictment, and it is devastating.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What moves me about that passage — Jo's passage — is the grammar of it. Dickens switches to the second person — "Jo, is it thou?" — as if he has stepped out of the fiction and is speaking directly to the boy. And then Jo's own voice — that phonetic spelling, "He wos wery good to me" — is the only evidence in his favour. But there is also something Dickens does nearby that I find quietly brilliant — the testimony of Mrs. Piper at the inquest. It is almost pure comedy — this torrent of parentheses and non-sequiturs. She keeps insisting on calling the dead man "the plaintive" — which is her mishearing of "plaintiff" — and it is ridiculous. And yet the comedy is also the point — this is what passes for testimony, for knowledge, for justice. Rumour and fear dressed up as evidence.

It really is one of the loveliest comic set-pieces Dickens ever wrote — and I use comic in the highest sense. Richard and Ada are so intoxicated with each other that they practically fall in love with Esther instead. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And what I want to add is that the sun doesn't cloud over randomly — it clouds over because of Chancery. Richard will spend every subsequent year of his life waiting on a lawsuit he did nothing to create. And Jarndyce sees the danger with complete clarity, even here, at the novel's most hopeful moment. The advice Jarndyce gives Richard is almost heartbreaking in retrospect: "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner." "Constancy in love is a good thing, but it means nothing, and is nothing, without constancy in every kind of effort." It's good counsel. Rational, experienced, generous. But what Chancery does — what courts of that kind are designed to do — is precisely to make effort feel futile. Richard doesn't fail because he's weak. He fails because the institution he's waiting on is designed to consume him.

And this is where Dickens's radicalism is most naked, I think — in the chapter's treatment of Jo. Jo is presented by the narrator as a graceless creature — the system's own language for the disposable child. But what Jo knows about Nemo is the most humanising testimony in the entire chapter. Grief, in this novel, is not distributed equally — but solidarity is. Jenny and Liz have nothing the system values. And yet they have built something the system cannot comprehend — mutual recognition. That is precisely what Tulkinghorn cannot do. That is precisely what the Court of Chancery cannot do.
