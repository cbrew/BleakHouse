James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me every time I return to those opening lines is that Dickens doesn't begin with a character, or a story, or even a place in any conventional sense. He begins with a condition. Listen to what he actually writes: "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That word "defiled" is doing extraordinary work — it's moral language applied to weather. The fog isn't merely atmospheric — it's already ethically charged before we've met a single person. And then notice how the fog descends the social ladder — from the ships and wharves down to the shivering apprentice boy pinching his toes and fingers on deck. The fog lands hardest on those who can least afford its cruelty.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that descent is so precisely engineered — it's not accidental, it's architectural. Dickens starts panoramic — the whole river, the marshes, the heights — and then he zooms, relentlessly, inward and downward. From geography to ships to barges to individual bodies — eyes, throats, toes, fingers. By the time we get to the shivering apprentice boy, the fog has become intimate — it's inside him. What I find thrilling is that this is also the novel's structural logic in miniature. Bleak House will keep performing this same movement — from the vast institutional to the unbearably personal — for nine hundred pages. Dickens shows you the system, and then he shows you what the system does to one small, cold body.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Which brings us directly into the courtroom — and to the passage that I think is one of the great polemical set-pieces in all of Victorian prose. Dickens places the Lord High Chancellor in his fog-wrapped court and then, with that savage irony of his, describes it all as exactly as it ought to be. "As here he is" — that repetition is withering. But the passage builds to something truly devastating: "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." Every shire. Every madhouse. Every churchyard. This is not London's problem — Dickens is insisting it is England's problem, a national pathology built into the very architecture of the law. And here's the detail that always stops me: the solicitors in this case have inherited it from their fathers, who made a fortune by it. The ruin of suitors is not an accident of the system — it is the system's product, and it is hereditary.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The anaphora in that passage — "which has... which has... which has" — it's almost liturgical. Dickens is doing something very deliberate with that rhythm: he's imitating the court's own procedural drone, its endless recitation of precedent. The form of the sentence enacts what the sentence is describing — the grinding, repetitive machinery of Chancery. And then he breaks it — with that extraordinary final warning: "Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here." That line is spoken, Dickens tells us, by honourable men who know perfectly well what they are part of. The system doesn't require villains — it requires complicity, and that is far more damning.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Complicity is exactly the right word, and it's one of the novel's great recurring themes — no one in Bleak House is simply innocent. This passage is extraordinary — and I'd argue it's one of the most politically charged moments in all of Victorian fiction. Dickens steps outside the story and speaks in his own voice — and what he says is almost unbearable to read clearly. He writes: "It must be a strange state, not merely to be told that I am scarcely human (as in the case of my offering myself for a witness), but to feel it of my own knowledge all my life!" He doesn't describe Jo's alienation from the outside — he ventriloquises it from within, which forces the reader into a position of impossible identification. We are made to feel, briefly, what Jo feels all his life.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That formal choice — the first-person ventriloquism — is one of Dickens's most daring moves in the whole novel. Remember that Bleak House has two narrators — the third-person omniscient voice and Esther Summerson's first-person memoir. But here, Dickens creates a third voice, briefly — the implied first person of Jo, who cannot narrate his own story because he cannot read or write. The very act of narrating on Jo's behalf is an act of testimony — and also an act of mourning for what Jo's own testimony would have been, if anyone had asked. And what makes it devastating is that parenthesis: "as in the case of my offering myself for a witness." Jo tried to participate in justice — he presented himself as a witness — and was turned away because he couldn't take the oath. The law made him invisible twice — once in life, and once in the very moment he tried to be seen.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And this is historically precise — under Victorian law, a witness who could not affirm a belief in God and in the divine sanction of an oath was legally incompetent to testify. Jo cannot read the Bible — he cannot even conceive of the institutional religion that underlies the law — and so the law simply excludes him. The system is structured so that the poorest, least educated members of society are also the least able to access its protections. And Dickens, with that corrosive irony, mentions the Constitution — "that inestimable jewel to him, if he only knew it." Jo does not know it — has never been permitted to know it — and it has never known him.

Reckless is exactly the right word — and it works, which is the miracle. The novel opens not with a character or a scene but with weather — fog, mud, rain — and Dickens hammers that weather into the very syntax. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the target of that satire is precisely chosen — Temple Bar was, at this moment in English legal history, a genuine scandal. Chancery in the 1840s and early 1850s, when Dickens was writing, had cases that had been running for decades, consuming estates that were the supposed prize. The Jarndyce case in the novel isn't exaggeration — it's barely heightened journalism. So when Dickens places the Lord High Chancellor at "the very heart of the fog" — that's not metaphor layered over fact. "At the very heart of the fog, sits the Lord High Chancellor in his High Court of Chancery." The fog IS the institution — confusion, delay, and opacity were its operating principles, not its failures. Dickens understood that intuitively, even if he never sat in a law library cataloguing the procedural history.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel's framing is compelling — and I'd add that the word 'connexion' Dickens chooses isn't neutral. In Victorian legal culture, connexion implied a relationship with social and sometimes legal standing — who is connected to whom determined your access to courts, to credit, to protection. Jo has no connexion in that sense — he knows nobody who knows anybody. Dickens gives him his famous verdict a few chapters later: Jo knows "nothink" — not because he's stupid, but because he's been systematically excluded from the networks that constitute knowledge and belonging. So the narrator's rhetorical question — 'what connexion can there be' — has a bitter double edge: the connexion exists, and Jo is the last to benefit from it.

It's one of those passages where I almost want to diagram it on a whiteboard — the architecture is that deliberate. Dickens gives us twelve sentences of landscape — the sodden trees, the soaked deer, the rifle shot that loses its sharpness — before Lady Dedlock herself appears. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's something worth pausing on in that phrase 'the Ghost's Walk' — the flagged pavement where the rain drips all night. Dickens drops it in almost parenthetically — 'called from old time the Ghost's Walk' — and moves on. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Esther's narration is one of the great achievements of the novel and one of its great critical puzzles — she's simultaneously self-deprecating and extraordinarily perceptive. In this passage, she watches Richard destroy himself through hope — and she almost can't bring herself to say it directly. The genius is in the pivot from Esther's carefully polite summary of Richard's reasoning to this: "Every one of them sunk him deeper in the infatuation." 'Every one of them' — that's Richard's arguments for Chancery. Each proof he marshals that the case will succeed becomes another weight pulling him under. And then Esther delivers the real verdict — not about the case, but about what Chancery is doing to a human life. "What a fatal link was riveting between his fresh youth and her faded age, between his free hopes and her caged birds, and her hungry garret, and her wandering mind." 'Riveting' — a word from metalwork, from industrial force — and it's riveting a link between youth and decay, freedom and confinement. That sentence is doing what the whole novel does in miniature — showing two lives at opposite stages, chained together by the same institution.

Miss Flite is historically quite accurate, which makes that passage even more chilling. There were real Miss Flites — people who had attached their entire identities to pending Chancery suits, haunting the court for decades. The institution was sufficiently opaque, and its timescales sufficiently impossible, that hope could become a kind of madness — sustainable precisely because the outcome was always deferred.
