James Blackstone (social_historian)
The title itself announces the central joke — and the central anxiety. 'Our Mutual Friend' — a phrase that was, even in Dickens's day, grammatically contested, socially loaded. It names the novel's inheritance plot before the plot has quite announced itself. And it points to a world where relationship is currency — who you know, who claims you, who you are prepared to be seen with. What I find fascinating is how Dickens materializes all of that abstraction into actual rooms, actual objects. Take the Bower — the Boffins' home — which Silas Wegg first enters and cannot quite classify. Dickens gives us the room in one perfectly calibrated sentence. "It was the queerest of rooms, fitted and furnished more like a luxurious amateur tap-room than anything else within the ken of Silas Wegg." 'Luxurious amateur tap-room' — that compound is doing extraordinary social work. The Boffins have money — sudden, bewildering, inherited money — and absolutely no template for what to do with it.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And the genius is that Dickens doesn't simply tell us the Boffins are class-displaced — he constructs a room that enacts it. There's a literal boundary in the floor — the flowery carpet stops, and sand and sawdust take over. On one side: drawing-room furniture, stuffed birds, waxen fruit under glass. On the other: a cold joint of meat, plainly visible on a shelf — honest appetite refusing to be decorative. Dickens is working architecturally here — the room is a cross-section of the social order, rendered in carpet and sawdust. And Wegg observing all of this — Wegg, who is himself a fraud, a man assembling a persona from borrowed culture — that's a richly comic irony.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What sits underneath all of that comedy, though, is a genuinely unsettling question about money and identity. Because the novel keeps asking: does inherited wealth corrupt — or merely reveal what was already there? John Harmon — who returns from abroad having already been left his father's fortune — is the most tortured expression of that question. Listen to how he describes his own homecoming — the self-interrogation of a man who cannot trust his own motives. "I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife." That triple repetition — shrinking, shrinking, mistrustful — is an almost liturgical self-accusation. And what is remarkable is that it is delivered as interior monologue — Harmon cross-examining himself: 'Is that so? That is exactly so.' The man cannot even trust his own memory without putting it to evidence.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That passage is one of the structural linchpins of the whole novel — and it comes buried in the middle, not at the beginning. Dickens holds the full explanation of Harmon's identity back for so long that by the time we get it, we have already formed our own suspicions — and been partly wrong. The deferred revelation is a formal strategy — Dickens is teaching the reader the same lesson Harmon is learning about himself. You cannot know who someone is until you know what they have endured — and what they have refused.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I find structurally remarkable about the Boffin passages is how they function as a kind of retrospective prologue to a character who is, at this point in the novel, already secretly present. John Harmon is listening, or will listen, to these stories about himself as a child — stories of love he was never permitted to receive fully. And then Mrs Boffin transforms that grief into a proposal that is itself a kind of narrative strategy. She says: The plan is to replace John Harmon with a child given John Harmon's name — which is an almost gothic manoeuvre, when you consider that the real John Harmon is standing in their midst, unnamed. Dickens is folding dramatic irony into what reads as simple tenderness. That is architectural genius.

And the legal dimension compounds the irony considerably. John Harmon's inheritance is conditional on marriage — a will designed by a man who used financial control as another form of the cruelty we saw with that bonnet.
