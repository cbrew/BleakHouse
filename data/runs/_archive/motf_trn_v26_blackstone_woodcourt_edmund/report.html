Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The Mill on the Floss is, among many other things, a novel about the cost of being exceptional in a world that cannot accommodate you. And Eliot establishes that cost with extraordinary economy very early on — in small scenes, in brief flashes of a child's inner life. There is a passage that I find almost unbearably precise about how love curdled by jealousy operates in a child — in Maggie's response to Lucy. Eliot writes — and the comedy here is perfectly calibrated — "Maggie would have thought a little while ago that she could never be cross with pretty little Lucy, any more than she could be cruel to a little white mouse." The white mouse is doing remarkable work there. It locates Lucy's prettiness in the register of the decorative and the helpless — something to be petted rather than befriended. And then Eliot shows us Maggie contemplating slapping Lucy — not out of cruelty exactly, but to wound Tom through her. It is a perfectly truthful account of how children — how people — experience love under threat.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I read that passage is how Eliot refuses to let us judge Maggie while absolutely showing us what Maggie is capable of. The narrator holds two things at once — moral clarity and absolute sympathy. And the syntax does so much of that work. The injustice of that — felt at seven years old — is the same injustice that will shadow Maggie's entire life. She is always already losing something she earned.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd add that this dynamic between Maggie and Tom isn't merely psychological — it is structural. Tom is sent away to be educated; Maggie stays home. And yet when Maggie visits Tom at Mr Stelling's, the novel shows us in precise, almost comic detail that she is the more intellectually alive of the two. Eliot describes how Maggie's questions about the Roman Empire — questions Tom would never have thought to ask — actually expanded his understanding. "He had actually come to a dim understanding of the fact that there had once been people upon the earth who were so fortunate as to know Latin without learning it through the medium of the Eton Grammar." "A dim understanding" — that is Eliot at her most surgically dry. Tom receives an expensive classical education and arrives at a dim understanding. Maggie, who is given nothing, illuminates the room.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Quite so — and there is a beautiful tenderness in that same passage that one must not allow the satire to eclipse. When Maggie is fetched away by Luke, Tom misses her — genuinely, grievously. Eliot writes that "he had really been brighter, and had got through his lessons better, since she had been there." The novel will not let Tom be simply the oppressor. He is limited — severely limited — but he is also capable of love, of needing Maggie. And the novel's final word on the two of them — carved, as it were, in stone — is only four words long. "In their death they were not divided." Everything the novel has built — every separation, every act of cruelty and tenderness — flows into that epitaph.

I can never read that line without feeling the full weight of what has happened to Maggie before those four words are even possible. The irrevocable wrong that must blot her life had been committed; she had brought sorrow into the lives of others,—into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love. The feeling of a few short weeks had hurried her into the sins her nature had most recoiled from,—breach of faith and cruel selfishness; she had rent the ties that had given meaning to duty, and had made herself an outlawed soul, with no guide but the wayward choice of her own passion. And where would that lead her? Where had it led her now? She had said she would rather die than fall into that temptation. She felt it now,—now that the consequences of such a fall had come before the outward act was completed. There was at least this fruit from all her years of striving after the highest and best,—that her soul though betrayed, beguiled, ensnared, could never deliberately consent to a choice of the lower. And a choice of what? O God! not a choice of joy, but of conscious cruelty and hardness; for could she ever cease to see before her Lucy and Philip, with their murdered trust and hopes? Her life with Stephen could have no sacredness; she must forever sink and wander vaguely, driven by uncertain impulse; for she had let go the clue of life,—that clue which once in the far-off years her young need had clutched so strongly. She had renounced all delights then, before she knew them, before they had come within her reach. Philip had been right when he told her that she knew nothing of renunciation; she had thought it was quiet ecstasy; she saw it face to face now,—that sad, patient, loving strength which holds the clue of life,—and saw that the thorns were forever pressing on its brow. James Blackstone (social_historian)
What that waking scene reveals, historically, is the impossibility of Maggie's position — not merely psychologically but materially and socially. She has transgressed — or is perceived to have transgressed — in a world that offered women almost no path to self-determination. She calls herself "an outlawed soul, with no guide but the wayward choice of her own passion." Outlawed is the precise word — in the older sense, someone outside the protection of the law. Women in Eliot's England who departed from the sanctioned path found themselves outside every system of social support. The flood at the end is symbolic, yes — but it is also Eliot's recognition that the social world had no place for Maggie to return to.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The passage that strikes me first in this segment is the narrator's portrait of Tulliver himself — a man whose self-image is entirely constructed by the flattery of his neighbours. George Eliot is ruthless about this particular form of self-deception — the way social consensus becomes the mirror in which a man sees himself as prosperous. The narrator puts it with magnificent economy — listen to this: "He had been always used to hear pleasant jokes about his advantages as a man who worked his own mill, and owned a pretty bit of land; and these jokes naturally kept up his sense that he was a man of considerable substance." Jokes. Not evidence. Not achievement. Jokes. That is a novelist doing sociology at the level of the individual psyche — showing precisely how a man comes to believe what is not true about himself.

They gave a pleasant flavour to his glass on a market-day, and if it had not been for the recurrence of half-yearly payments, Mr Tulliver would really have forgotten that there was a mortgage of two thousand pounds on his very desirable freehold. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And what makes that psychologically devastating is the legal reality underneath it — a two-thousand-pound mortgage on a freehold that Tulliver has essentially forgotten exists. Eliot understands perfectly how provincial credit worked in the early Victorian period. A miller with freehold land would be seen as creditworthy, and neighbours would borrow from him precisely because he appeared solid. But those loans are exactly what prevent him from paying down the debt — so reputation and ruin feed each other, invisibly. And then there is Basset itself — the portrait of that parish is as precise a piece of rural economics as you'll find in Victorian fiction. Poor soil, poor roads, a non-resident landlord, a non-resident vicar — every institution that should support the community has absented itself.

And that reckoning is where the novel becomes most difficult — and, I would argue, most honest. Maggie's speech to Stephen is not the language of a woman broken by society's judgment. It is her own moral code, applied to herself with extraordinary severity. I find myself agreeing with James more than I expected to, and I want to say why. There is a tendency among some readers — sympathetic readers — to treat Maggie's renunciation as a kind of defeat. As though Eliot is endorsing her society's cruelty. But that speech to Stephen is not the voice of defeat. It is the voice of someone who has looked at what she has done to Lucy — to Philip — and cannot look away. She says: 'I feel no excuse for myself, none.' That is not society speaking. That is conscience. Eliot believes — as Johnson believed, as Burke believed — that the moral life is lived in the particular, in the face of the specific person you have wronged.
