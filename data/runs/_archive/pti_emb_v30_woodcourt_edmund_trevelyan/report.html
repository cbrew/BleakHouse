Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I come back to these opening paragraphs is that Forster describes the caves before any character enters them. There is, as he puts it, little to see and no eye to see it. The caves exist in a kind of absolute darkness — and then the visitor arrives and strikes a match. And what Forster does with that single match is — honestly — one of the most beautiful things in the novel. He writes — and I want to read this carefully — "Fists and fingers thrust above the advancing soil — here at last is their skin, finer than any covering acquired by the animals, smoother than windless water, more voluptuous than love." More voluptuous than love — written about rock. Forster is describing geological matter, and he gives it the language of longing.

What gets me — every single time — is the two flames. "The two flames approach and strive to unite, but cannot, because one of them breathes air, the other stone." Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'd add one thing, if I may — Forster ends that passage with a sentence of devastating simplicity. "The cave is dark again, like all the caves." After all that evanescent beauty — the nebulae, the comet's tail, the voluptuous stone — we return to absolute equivalence. Every cave is the same cave. Now — the hills themselves, seen from the train. Forster gives us Kawa Dol — this single slab of rock that simply shoots upward. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Which brings us to that extraordinary paragraph as the elephant moves toward the hills. The passage begins with something Forster calls a spiritual silence — and he is careful to say it invaded more senses than the ear. Then comes the line I find genuinely disturbing, each time I read it: "Life went on as usual, but had no consequences, that is to say, sounds did not echo or thoughts develop." "Everything seemed cut off at its root, and therefore infected with illusion." Infected is exactly the right word — not suffused, not touched, infected. Something has entered the atmosphere that corrupts the ordinary processes of experience.

We turn now to something Forster does with particular relish — the comedy of empire. The Anglo-Indian world of Chandrapore is populated with figures who are both absurd and alarming — and Forster holds both things at once. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Absolutely — and the proof is right there in chapter five, in this wonderful little portrait of the Anglo-Indian community doing amateur dramatics. They've performed Cousin Kate — the very embodiment of comfortable, undemanding English gentility — and next year, Forster tells us, they'll do Quality Street or The Yeomen of the Guard. Save for this annual incursion, Forster writes, they left literature alone. That word — incursion — is doing a great deal of work, isn't it? As if literature were a foreign territory they made one reluctant border crossing into per year, then retreated back to safety. But then there's poor Ronny's viola. Forster writes — and I adore this sentence — that "a viola was almost a demerit, and certainly not the sort of instrument one mentioned in public." The viola — that most melancholy, most interior of instruments — reduced to something faintly shameful. It's snobbery turned inside out — not the snobbery of the cultivated against the crude, but of the crude against the cultivated.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
One sees exactly what Forster is doing — the Public School attitude, he calls it, flourishing more vigorously than it can yet hope to do in England. It's a sharp observation, and I won't deny it. The colonial context intensifies certain English vices — the fear of originality, the terror of distinction — because distance from home creates a kind of institutional anxiety. Where I would gently resist the reading is here: Forster risks making Ronny merely a symptom rather than a person. The viola incident is marvellously funny — but it matters that Mrs. Moore notices the change in her son. That is a mother watching a son she loves become a man she no longer entirely recognises. The comedy and the grief are inseparable — and that, to my mind, is where Forster's real moral seriousness resides.

That passage is one of the great pieces of Forsterian sleight-of-hand — the mundane and the transcendent held in perfect suspension. But I want to bring in Aziz, because his relationship to comedy is something altogether stranger and more interesting. There's this small, precise moment in chapter six where Forster writes — "When his spirits were up he felt that the English are a comic institution, and he enjoyed being misunderstood by them." A comic institution — what a gift of a phrase. The English have spent centuries congratulating themselves on their singular genius for self-mockery. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to dwell on that word fragility — and it connects, I think, to what happens later when the herd instinct overwhelms individual judgment entirely. In chapter seventeen, after the accusation against Aziz, Forster turns his satirical eye on the collective psychology of Anglo-India under pressure. He describes Fielding — who has kept his head — being viewed with open hostility by the Collector. And then Forster delivers one of the most polished sentences in the novel. "Nothing enrages Anglo-India more than the lantern of reason if it is exhibited for one moment after its extinction is decreed." The image of the lantern — extinguished by collective decree — is quite magnificent in its precision. Reason is not argued down — it is simply ruled out of order, as if by some unspoken procedural vote. And pity, wrath, heroism fill the vacuum — all the noble-sounding emotions that in fact require no thought whatsoever.

I want to push on that word yet — because it is doing enormous moral work. Not yet implies that the thing withheld is possible — that it is time and circumstance, not human nature, that prevents it.
