Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Dickens is doing in those opening pages is something quite audacious — he's refusing to give you a character, a plot, an entry point. Instead he gives you a condition. The fog isn't atmosphere in the decorative sense — it is the first argument of the novel. Consider the sentence he builds toward — the one that names the Court of Chancery for the first time. "Never can there come fog too thick, never can there come mud and mire too deep, to assort with the groping and floundering condition of this High Court of Chancery." The fog doesn't represent Chancery — Dickens is saying they are the same substance. The legal system and the weather share a nature: they descend on everyone indiscriminately, they obscure, they cost you your life by inches. And this is not sentiment — it is a structural claim about how power operates in Victorian England.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel is right that the opening is an argument — I'd only quarrel with what the argument is for. The fog is an extraordinarily precise literary device — but its precision is moral, not economic. Dickens is establishing that Chancery is a place where judgment is impossible — where no one can see clearly enough to tell right from wrong. That is a theological complaint before it is a political one. He describes the Lord Chancellor — the highest legal authority in England — sitting in the fog, presiding over the fog. "On such an afternoon, if ever, the Lord High Chancellor ought to be sitting here — as here he is — with a foggy glory round his head." 'Foggy glory' — that is one of the great withering phrases in Victorian prose. Dickens is not saying that power exploits people — he is saying that power has confused itself, mistaken its own mist for divinity.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I re-read those pages is the rhythm — the sheer insistence of repetition. Fog everywhere. Fog up the river. Fog down the river. Fog on the Essex marshes. It's almost hypnotic — you surrender to it before you've had a chance to resist. And I think that's deliberate — Dickens is putting you inside the fog's experience, not just describing it from outside. What I love is the moment he brings in the people — 'the chance people on the bridges peeping over the parapets.' "Peeping over the parapets into a nether sky of fog, with fog all round them, as if they were up in a balloon, and hanging in the misty clouds." 'As if they were up in a balloon' — that is such a tender simile. These ordinary Londoners are lost — but the image makes their lostness briefly, strangely beautiful.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Caroline touches on something I think is underappreciated — that the fog democratises misery, at least in the opening. Everyone is in the fog — the Lord Chancellor on his bench, the mudlark on the river, the chance people on the bridges. But the novel is going to spend the next nine hundred pages showing us that the fog does not land on everyone equally. There is a character named Jo — a crossing-sweeper, illiterate, parentless — who exists in the fog with no map, no name anyone will use, no one to claim him. Dickens gives him one of the most devastating sentences in the book — and I want us to come back to it. But the opening already tells us: this is a novel about who gets to navigate and who simply drowns.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I wouldn't dispute Daniel's observation about Jo — but I'd locate the tragedy slightly differently. Jo's suffering in this novel is not merely systemic — it is personal, and it implicates specific people who failed him, ignored him, passed him along. That is rather more uncomfortable than blaming a system, because it requires us to ask what we ourselves would have done. Dickens understood — as Burke understood — that the abstract can be a refuge from the specific. The fog is everywhere, yes — but the question the novel presses is: what did you do, you in particular, while it was thick?
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I find myself wanting to hold both of those readings at once — and I think the novel actually asks you to. Because there's a passage — quite early — where the two narrators announce themselves, and the shift in voice is almost physical. You have the omniscient narrator, bitter and scything — and then suddenly Esther: 'I have a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write my portion of these pages.' "I have a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write my portion of these pages, for I know I am not clever." She's immediately self-deprecating — and immediately, completely trustworthy. The system narrator sees the fog; Esther sees people. And that double vision is what makes Bleak House unlike anything else Dickens ever wrote.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me about Bleak House — and it strikes me every time I return to it — is that Dickens understands language as a site of power. The law does not merely adjudicate disputes — it controls what can be said, what can be meant, what can even be implied. There's a moment in chapter thirty-eight where Mr. Guppy — this wonderfully slippery little lawyer's clerk — refuses to acknowledge that he has implied anything at all. "Excuse me, miss — but we had better not travel out of the record into implication. I cannot admit that I implied anything." 'Travel out of the record into implication' — that phrase is doing extraordinary work. The record is the law's official account of events — and anything outside that record simply does not exist. Jo, the crossing-sweeper, cannot read, cannot write — he cannot enter the record at all. Guppy's courtroom logic is the comic surface of something Dickens is deadly serious about: the system erases people by refusing to admit they imply anything.
