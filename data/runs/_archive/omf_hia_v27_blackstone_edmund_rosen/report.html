Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The dust heaps are, first and foremost, a magnificent grotesque — the kind of invention that makes one grateful to be a reader of English prose. Dickens understood that the great literary image must be both literal and resonant — and the Mounds are precisely that. They are actual heaps of refuse — ash, bones, the accumulated waste of the city — and they represent an enormous inherited fortune. What interests me most, at the outset, is the will that Wegg discovers — and what it tells us about old John Harmon, the miser who amassed this fortune. Wegg reads it aloud to his confederate Venus, and the language is savagely comic — listen to the stated reason for disinheritance. "Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown." The monstrous logic of it — a man so thoroughly estranged from human affection that he punishes his family from beyond the grave, and calls that justice. That is a study in the deformation of character — not a political thesis, but a portrait of what avarice does to the soul over a lifetime.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is absolutely right about the will's grotesque comedy — but I'd add that a contemporary reader would have felt its legal absurdity quite sharply. The Crown as residual legatee — that is, property passing to the state for want of any heir — was a real mechanism, and a deeply resented one. To choose the Crown over one's own family was not merely eccentric — it was a kind of aggressive social statement, a final act of contempt. And what Wegg immediately does with this document is equally legible in institutional terms — he sees it as leverage, as a mechanism of extraction. His scheme is elaborately laid out — let Boffin do the expensive work of excavating the Mounds, then produce the earlier will at the moment of maximum vulnerability. Dickens captures the legal menace of the thing with one perfectly chosen word, which Wegg delivers as though it were an entire contract. "He considered it plain that such price was stateable in a single expressive word, and that the word was, 'Halves!'" One word — and Wegg immediately begins negotiating with himself over whether it ought to be two-thirds, or three-fourths. The comedy is exquisite, but it is grounded in a perfectly real form of blackmail — the threatened exposure of a legal instrument.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me most about that passage is the language Dickens gives Wegg — "moral bondage and slavery." Those are not accidental words in a novel about inheritance and labour and the ownership of human beings' time and futures. Wegg proposes — and I want to quote this exactly — that Boffin should be kept "in a state of abject moral bondage and slavery until the time when they should see fit to permit him to purchase his freedom at the price of half his possessions." Purchase his freedom — that is the language of emancipation, deployed here as the vocabulary of extortion. Dickens is writing in the years immediately following the American Civil War — that resonance was not lost on his readers, and I don't think it was lost on him. And what Wegg reveals — without intending to — is that the entire structure of inheritance he hopes to exploit is itself a system of bondage dressed up as property law.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I would resist only slightly the weight Daniel places on the political register — not because it isn't there, but because I think it risks obscuring what is most interesting about Wegg as a character. Dickens gives us something rarer than a social type — he gives us a specific study in the psychology of petty power. There is a passage that I find almost unbearably acute — Wegg cannot sleep without first going to hover over Boffin's house in what Dickens calls "the superior character of its Evil Genius." "Power — unless it be the power of intellect or virtue — has ever the greatest attraction for the lowest natures." That is a moral observation of the first order — not a structural critique, but a truth about human nature that would have satisfied Dr Johnson himself. Wegg doesn't want Boffin's money, not yet — he wants the sensation of being able to destroy him. That is a portrait of evil that transcends any particular historical moment.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That image of Wegg hovering over the house — "the roof of a house of cards" — is worth pausing on for a moment. The Boffin fortune is, structurally, exactly that fragile — built on a fortune in refuse, secured by a will whose legitimacy is now in question, threatened by a document in the hands of a man with a wooden leg and no scruples. The precariousness of Victorian inherited wealth was not metaphorical — it was genuinely institutional. Wills were contested, documents were lost and found, fortunes turned on the testimony of witnesses long dead. Dickens knew this world intimately — and he knew that the dust heap was the perfect symbol for it: something valuable buried in something everyone else had discarded.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
He is doing something rather old-fashioned and rather courageous. He is dramatising the moment when a young woman's vanity is confronted by her own better nature, and finds itself, for the first time, embarrassed. The mirror is not Dickens being clever about symbolism. It is Bella caught in the act of self-examination — and unable to like what she finds. The passage is almost brutally precise about the split in her mind. She thinks to herself: 'What he said was very sensible, I am sure, and very true, I am sure. It is only what I often say to myself.' And then, with devastating self-honesty, she continues: 'Don't I like it then? No, I don't like it.' That gap — between what she knows to be true and what she can bear to endorse — is where the whole moral drama of Bella Wilfer lives. It is not, I would insist, about money in any abstract structural sense. It is about a particular young woman discovering that her professed values are ones she is not actually willing to live by.

Edmund, I don't dispute the psychological acuity of the scene — it is quite brilliant. But I think we need to ask what it is that Boffin actually said to Bella that she cannot bring herself to like. He told her to marry for money. He told her to be prudent, to treat matrimony as a transaction. And what Dickens has so ruthlessly observed is that this advice is entirely continuous with how Boffin himself is now behaving — corrupted by his new wealth. Look at what Bella actually sees when she glances at him in that earlier moment. 'A dark cloud of suspicion, covetousness, and conceit, overshadowing the once open face.' That is not a portrait of an individual moral failing. That is what money does to people — reliably, systematically — within the world this novel is mapping. Bella's revulsion is her conscience registering something she cannot yet fully articulate: that the logic of wealth is corroding everyone around her.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I think both of you are right, and the tension between your positions is precisely what makes this novel so historically interesting. But let me put Boffin's behaviour in a slightly different frame. There is a scene, very close to these chapters, where Boffin erupts about what he calls the absurdity of charitable patronage. He asks, with genuine indignation, what all the titles actually mean. James has identified something crucial: the novel's philanthropy is not a counterweight to the money problem, it is a further expression of it. That institutional dimension is exactly what I want to dwell on — because the Duke of Linseed episode is historically precise in ways that are easy to miss. Dickens gives us the form letter in full, including the postscript about the steward's fee. "Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet." The word "private" is doing all the satirical weight there — it is of course printed by the hundreds. The steward system was a real mechanism — you paid your fee, your name appeared on the printed bill, and the duke got the dinner. The charity was secondary — the social transaction was primary.

These are the corporate beggars. But there are, besides, the individual beggars; and how does the heart of the Secretary fail him when he has to cope with them! And they must be coped with to some extent, because they all enclose documents (they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf), the non-return of which would be their ruin. That is to say, they are utterly ruined now, but they would be more utterly ruined then. Among these correspondents are several daughters of general officers, long accustomed to every luxury of life (except spelling), who little thought, when their gallant fathers waged war in the Peninsula, that they would ever have to appeal to those whom Providence, in its inscrutable wisdom, has blessed with untold gold, and from among whom they select the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, for a maiden effort in this wise, understanding that he has such a heart as never was. The Secretary learns, too, that confidence between man and wife would seem to obtain but rarely when virtue is in distress, so numerous are the wives who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted husbands, who would never permit it; while, on the other hand, so numerous are the husbands who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted wives, who would instantly go out of their senses if they had the least suspicion of the circumstance. There are the inspired beggars, too. These were sitting, only yesterday evening, musing over a fragment of candle which must soon go out and leave them in the dark for the rest of their nights, when surely some Angel whispered the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, to their souls, imparting rays of hope, nay confidence, to which they had long been strangers! Akin to these are the suggestively-befriended beggars. They were partaking of a cold potato and water by the flickering and gloomy light of a lucifer-match, in their lodgings (rent considerably in arrear, and heartless landlady threatening expulsion ‘like a dog’ into the streets), when a gifted friend happening to look in, said, ‘Write immediately to Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire,’ and would take no denial. There are the nobly independent beggars too. These, in the days of their abundance, ever regarded gold as dross, and have not yet got over that only impediment in the way of their amassing wealth, but they want no dross from Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire; No, Mr Boffin; the world may term it pride, paltry pride if you will, but they wouldn’t take it if you offered it; a loan, sir—for fourteen weeks to the day, interest calculated at the rate of five per cent per annum, to be bestowed upon any charitable institution you may name—is all they want of you, and if you have the meanness to refuse it, count on being despised by these great spirits. There are the beggars of punctual business-habits too. These will make an end of themselves at a quarter to one P.M. on Tuesday, if no Post-office order is in the interim received from Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire; arriving after a quarter to one P.M. on Tuesday, it need not be sent, as they will then (having made an exact memorandum of the heartless circumstances) be ‘cold in death.’ There are the beggars on horseback too, in another sense from the sense of the proverb. These are mounted and ready to start on the highway to affluence. The goal is before them, the road is in the best condition, their spurs are on, the steed is willing, but, at the last moment, for want of some special thing—a clock, a violin, an astronomical telescope, an electrifying machine—they must dismount for ever, unless they receive its equivalent in money from Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire. Less given to detail are the beggars who make sporting ventures. These, usually to be addressed in reply under initials at a country post-office, inquire in feminine hands, Dare one who cannot disclose herself to Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, but whose name might startle him were it revealed, solicit the immediate advance of two hundred pounds from unexpected riches exercising their noblest privilege in the trust of a common humanity?

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The system producing that kind of desperation had a very specific institutional face — the Poor Law. And this is where Dickens steps out of the fiction entirely and speaks in his own voice — in the passage about Betty Higden. He anticipates the objection that he is being sentimental or utopian — and he dismisses it with considerable force. The passage builds to a verdict that I think deserves to be read slowly and fully. "I believe there has been in England, since the days of the Stuarts, no law so often infamously administered, no law so often openly violated, no law habitually so ill-supervised." And then — and this is the legal historian's point — he adds the key line. "In the majority of the shameful cases of disease and death from destitution, that shock the Public and disgrace the country, the illegality is quite equal to the inhumanity." He is not making a sentimental argument — he is making a legal one.

In my social experiences since Mrs Betty Higden came upon the scene and left it, I have found Circumlocutional champions disposed to be warm with me on the subject of my view of the Poor Law. My friend Mr Bounderby could never see any difference between leaving the Coketown ‘hands’ exactly as they were, and requiring them to be fed with turtle soup and venison out of gold spoons. Idiotic propositions of a parallel nature have been freely offered for my acceptance, and I have been called upon to admit that I would give Poor Law relief to anybody, anywhere, anyhow. Putting this nonsense aside, I have observed a suspicious tendency in the champions to divide into two parties; the one, contending that there are no deserving Poor who prefer death by slow starvation and bitter weather, to the mercies of some Relieving Officers and some Union Houses; the other, admitting that there are such Poor, but denying that they have any cause or reason for what they do. The records in our newspapers, the late exposure by The Lancet , and the common sense and senses of common people, furnish too abundant evidence against both defences. But, that my view of the Poor Law may not be mistaken or misrepresented, I will state it. I believe there has been in England, since the days of the Stuarts , no law so often infamously administered, no law so often openly violated, no law habitually so ill-supervised. In the majority of the shameful cases of disease and death from destitution, that shock the Public and disgrace the country, the illegality is quite equal to the inhumanity—and known language could say no more of their lawlessness.
Dickens defends his critique of the Poor Law as grounded in observable fact and ethical principle, arguing it is the most infamously administered law in English history.
