Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
They do represent an extraordinary range — and yet I think they speak to a single underlying question that Gaskell never stops asking. That question is: what does loyalty cost, and who is made to bear the price? The first passage — Higgins describing Boucher's betrayal of the Union — is one of the most morally compressed scenes in the novel. Higgins is not a sentimental man, and his dialect carries a weight of working-class grievance that Gaskell renders with forensic precision. Listen to what he says about Boucher going to Hamper's to beg for work: "He went wi' his mealy-mouthed face, that turns me sick to look at, a-asking for work, though he knowed well enough the new rule, o' pledging themselves to give nought to th' Unions; nought to help the starving turn-out!" That phrase — mealy-mouthed — is doing enormous work. It's not simply cowardice Higgins is condemning — it's the particular dishonesty of the man who smiles while he betrays.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to resist, for a moment, the purely sociological reading of Boucher. What Gaskell is also examining here is weakness of will — a failure of individual moral fibre that is not reducible to class position. Boucher is a man who has accepted the Union's charity and then sought to profit by abandoning its principles. Higgins himself recognises this — note that his condemnation is moral, not merely tactical. He says the man would have, in his own words, "clemmed to death" without the Union's help. "Why he'd a clemmed to death, if th' Union had na helped him in his pinch." That is gratitude betrayed — and betrayal of gratitude is among the oldest and most serious moral failings any civilisation has named.

Edmund is right that Gaskell frames this morally — but the moral frame itself is produced by material conditions. Boucher doesn't betray the Union because he is a bad man — he betrays it because his children are starving. The system creates the desperation that makes betrayal thinkable — and then condemns the individual for the choice the system forced. Which brings me to the second passage — Margaret at Helstone, watching the light change through windows where trees have been cut down. It seems on its surface to be pure pastoral elegy — but even here, the forces of change are not natural, they are imposed. That parenthetical — a great improvement it was called — is one of those lines you have to read twice before you hear the blade in it. On the page it looks like neutral description — read aloud, the irony sharpens into something close to grief. But then Gaskell does something quite extraordinary — she lifts the whole passage from the particular into the universal. The image she reaches for: "the natural mutations brought by days and months and years, which carry us on imperceptibly from childhood to youth, and thence through manhood to age, whence we drop like fruit, fully ripe, into the quiet mother earth." We drop like fruit, fully ripe — that is not consolation, quite. It is something older and stranger — acceptance without self-pity.

It is perhaps the finest piece of prose in the novel — and it earns its beauty because Margaret is not indulging in sentiment. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And then we come to the third passage — and what a turn it takes. Margaret is proposing to lend Thornton nearly two thousand pounds from her own savings — to keep Marlborough Mills running. She frames it, pointedly, as a business arrangement advantageous to herself — and that framing is doing crucial work. Her exact words: "if you would take some money of mine, eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds, lying just at this moment unused in the bank, and bringing me in only two and a half per cent.—you could pay me much better interest, and might go on working Marlborough Mills." She is a woman of independent means proposing to invest in industrial production — in 1855, that is almost unprecedented for a woman of her station. The trembling in her hands as she searches for the papers — Gaskell notes that — tells us that beneath the businesslike composure she is terrified.
