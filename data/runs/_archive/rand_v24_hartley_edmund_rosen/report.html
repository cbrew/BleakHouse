Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Dickens does in those first pages is almost avant-garde — and it's easy to miss that because the prose is so seductive. He gives us two narrators for the whole novel — and he introduces them without announcing it, without any apparatus of explanation. The first narrator is this cold, omniscient, present-tense voice — the fog voice, I call it. It sees everything and explains nothing. And then there is Esther — warm, self-deprecating, retrospective — who begins her own narrative entirely without fanfare. The structural provocation is this: Dickens is telling you, before a single plot event has occurred, that reality in this novel will always be double. There is what the system sees — the fog's-eye view — and there is what the individual feels. And those two things will never quite coincide.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think that is beautifully put, and I largely agree — though I might frame the duality rather differently. The fog, for me, is first and foremost a moral atmosphere. It represents a condition of human confusion — what happens when institutions meant to embody justice have so entirely lost their way that no one can see clearly anymore. Dickens writes — and I consider this among the great opening sentences in English fiction — "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping." That word — defiled — is doing extraordinary moral work. The river is not merely obscured — it is corrupted, polluted by its passage through human society. Dickens is a moralist above all, and the fog is his judgment rendered as weather.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund is right that the fog is moral — but I think we need to ask: whose morality, and in whose interest? The fog doesn't fall equally on everyone in this novel. It smothers Tom-all-Alone's — the slum — in a way it never smothers Chesney Wold. Think of Jo — the crossing sweeper — and what Dickens actually gives him to say about the law. He brings Jo before the coroner's inquest and the boy simply cannot answer the questions — not because he is stupid, but because the law has never once spoken to him in a language he can understand. Dickens writes it with devastating flatness — "Name, Jo. Nothing else that he knows on. Don't know that everybody has two names. Never heerd of sich a think." That is not sentiment — that is a precise social diagnosis. The fog is the system's opacity, and it is not an accident — it is a structure.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Daniel has just done — reading Jo's dialect as diagnostic rather than decorative — that's exactly right, and it opens something important about how Dickens uses voice. Every character in this novel speaks a register that places them socially — instantly, unmistakably.
