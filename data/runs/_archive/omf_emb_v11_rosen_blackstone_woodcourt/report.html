The first thing to understand about this novel is that its central symbol is not metaphorical — it is literal. The wealth that drives every relationship in the book comes from dust heaps — mountains of the city's refuse. And Dickens, through Mortimer, gives us the inventory with a kind of deadpan relish that I find devastating. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The dust heaps were real, and the money in them was real — which is worth saying clearly, because modern readers sometimes assume Dickens is exaggerating for effect. Victorian dust yards were enormously profitable operations — the waste was sorted, sold on, and recycled into the industrial economy in ways that made certain contractors genuinely wealthy. So when Dickens writes that the old man "grew rich as a Dust Contractor, and lived in a hollow in a hilly country entirely composed of Dust" — that's not fantasy. "He grew rich as a Dust Contractor, and lived in a hollow in a hilly country entirely composed of Dust." That is a precise social observation dressed in comic language. What Dickens understood — and what I think Daniel is absolutely right to emphasise — is that the system of accumulation this man represents was perfectly legal and perfectly respectable. That is what makes it so corrosive.

The 1834 Poor Law Amendment Act was — in principle — a rationalisation of the relief system. In practice, it introduced the principle of 'less eligibility': conditions inside the workhouse had to be worse than the lowest independent labourer's life outside. The theory was that this would deter the undeserving poor from seeking relief. The effect was that it punished anyone desperate enough to need it. Dickens, writing in his postscript to the novel, is unusually combative on this point — he names the law directly and makes a very precise legal charge.
