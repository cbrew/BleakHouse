Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right to isolate that image, and I think it points to something rather more specific than structural satire. What Dickens understands — and what makes him morally serious rather than merely polemical — is that the law destroys character. Not just finances, not just time — character itself. Mr. Jarndyce explains the suit to Esther with what I can only call magnificent exasperation. He describes how equity sends questions to law, and law sends them back — endlessly, pointlessly — and then arrives at this: "And thus, through years and years, and lives and lives, everything goes on, constantly beginning over and over again, and nothing ever ends." The repetition there — years and years, lives and lives — is not rhetorical decoration. It is the formal enactment of the very futility he is describing. Dickens is not simply an entertainer with a grievance — he is a moralist who has found the perfect dramatic form for his indignation.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The opening of Bleak House is one of the most audacious gambits in the history of the English novel. Dickens gives us no character, no scene, no dialogue — just a condition. He simply announces the state of the world, and the word he uses is the most important single word in the book. "Fog everywhere." Two words, and the entire moral atmosphere of the novel is already in place. Then Dickens expands it, almost obsessively — he won't let the fog go. "Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." Notice the parenthesis — "and dirty" — dropped in with such casual contempt, as though the narrator can barely suppress a private joke at London's expense.

I want to gently resist the reading of fog purely as political metaphor — Chancery as fog, capitalism as fog, and so forth. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Edmund, I think we're closer than we appear — and I'll show you why through a very different passage. In chapter eleven, Dickens gives us the dead body of Nemo — the unknown man, the man without a name. And the narrator performs something astonishing: he imagines the dead man's mother looking at her infant son and then asks — "Oh, if in brighter days the now-extinguished fire within him ever burned for one woman who held him in her heart, where is she, while these ashes are above the ground!" "These ashes" — a dead man, referred to as ashes while his body is still above ground. The narrator is performing grief — or rather, performing the grief that no living person has come to perform. That is both a moral gesture, Edmund, and a structural one — the novel itself mourns what society has left unmourned.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Esther is the most technically interesting narrator in Victorian fiction, and she is also the most deceptive — deceptive in the most generous sense. She claims she doesn't know things, that she can barely interpret what she sees — and then reveals, through the very structure of her sentences, that she knows everything. There's a moment in chapter six where she describes a glance from Mr. Jarndyce — just a glance — and unpacks it with extraordinary precision. The triple clause — confided, knew that he confided, and that she received the confidence — is a phenomenology of a single wordless exchange. Esther doesn't just notice the communication — she models how mutual understanding works between two people who trust each other implicitly.

“My dear Miss Summerson,” she replied, “on Mr. Carstone’s character and conduct. He is of such a very easy disposition that probably he would never think it worth while to mention how he really feels, but he feels languid about the profession. He has not that positive interest in it which makes it his vocation. If he has any decided impression in reference to it, I should say it was that it is a tiresome pursuit. Now, this is not promising. Young men like Mr. Allan Woodcourt who take it from a strong interest in all that it can do will find some reward in it through a great deal of work for a very little money and through years of considerable endurance and disappointment. But I am quite convinced that this would never be the case with Mr. Carstone.”
