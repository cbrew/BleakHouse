Edmund Leigh
The fog in c1:p0 and c1:p1 is not a metaphor for institutional corruption — it is the moral correlative of institutional akrasia. Dickens is making a claim Johnson would have recognised and Burke would have ratified: when institutions which embody a society's practical wisdom decay, the result is not injustice in the abstract but literal, physical, unchosen suffering inflicted on the innocent. The fog is not like Chancery. The fog is what Chancery does to lived experience. And when Kenge says 'Repeal it? Never' — he is speaking for the fog.
Key points:
  • The fog passages (c1:p0 and c1:p1) are not primarily sociological but moral-temporal: the Megalosaurus signals moral anachronism — the persistence of archaic suffering into a moment that ought to have moved beyond it. The fog is the moral correlative of institutional akrasia, not merely a symbol of industrial London.
  • The Vholes passage (c39:p4) is where Dickens makes his precise moral-philosophical argument: respectability is not virtue, and in a corrupted institution, respectability actively replaces and extinguishes virtue. Kenge's speech enacts institutional akrasia — the system knows what is right, gestures toward it in the subjunctive, and then structurally declines to act.
  • Dickens is not a Benthamite reformer. His argument is more Burkean and more troubling: once an institution has decayed past a certain point, the people who staff it are no longer fully responsible agents. Vholes is not a conventional villain — the institution has colonised his character. A purely political reading flattens this into class analysis.
  • The grammatical architecture of Kenge's speech — nested subjunctives, parenthetical retreats, active declaratives for institutional self-defence — is itself the satirical point. The asymmetry between grammatical weight and moral weight enacts the corruption Dickens is diagnosing.

Daniel Rosen
The opening of Bleak House is not a description of mystery. It is a description of a working cost-extraction system, and the fog is its cover story. The only people confused are the ones paying — and they are paying because the system is designed to confuse them. Kenge is not in the fog. He is selling it.
Key points:
  • The opening of Bleak House is not a mood piece or atmosphere — it is a precise economic description of a city under coal capital, with suffering distributed according to class position
  • The fog inventory in c1:p1 functions as a class map, moving syntactically from upstream landed property down to the unfree apprentice labour at the economic bottom, with the fog's verbs shifting from ambient to 'cruelly pinching' as class position descends
  • The Megalosaurus passage places Holborn Hill — a collision point of financial capital, legal capital, and slum rookeries — in deep time to argue the city has industrialised its upper circuits while leaving infrastructure for the poor in a pre-modern condition
  • The Vholes passage in c39:p4 is a textbook case of regulatory capture a century before the concept had a name: institutional interest is converted into moral claim via 'three daughters,' and Kenge's passive voice naturalises politically contingent fee extraction as inevitable

Daniel Rosen (marxist_cultural_historian)
What strikes you immediately, once you see it, is that Dickens doesn't treat all bodies equally in that fog. At the top of the passage, the fog flows and rolls — those are verbs with a kind of grandeur, almost geological. Then it creeps into cabooses of collier-brigs and droops on gunwales — it's already becoming more intimate, more enclosing. And then you reach the bottom of the social ladder, and the grammar changes completely. Dickens writes — and I want to read this carefully — 'fog cruelly pinching the toes and fingers of his shivering little 'prentice boy on deck.' Cruelly pinching. Not flowing, not rolling — pinching. The fog has become an agent of physical harm the moment it reaches someone with no protection. A first-time reader feels the atmosphere. What I'm saying is that the atmosphere is structured — it's a diagram of who suffers and how much. The apprentice boy doesn't get metaphor. He gets cold toes.

Edmund Leigh (moral_philosopher)
Daniel's observation about the verbs is acute — I don't want to dismiss it. But I think we're in danger of reading the passage as a sociological chart when it is something more unsettling than that. Before the fog even arrives, Dickens gives us this extraordinary sentence — let me find the exact words — 'As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill.' Now — what is that sentence doing, morally? It is placing London outside of time — before civilization, before law, before any of the institutional arrangements we rely on to distinguish ourselves from brutes. What I've called the fog as moral correlative is precisely this: the fog doesn't symbolise Chancery from a distance — it enacts what Chancery does to the capacity for deliberate, purposeful action. Akrasia is the ancient term for weakness of will — knowing the right thing and being unable to do it. Chancery produces institutional akrasia: the system knows it causes harm and continues anyway. The fog is not a class map. It's a picture of what it feels like to be inside a structure that has lost its moral will.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_cultural_historian)
Edmund, I want to be honest about where I push back — because I think the moral-philosophical frame, however elegant, carries a risk. When we say the fog enacts institutional akrasia, we're inside a warm room making sense of suffering. The 'prentice boy on deck is not thinking about the decay of deliberate agency. I'm saying that concentrating on meaning deflects from cause — and Dickens, I think, wants both.

Oliver Trevelyan (actor_and_narrator)
Can I just — as the person in this room who has actually had to read this passage aloud — say something about what it does to your lungs? The word 'fog' appears — I've counted — ten times in that opening paragraph. And each time you say it, you have to push through a soft obstruction in the throat. F-og. Dickens has written a sentence that physically enacts the condition it describes. You are fogged as you speak it. And then you hit the payoff — which is the one I always savour — 'Chance people on the bridges peeping over the parapets into a nether sky of fog, with fog all round them, as if they were up in a balloon and hanging in the misty clouds.' The balloon image is sublime — and it's a joke. You've been sinking through fog for the whole paragraph and suddenly you're floating above it, except you're not, you're still inside it. Daniel and Edmund are both right, I think, and the passage earns both readings because Dickens is working at every level simultaneously — sonic, comic, moral, economic.

Edmund Leigh (moral_philosopher)
With pleasure. The passage begins with a client who wants to change the law — and Kenge's response is a masterpiece of institutional self-protection dressed as ethical concern. Listen to how it moves — 'Repeal this statute, my good sir? Repeal it, my dear sir? Never, with my consent. Alter this law, sir, and what will be the effect of your rash proceeding on a class of practitioners very worthily represented, allow me to say to you, by the opposite attorney in the case, Mr. Vholes?' The grammar performs the evasion. Every clause is hypothetical — 'what will be the effect' — but the feeling it produces is absolute certainty. Kenge never says the law is good. He says only that repealing it would harm Vholes — and Vholes has been pre-established as respectable. The argument is circular, but the grammar disguises the circle as a chain of reasoning. That is not an accidental stylistic choice — it is the moral anatomy of how bad institutions survive.

Dickens is not a radical about poverty — he is a conservative about it in the deepest Burkean sense. And the proof is a ratio: in the three Jo passages there are nine assertions of absence or negation to one assertion of moral presence. The method is accumulation of negation toward a single positive. Strip away name, grammar, family, school, faith, and testimony, and what remains — that one-ninth irreducible remainder — is the capacity for gratitude toward a specific remembered person. Dickens's claim is that this, and not rights or education or religion, is the irreducible minimum of the moral life.
Key points:
  • Dickens constructs a tripartite moral taxonomy of witnessing: Mrs. Piper testifies without knowing, Jo knows without being permitted to testify, and the narrator bears witness outside the courtroom altogether — posing the oldest question in practical ethics: what do we owe to what we see?

Tulkinghorn is not a villain who disrupts the aristocratic order — he is the person who makes the aristocratic order possible, managing the gap between the public performance of aristocratic dignity and the actual mess of human lives underneath it. Dickens's comic passages are not relief from the novel's moral argument — they ARE the novel's moral argument, stated with greater precision than the tragic passages. When Dickens is in tragic mode — Jo dying, Lady Dedlock freezing on the steps — the moral claim is presented through emotion, and emotion can mislead. When he is in comic mode, he is anatomising. We learn more about the moral condition of the aristocracy from the Gulliver simile than from anything Lady Dedlock does in a scene of pathos. We learn more about Chancery's destruction of human souls from the curtsying ward-woman's 'Day of Judgment' than from the fog-and-mud opening of the novel. The comedy is epistemologically superior as moral evidence.
Key points:
  • These four passages are not comic interludes but four distinct modes of moral failure: self-deception (c2:p12), institutional contempt for persons (c3:p159), jealousy distorting practical reason (c25:p58), and avarice without even the dignity of successful avarice (c62:p55). The laughter in each passage is directed at a specific vice, and the vices are not the same.
  • Dickens's comic passages are not relief from the novel's moral argument — they ARE the novel's moral argument, stated with greater precision than the tragic passages. When Dickens is in comic mode, he is anatomising; the laughter is the scalpel. The comedy is epistemologically superior as moral evidence.
  • The Gulliver simile in c2:p12 performs a double inversion of Swift's original moral: the aristocracy who believe themselves giants are revealed as Lilliputians, and the tradespeople who serve them are revealed as the giants who effortlessly command them. The ethical proposition: flattery requires a susceptible vanity in the recipient to complete the circuit — Lady Dedlock is complicit in her own deception.
  • The shadow sentence in c25:p58 uses syntactic repetition as moral argument: the phrase 'his own' circling obsessively enacts jealousy's psychology, and the Genesis allusion ('bone of his bone, flesh of his flesh, shadow of his shadow') converts the utterance of marital union into the sentence of marital surveillance — the structure of commitment persists while the virtue animating it has been replaced by its counterfeit.

There is nobody in this family who chooses not to sell the others.
Disagreement angles:
  • Against Oliver Trevelyan's likely framing of these passages as 'comic relief': the Burkean objection — laughter that does not arise from a clear perception of what is being laughed at is morally dangerous. To read the Gulliver allusion as charming whimsy is to miss that Dickens has made the comedy corrosive, not comforting. 'Oliver, you've used the word relief. I want to press on that. Relief from what, and for whom? Dickens has just told us that the people who serve Lady Dedlock understand her better than she understands herself. That is not a respite from the novel's darkness — that is the darkness, wearing a rather elegant comic coat.'
  • Against a purely historicist or structuralist reading of the Smallweed passage as economic satire: the virtue-ethics dimension is indispensable. The score for 'possesses operative virtue' in the Smallweed family is 0 out of 4 — and Mrs. Smallweed's exemption is not innocence but incompetence. No structural analysis captures this: it is a picture of total moral vacancy that MacIntyre's account of Enlightenment moral failure describes precisely.
  • On Mrs. Snagsby as 'comic grotesque': she must be distinguished precisely from Dickensian wickedness. She is Aristotelian akrasia — weakness of will, not kakia. Her jealousy has overwhelmed practical reason, but it is not unreasonable in origin given Snagsby's secretiveness. The comedy is the comedy of recognition; the moral assessment is pitiless. She is not funny — she is a warning.
