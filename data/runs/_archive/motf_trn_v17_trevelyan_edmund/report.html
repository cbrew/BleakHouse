Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver's delight is infectious and entirely warranted. But I want to hold onto something rather serious beneath the comedy — which is that Maggie's escapism is already a symptom of her deepest problem. She cannot endure reproach — she must flee from it rather than face it and discipline her will. Eliot is quite precise about this — and the passage on Maggie's education makes it plain. "Her thoughts generally were the oddest mixture of clear-eyed acumen and blind dreams." That, I think, is the novel's central diagnosis — announced early and confirmed throughout. The tragedy is not society's failure to accommodate Maggie — it is Maggie's failure to accommodate reality.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What I love about that early scene with Maggie and little Lucy is the sheer wickedness of Eliot's observation. She gives us a child in the grip of jealousy and then — with perfectly deadpan precision — she tells us what that jealousy is actually capable of producing. The narrator sets it up with such apparent tenderness before the knife goes in. "Maggie would have thought a little while ago that she could never be cross with pretty little Lucy, any more than she could be cruel to a little white mouse." The white mouse! A creature so small, so defenceless, so obviously safe from harm — and Eliot puts it there precisely to show us how far Maggie has already travelled from that safety. Because within two sentences Maggie is fantasising about slapping and pinching Lucy — not out of cruelty, but out of love for Tom, twisted by his indifference. That is devastating child psychology delivered in three sentences of comic prose.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And notice what Eliot is doing structurally here — the jealousy doesn't attach to Tom directly, because Tom is, as the text admits, of no use to slap. So it displaces onto Lucy, the nearest available body, the softest available target. Eliot is mapping the mechanics of resentment with almost clinical accuracy — and doing it through a nine-year-old girl. What this establishes, very early, is that Maggie's emotional life is constitutively relational. She cannot feel anything in isolation — every emotion is tangled with someone else, refracted through someone else's response to her. Tom, by contrast, is being constructed throughout this novel as a mind that seals itself off — that processes the world through rules and categories rather than through feeling. And the schoolroom chapters show us exactly how that happens.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to resist slightly the idea that Tom is simply sealed off or emotionally deficient. The schoolroom passage is in fact one of the most tender things in the novel, and it is tender precisely about Tom. Eliot tells us that under Mr. Stelling's rigorous and frankly unsuitable instruction, Tom became — and this is extraordinary — more like a girl than he had ever been in his life. His pride — which had previously rested quite comfortably on unquestioned local authority — suddenly found nothing but, as Eliot says, bruises and crushings. And what does this boy do with his humiliation? He prays. Privately, haltingly, improvising a petition for which he knows of no precedent. "And please to make me always remember my Latin." There is more genuine pathos in that one whispered clause than in pages of explicit suffering. Tom is not unfeeling. He is simply in a world — a world of classical education, of masculine expectation — for which his particular intelligence is entirely unsuited.

That is finely observed — and I think it points to something Eliot understands with great sorrow: that the renunciation doctrine is both genuinely consoling and genuinely insufficient for a mind like Maggie's. I think one must be careful not to reduce the irony here to a merely technical effect. Eliot's distance from Maggie in this passage is the distance of moral wisdom — the narrator sees the child whole, as the child cannot see herself. What is funny about Maggie giving away her last sixpence is not the act — the act is perfectly in keeping with her generous nature. That is not irony as a literary device so much as irony as a condition of human experience.
