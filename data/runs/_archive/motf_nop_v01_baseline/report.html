James Blackstone (social_historian)
Tulliver is doing something that a great many small landowners and millers in the early Victorian period did — he is using litigation as a form of self-assertion. The county courts weren't what they became after 1846 — before that reform, pursuing a grievance about water rights or a right of way meant expensive Chancery or common law proceedings. And Tulliver's particular grievance — the diversion of the river Ripple by a man named Pivart — is precisely the kind of dispute that could ruin a mill if left unchallenged. But here is the thing Eliot understands with devastating clarity: the law is not a neutral instrument in the hands of someone like Tulliver. He is a man of passionate conviction and limited capital, and those two qualities together are a prescription for ruin in any legal system, then or now. What's particularly sharp in Eliot is that Tulliver goes to law against a man — Wakem — whose son he has simultaneously chosen as his own son's schoolmaster. That is not comedy — that is the self-defeating logic of a proud man who cannot see how institutions connect.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That scene with Mrs. Tulliver and Wakem is one of the most historically precise moments in the entire novel, in my view. What she is doing, without knowing it, is revealing to the creditor's solicitor the full emotional and financial vulnerability of the family. In actual Victorian insolvency proceedings, the moment a creditor's agent understood there was genuine attachment to specific property, the value of that attachment became leverage. Wakem is not a simple villain — Eliot is careful about this — but he is a man who operates entirely within the instrumental logic of law and property. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That final encounter on the road — Tulliver horsewhipping Wakem — is Eliot staging a collision between two entirely different legal and moral orders. Wakem has the law absolutely on his side — the debt has been paid, the proceedings are closed, every formal obligation is settled. But Tulliver is operating on a completely different code — the code of personal honour, of injury and redress that predates the whole apparatus of civil litigation. And Eliot, characteristically, does not let us simply cheer for either position. Tulliver's violence is understandable, morally serious, and it kills him — not as punishment by the plot, but because his body cannot contain the force of what it has carried for so long.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to add something about the geography itself — because the flat, low-lying English Midlands are not incidental to this story. The Trent valley — and Eliot is clearly drawing on the landscape around Gainsborough in Lincolnshire — is flood country. Mill-owners on rivers like the Floss lived with seasonal inundation as a constant economic reality. The mill itself is a working industrial machine, not a picturesque ruin — it represents the Tulliver family's entire capital and social standing. So when the river threatens that mill, it isn't poetic — it is financial catastrophe. Eliot understands this double register — the symbolic and the brutally material — and she never lets us forget either one.
