James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the accusation is very specific — which is easy to miss if you don't know what you're standing in front of. When Dickens places his fog at the heart of Chancery, he is locating it at an institution that was, by the 1850s, genuinely notorious. The Court of Chancery handled equity cases — wills, trusts, inheritances — and some of those cases had been running for generations. There was a real case — Jennens v Jennens — that had been grinding through Chancery for over a century when Dickens was writing. The estate was worth millions; when the case finally concluded, the costs had consumed nearly everything. Jarndyce and Jarndyce was not a grotesque exaggeration — it was barely a heightening of what people already knew to be true. And Dickens says as much, in that magnificent deadpan — let me find the line — "Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. This scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means." James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it was a diagnosis that a great many of Dickens' contemporaries recognised as accurate, which is what gave the novel its immediate force. The Court of Chancery, by the 1850s, was a byword for institutional paralysis. Cases had been running for decades — some for generations. There was a real case, Jennens v. Jennens, that had been grinding through the courts for nearly a century, and is thought to be one of Dickens' inspirations for Jarndyce and Jarndyce. What Dickens understood, with that satirist's precision, is that Chancery wasn't merely slow — it was structurally incapable of resolution. The costs consumed the estate. That's not a flaw in the system — by the time Dickens was writing, it was effectively the system.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That contrast between Richard and Jo is the novel's great structural argument, and I don't think Dickens ever made it more sharply anywhere else in his work. Richard is destroyed by a system he was taught to trust. Jo is destroyed by a system that was never designed to notice him at all. And historically, that's precisely accurate. Chancery was a court of equity, which in theory existed to provide justice where common law failed. But equity required property. Without a stake in the contested estate, you simply didn't exist in Chancery's eyes. Dickens' genius is to show us the court's shadow falling on people who aren't even party to the suit — the Jellyby children, Gridley, poor mad Miss Flite with her caged birds.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Miss Flite — now there's a character I find both funny and unbearable in equal measure. She's attended the court every day for years, waiting for judgment. And her birds — she names them. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and this is where I think the novel rewards careful historical attention, because Dickens is making an argument that goes well beyond legal reform.
