Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Caroline is right to linger there, and I think what Eliot is doing is something very precise — she is mapping the exact moment when love becomes contingent. Maggie's feeling for Lucy was not conditional before — it simply existed, as children's affections do, without calculation. But the moment Tom prefers Lucy, Maggie cannot hold affection and injury in the same hand — and so she drops the affection. That is not a failure of character in the damning sense — it is simply what it is to be a passionate child, and, one suspects, to be a passionate person of any age. The novel earns its tragedy precisely because Eliot will not let Maggie be simply good — she must be human, which is rather harder.

I want to agree with both of you — and then add something that I think changes the picture. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It's one of the funniest passages in Victorian fiction — and I mean that seriously, because the comedy is doing real analytical work. Maggie's resolution, after Tom and Lucy have walked away and left her, is — and the narrator states this with perfect deadpan — not so simple as merely going home. "No! she would run away and go to the gypsies, and Tom should never see her any more." Now, why the gypsies? Because Maggie has been told, repeatedly, that she is like a gypsy — half wild — and she has absorbed that verdict and is trying to make it liveable. The family's contempt for her wildness becomes, in her imagination, the qualification for membership of a different community — one that will value exactly what the Tullivers penalise. That is a very precise image of what marginalisation does to a child's self-conception — it doesn't destroy identity, it reroutes it.

There is a passage early in the novel that I consider one of the most perfectly constructed sentences George Eliot ever wrote. It describes a moment of childhood violence — Maggie pushing her cousin Lucy into the mud — but the narrator refuses to let it be simply comic or simply shocking. She reaches for the language of Greek tragedy, and then — with exquisite timing — she deflates it. Listen to what she writes: "There were passions at war in Maggie at that moment to have made a tragedy, if tragedies were made by passion only; but the essential τι μέγεθος which was present in the passion was wanting to the action." Maggie possesses the interior of a tragic heroine and the reach of a seven-year-old.

I should push back on Daniel's characterisation, though I appreciate the diagnosis. Dr Kenn is not paralysed by a deficient social framework — he is paralysed precisely because he takes Maggie's conscience seriously. The novel's moral grammar rests on the idea that conscience is not negotiable — that you cannot simply weigh outcomes and arrive at the right answer. "Each immediate step was clogged with evil" — that is not the failure of an institution; it is the condition of life as George Eliot understood it. The flood, when it comes, is Eliot's mercy — the only resolution she could find that did not betray either Maggie's love or her renunciation.

When Maggie had left him, Dr Kenn stood ruminating with his hands behind him, and his eyes fixed on the carpet, under a painful sense of doubt and difficulty. The tone of Stephen’s letter, which he had read, and the actual relations of all the persons concerned, forced upon him powerfully the idea of an ultimate marriage between Stephen and Maggie as the least evil; and the impossibility of their proximity in St Ogg’s on any other supposition, until after years of separation, threw an insurmountable prospective difficulty over Maggie’s stay there. On the other hand, he entered with all the comprehension of a man who had known spiritual conflict, and lived through years of devoted service to his fellow-men, into that state of Maggie’s heart and conscience which made the consent to the marriage a desecration to her; her conscience must not be tampered with; the principle on which she had acted was a safer guide than any balancing of consequences. His experience told him that intervention was too dubious a responsibility to be lightly incurred; the possible issue either of an endeavor to restore the former relations with Lucy and Philip, or of counselling submission to this irruption of a new feeling, was hidden in a darkness all the more impenetrable because each immediate step was clogged with evil.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Edmund, that word — mercy — is exactly right, and I think it's also the most contested word in the novel's reception. Readers have argued for a hundred and sixty years whether the ending is earned or whether it is an evasion. But every time I come back to it, I think of Tom holding Maggie as she sobs — that convulsive arm — and then I think of the flood returning him to her. The flood doesn't resolve the moral problem. It dissolves it — into something older and more primary than judgement.

With these last words, Maggie’s sobs burst forth with the more violence for the previous struggle against them.
