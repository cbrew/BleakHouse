James Blackstone (social_historian)

Richard's Slow Unraveling: Ambition and Ruin (deep_dive)

Host (host)
Welcome back to Bleak House Unpacked — the show that takes Dickens seriously, which means taking him slowly. Today we're turning to one of the novel's most quietly devastating arcs: the unraveling of Richard Carstone. A young man of charm, of promise — consumed by the very suit that was supposed to secure his future. With me today are three remarkable readers of this novel, each of whom sees Richard's ruin through a very different lens. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don whose lifetime with Dickens has convinced him that the novel's deepest concern is moral character — tested, revealed, and sometimes broken. Professor James Blackstone is a legal historian who knows exactly what Chancery was, what it did to people, and why Dickens was not exaggerating. And Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads every institution in this novel — Chancery included — as a machine for protecting the powerful at the expense of the vulnerable. Edmund — you've spent decades with Richard Carstone. Where does his story begin to go wrong?
Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The question, I think, is whether it ever goes right in the first place. Richard is not a villain. He is something more troubling: a young man of genuine gifts who cannot apply them. Dickens shows us early on that he is charming, warm, well-liked — and entirely without fixity of purpose. What the Jarndyce suit does — and this is the novel's real psychological insight — is give his irresolution a cause to hide behind. He need not commit to medicine, to law, to soldiering — because all of it is temporary, pending the outcome of Jarndyce and Jarndyce. There is a passage I keep returning to — Esther watching Richard with that quiet, almost unbearable clarity she brings to everything. She sees that he has begun to look upon Jarndyce and Jarndyce as if it were not a millstone around his neck, but the key to every door. That inversion — a millstone mistaken for a key — is the whole tragedy compressed into one image. Burke would have called it a corruption of hope — and Dickens, I think, would not have disagreed.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund puts it beautifully, and I don't want to undercut that — but I do want to supply the institutional dimension. Because Chancery was not merely a literary device. It was a real and genuinely catastrophic system. By the mid-nineteenth century, suits in Chancery routinely ran for decades — some for generations. The costs were ruinous, the process opaque, the outcomes nearly random. Dickens had watched this. His own family had brushed against institutional debt and its consequences. He was not inventing a nightmare — he was transcribing one. Now — Esther's acceptance of Jarndyce touches something related, something I find quite affecting in the legal sense of the word. She frames it in the language of gratitude — of debt and repayment. Listen to how Dickens writes her reasoning. To devote my life to his happiness was to thank him poorly, and what had I wished for the other night but some new means of thanking him? That word — poorly. She is not saying it would be insufficient. She is saying it would still be inadequate, and she accepts that inadequacy. The contrast with Richard is almost unbearable. Esther converts obligation into devotion. Richard converts expectation into paralysis.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to push back on something — gently, but firmly. The framing of Richard as a moral failure, as a study in weak will — it's not wrong, exactly. But I think it lets the system off the hook. Chancery doesn't just give Richard's irresolution somewhere to hide. It actively produces that irresolution in people — that's what it does. Consider what the institution actually is: a machine that converts hope into dependency, that keeps people suspended indefinitely, waiting for a resolution that never comes. He is a young man placed inside a system designed — structurally — to make weakness the only rational response.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
There is a passage near the end of the novel — a small thing, almost a footnote — that I find myself returning to more than almost any other. Jo has been moved, shuffled, hounded, told to move on at every turn — and now a woman speaks of what he actually did for her. Dickens gives her the most devastating simplicity. "No, sir, no. Robbed me? He did nothing but what was kind-hearted by me, and that's the wonder of it." That's the wonder of it. The wonder — that a boy with nothing, a boy the law cannot even see, should have acted with such simple human grace. Dickens is doing something quite precise here: he is insisting that virtue is not a property of circumstance, not a reward for comfort. Jo possesses it in full — which is exactly what makes his destruction so unbearable to read.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Both of you are right, and the tension between you is exactly what makes Dickens so interesting historically. But I want to bring in a different kind of detail — the way Dickens renders the religious and charitable establishment that is supposed to fill the gap Jo falls through. There is the Chadband scene — and it is one of Dickens' most perfectly calibrated satirical set-pieces. Guster prepares the room with extraordinary care, because Chadband is coming to hold forth on spiritual matters. "There is excellent provision made of dainty new bread, crusty twists, cool fresh butter, thin slices of ham, tongue, and German sausage, and delicate little rows of anchovies nestling in parsley." And Dickens immediately delivers his verdict: "For Chadband is rather a consuming vessel — the persecutors say a gorging vessel — and can wield such weapons of the flesh as a knife and fork remarkably well." The joke is brilliant, but the point is savage: the institution of charity in this novel devours rather than distributes. Chadband and the telescopic philanthropists occupy the space that should belong to actual relief — and they fill it with themselves.
