Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about George Eliot's setting is how quietly the landscape does structural work. The Red Deeps — that ravine of fir trees where Maggie and Philip meet in secret — is not simply a convenient lovers' retreat. It's charged with everything the respectable surface of St Ogg's refuses to accommodate — desire, strangeness, the irregular. And Eliot introduces that place to us, brilliantly, through the most mundane social channel imaginable — a family dinner table. Aunt Pullet, quite obliviously, is the one who brings it up. She says, with magnificent innocence: "we never come along the road but he's a-scrambling out o' the trees and brambles at the Red Deeps." That verb — scrambling — does so much work. It makes Philip Wakem slightly feral, slightly uncanny — someone who inhabits the margins, literally and otherwise. And the comedy of it — being reported by Aunt Pullet, of all people — makes the menace sharper, not duller.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think that's exactly right, though I'd add something about what Eliot is doing with Philip Wakem's moral position in the landscape. Philip is a figure defined by exclusion — excluded by his deformity from the ordinary masculine world of commerce and respectability that St Ogg's prizes above all. Aunt Pullet's verdict — that he's very queer and lonely and may go out of his mind — is the town's verdict delivered in miniature. Society cannot account for a person who doesn't fit its categories, so it reaches for gothic explanations — madness, strangeness, a predilection for brambles. What I find morally interesting is that Eliot doesn't mock Philip — she mourns him. She shows us, at the novel's close, the wreckage left by the world's indifference to souls who don't conform.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That arc — from Aunt Pullet's comic gossip about a figure scrambling in brambles, to this spectral solitude — is one of the quiet structural triumphs of the novel. Eliot plants the Red Deeps early, casually, almost as a joke — and then she charges it, over hundreds of pages, until it holds almost unbearable weight. That's what I mean by structural patience — the willingness to let a setting accumulate meaning rather than announce it. And it's inseparable from the moral argument, because the Red Deeps is exactly what St Ogg's cannot see — the inner life, hidden, flourishing in secret, and finally swallowed by the world.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Miss Woodcourt's phrase about being entirely himself in that place resonates, because Eliot is acutely aware that the respectable world punishes selfhood that doesn't conform to type. She captures that mercilessness most brilliantly — and most bitterly — in her portrait of St Ogg's public opinion after Maggie's return. There is a passage of withering irony — I think it's among the finest satirical writing in the Victorian novel — that deserves quoting at some length. "We judge others according to results; how else? — not knowing the process by which results are arrived at." That is a sentence that ought to stop any reader cold. Eliot does not rage — she simply makes the logic explicit, and the logic is damning enough. The world judges by outcomes because it refuses — or cannot be bothered — to imagine the inner process. And Maggie, of course, is destroyed precisely because she chose the inner process over the comfortable outcome.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And what makes that passage almost unbearable to read is the voice Eliot adopts — that gossipy, collective, feminine voice. She calls it, quite precisely, public opinion of the feminine gender — not the world, but the world's wife. And then she performs that voice — the whole cascade of rationalisations. "a deformed young man, you know! — and young Guest so very fascinating; and, they say, he positively worships her." There it is — Philip reduced to a parenthesis, a disqualification. And Maggie's genuine moral struggle becomes mere gullibility in the face of a very charming man. It's one of the cruelest passages in Victorian fiction — and one of the funniest, which makes it even more cruel.

Maggie's Appetite: Intellect, Yearning, and the Female Mind (deep_dive)

Host (host)
Welcome to The Mill on the Floss Unpacked, a close and passionate reading of George Eliot's great novel of provincial life, childhood, and the hunger of an exceptional mind. And Ms. Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who has read this novel many times over — someone who knows exactly which sentences make you stop, turn back, and read them again. Let's begin with Maggie as a child — not the tragic Maggie of the novel's final movement, but the eight-year-old Maggie: ferociously curious, magnificently ill-equipped, and already utterly herself.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to the early Maggie passages is how Eliot uses comedy to protect her — and us — from sentimentality. She doesn't present this child as a little genius to be admired and pitied in equal measure. She shows us a mind that is genuinely odd — patchwork, brilliant in places, bewilderingly blind in others. There's that wonderful passage about her self-taught education — the dictionary reading, the deductions about Greek prefixes. "in travelling over her small mind you would have found the most unexpected ignorance as well as unexpected knowledge." That phrase — 'her small mind' — is doing something quite audacious. It sounds almost dismissive, and then the sentence immediately undermines that dismissal by revealing the scope of what lives inside that smallness. Eliot is teaching us, from the very first chapters, to mistrust the categories we bring to female intelligence.

What I can never quite get over is the gypsy scene — because it shows us something Edmund is right about, but also something more. Maggie's mind doesn't just dream — it escalates. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)

One might add that the linen passage is also a perfect illustration of how Eliot refuses to sentimentalise the Tullivers.
