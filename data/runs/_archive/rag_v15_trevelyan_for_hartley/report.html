Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
From the inside, it feels like being handed the keys to a house with forty rooms and being told — go on, explore. Except half the rooms are locked, and the key-holder is a man in a rusty black coat who gives absolutely nothing away. And that, really, is Tulkinghorn — who is perhaps the most sinister figure in a novel that is absolutely full of sinister figures. Dickens describes his arrival in Chapter Twenty-Seven with this extraordinary catalogue of negatives. "In manner, close and dry. In voice, husky and low. In face, watchful behind a blind." It's a portrait built entirely from what he withholds — manner, voice, face, all closed off, all armoured. And then Dickens can't resist the ironic sting at the end. "The peerage may have warmer worshippers and faithfuller believers than Mr. Tulkinghorn, after all, if everything were known." "If everything were known" — that conditional clause is doing the work of an entire chapter. Dickens is telling us: this man has secrets, and they will destroy someone.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Oliver has put his finger on is something I find historically fascinating — the way Chancery lawyers of this period cultivated opacity as a professional virtue. Tulkinghorn is not a caricature, or not only a caricature — he is a recognisable institutional type. The great solicitors of the Victorian era held their clients' secrets the way banks hold money — as a form of power over them. And Dickens understood that perfectly — which is why that portrait passage works so well as sociology as well as satire. There's a later moment, in Chapter Thirty-Nine, where Tulkinghorn examines a portrait of Lady Dedlock — and the lines are devastating. "Who is this? 'Lady Dedlock.' Ha! A very good likeness in its way, but it wants force of character." He is, ostensibly, commenting on a painting — but of course he means the woman, and she knows it, and he knows she knows it. That is the fog in its most refined and vicious form — cruelty delivered with perfect social deniability.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me, reading these passages again, is how much Dickens communicates through pure syntax — not what his characters say, but how they say it, or refuse to. Compare Tulkinghorn with someone like Richard Carstone — who is warm, impulsive, almost desperately open. There's a line where Richard sees Woodcourt unexpectedly, and his delight simply bursts out of him. "Woodcourt, my dear fellow — you come upon my vision like a ghost." It's comic and tender at once — and it's everything Tulkinghorn could never be. Richard opens himself completely — and that openness, that refusal to be guarded, is precisely what will undo him. So the fog doesn't only conceal the secrets of the powerful — it also fails to protect the vulnerable. And that, I think, is the true cruelty of Bleak House as a world — transparency is not safety.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That Krook scene always reminds me of how deliberately Dickens structured the novel's epistemology — who knows what, and when, and whether knowing is the same as understanding. Chancery, as an institution, was predicated on the idea that information would eventually resolve into justice — given enough time, enough paper, enough procedure. The reality, of course, was precisely the opposite — the accumulation of information became the obstruction of any possible outcome. Dickens understood this viscerally — which is why the fog is not merely atmospheric scene-setting, but the novel's central argument. The fog is the system, and the system is the fog.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me, from a historian's perspective, is precisely who those visitors are and what their presence implies. The only people who come to nurse Jo are those whom the official system has also failed, marginalized, or made invisible in one way or another. Phil Squod is a crippled man, a former street performer. George is a soldier who's been caught up in a legal trap of his own. Even Jarndyce himself is a man defined by his refusal to trust the very legal system that was supposed to resolve his inheritance. What Dickens is showing us — without stating it — is that care in this novel flows along the channels the state has abandoned. Compassion occupies the vacuum that law and institution have left behind.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Skimpole dynamic is important. But I want to push us toward the institutional version of the same maneuver — because Dickens stages it with astonishing precision. The Kenge passage — the mock parliamentary inquiry into whether Chancery practice should be reformed — is one of the most devastating pieces of satire in the novel. The entire machinery of respectable legal opinion is mobilized to protect one man's livelihood — Mr. Vholes — while the people being destroyed by the system are called "a little hard" as a concession. Here is Kenge, in full flight, addressing a client who has the impertinence to suggest that the law might be changed. "Sir, that class of practitioners would be swept from the face of the earth. Now you cannot afford — I will say, the social system cannot afford — to lose an order of men like Mr. Vholes." Notice the pivot: from "you cannot afford" to "the social system cannot afford." The individual complaint is seamlessly absorbed into an argument about the preservation of an entire class of men. And then the Parliamentary inquiry is killed — for ten years — by a single answer: that Vholes is considered, in the profession, a most respectable man. That is not satire in the sense of exaggeration. That is a precise description of how reform actually fails.

The irony runs even deeper when you consider what Chancery actually was in 1852. It was a court of equity — supposedly the court of conscience, designed to do justice where the common law could not reach. And yet by Dickens' time it had become so clogged with inherited cases, contested estates, and procedural delay that suitors routinely died before their cases concluded. The short answer is: almost all of it. Chancery by the 1850s was a system so encrusted with delay and fees that suits routinely outlasted the litigants who brought them — sometimes by generations. Some say she really is, or was, a party to a suit, but no one knows for certain because no one cares. She carries some small litter in a reticule which she calls her documents, principally consisting of paper matches and dry lavender. A sallow prisoner has come up, in custody, for the half-dozenth time to make a personal application “to purge himself of his contempt,” which, being a solitary surviving executor who has fallen into a state of conglomeration about accounts of which it is not pretended that he had ever any knowledge, he is not at all likely ever to do. I want to bring in the Skimpole passage here, because I think it's the most uncomfortable comedy in the novel. Skimpole takes the injustice of Chancery — Richard's destruction — and aestheticises it. He turns it into pastoral poetry, with Richard as a shepherd and Chancery as a kind of enchanted wood. And the speech is funny — Skimpole is always funny — but it is also a precise anatomy of a certain kind of complicity. The person who admits a system is abusive but finds it personally agreeable and therefore does not act. "My growling friend, I DON'T defend them, but they are very agreeable to me." The capitals on DON'T are Dickens' own — and they are doing exactly what bold type does. Skimpole wants credit for the admission while avoiding any obligation it might create.

I want to linger on that word 'low boy' for a moment — because it's easily passed over, and it shouldn't be. And Dickens published this in monthly parts between 1852 and 1853 — the Chancery Reform Act came in 1852, mid-serialisation. So his readers were watching the novel and watching Parliament simultaneously. The question was whether the reality would catch up with the satire. Chancery was eventually reformed. The specific abuses Dickens describes were, over decades, substantially addressed. But the deeper argument — about institutions that calcify, that consume the people they were designed to serve — that argument has never gone out of date.
