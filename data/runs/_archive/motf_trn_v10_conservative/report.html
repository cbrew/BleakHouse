And notice what Eliot does structurally with that comedy. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I should say, though, that one risks misreading these early chapters if one takes the satire as Eliot's final word on Maggie's formation. The comedy is real, but it serves a moral purpose — Eliot is showing us a soul educating itself in the only materials available to it. That is, frankly, rather magnificent — whatever one thinks of the outcomes. The passage about the gypsies that follows is a masterclass in how self-constructed imaginative worlds can turn on their creator. Eliot shows us Maggie's mind moving, in five minutes, from romantic adventurer to potential victim of cannibalism — and then to theologian. She sets up the passage, if I may — Eliot writes that Maggie had begun to think that the gypsies meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking. Note that phrase — 'gradual cooking.' It is a nine-year-old's precision applied to her own imagined demise. And then — with a keenness of interest, Eliot says, 'that no theologian could have exceeded' — she wonders whether the Devil would know her thoughts. Character, not circumstance — that is what Eliot is building here.

And that quality carries all the way through to the devastating passages later — even the elder Tulliver's breakdown has it. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And the moral seriousness doesn't let up even in the passages where Eliot steps back and gives us a character reflecting rather than acting. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to enter a gentle caveat about reading these passages primarily as craft demonstrations. What Eliot is showing us here is something morally precise — the way a child's will, frustrated, does not submit but finds a grander theatre for itself. The gypsy scheme is not merely charming child's fantasy. It is a foreshadowing of every subsequent escape Maggie will attempt — and each one will be equally, perhaps more disastrously, self-deluding. She imagines the gypsies will receive her with respect on account of her superior knowledge — a detail Eliot gives us with beautiful, pitiless irony. Maggie's tragedy is not that the world is unkind. It is that she perpetually constructs a more magnificent version of herself than circumstances can sustain.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
This is, for me, the most structurally daring thing Eliot does in this novel. She begins in close third person — we're behind Maggie's eyes, feeling the bitterness of those tears. Then, without warning, the narrator pivots and speaks to us — to all of us who have lived past this moment. "There is no hopelessness so sad as that of early youth, when the soul is made up of wants, and has no long memories, no superadded life in the life of others." And then — this is the extraordinary turn — she implicates us. "Though we who looked on think lightly of such premature despair, as if our vision of the future lightened the blind sufferer's present." The narrating voice is not simply observing Maggie — it is correcting us for observing her too comfortably. That shift — from inside the character to outside the reader — is a technical masterstroke.

Mr Glegg is, I think, one of Eliot's finest minor achievements — and it is worth pausing on what makes him so. He is not a villain, not a fool, and not a saint. What strikes me as a craftsperson is how Eliot builds that portrait through an extended culinary metaphor — and then pays it off. Chapter 33
Maggie shook her head slowly, and was silent, under conflicting thoughts. It seemed to her inclination, that to see Philip now and then, and keep up the bond of friendship with him, was something not only innocent, but good; perhaps she might really help him to find contentment as she had found it. The voice that said this made sweet music to Maggie; but athwart it there came an urgent, monotonous warning from another voice which she had been learning to obey,—the warning that such interviews implied secrecy; implied doing something she would dread to be discovered in, something that, if discovered, must cause anger and pain; and that the admission of anything so near doubleness would act as a spiritual blight. Yet the music would swell out again, like chimes borne onward by a recurrent breeze, persuading her that the wrong lay all in the faults and weaknesses of others, and that there was such a thing as futile sacrifice for one to the injury of another. It was very cruel for Philip that he should be shrunk from, because of an unjustifiable vindictiveness toward his father,—poor Philip, whom some people would shrink from only because he was deformed. The idea that he might become her lover or that her meeting him could cause disapproval in that light, had not occurred to her; and Philip saw the absence of this idea clearly enough, saw it with a certain pang, although it made her consent to his request the less unlikely. There was bitterness to him in the perception that Maggie was almost as frank and unconstrained toward him as when she was a child.

Philip is right, of course — and yet one must not too hastily applaud him. That's the thing I keep coming back to on every re-reading — the sheer scale of what Eliot sees in ordinary people. She says outright that Maggie had passions at war sufficient to make a tragedy — and she means it. The cow-trodden mud is funny, and Eliot intends us to laugh — but the laugh catches in your throat.
