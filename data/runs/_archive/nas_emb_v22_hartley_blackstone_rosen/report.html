James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me about that passage, from a historical vantage point, is the sheer accuracy of the geography of feeling it describes. In the mid-nineteenth century, the cultural distance between Hampshire and industrial Lancashire was not merely sentimental — it was structural, almost absolute. A southern clergyman's daughter moving to Milton would have experienced something close to what we might now call culture shock — except without any framework to name it. Gaskell understood that the North was not simply different in degree — it was different in kind. And the fog that Margaret encounters in her bedroom shortly after arrival is Gaskell being almost documentary in her precision. Industrial Manchester — the model for Milton — was genuinely one of the most polluted environments in the world at that moment. So when Gaskell writes of the window that "it loomed through the fog like a great barrier to hope," she is doing something more than gothic atmospheric writing — she is recording a material reality.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold that image a little longer, though — because there's something uncomfortable lodged in that bedroom scene that I think we should name. Margaret's suffering in that fog-shrouded room is real, and Gaskell renders it with great sympathy. But she is sitting on a box, surrounded by her family's possessions, in a rented house — she has arrived in Milton as a voluntary exile. The workers whose labour fills the air with that very fog have no equivalent escape into Edith's letter from Corfu. And Gaskell is completely aware of this — she doesn't let us forget it. Later in the same chapter of the novel, we get Margaret in the mill yard — and the atmosphere shifts entirely. The industrial machinery is silent, and something far more threatening is gathering: "There was no near sound,—no steam engine at work with beat and pant,—no click of machinery, no mingling and clashing of many sharp voices;" "but far away, the ominous gathering roar, deep-clamouring." The silence of the machines is not peace — it is the silence before collective action. Gaskell is recording the sound of a strike — and the grammar of that sentence, all those negatives, enacts the sudden absence of industrial routine.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That passage is historically extraordinary in its precision — Gaskell understood exactly what the cessation of mill noise meant in a town like Manchester. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And yet — and this matters enormously — by the time we reach the vigil passage near Margaret's mother's sickbed, those categories have dissolved. The strike, Boucher's suffering, the workers' lives — all of it becomes, in Margaret's grief, what Gaskell calls 'like a dreaming memory of some former life.' "Everything that had passed out of doors seemed dissevered from her mother, and therefore unreal." That word 'dissevered' is doing something ethically complex. Grief is being used — I don't say this harshly — to suspend Margaret's moral attention to the people she has been learning to see. Gaskell then reaches for one of the most extraordinary images in the novel to describe this dissolution of time and meaning: "It was as if from some aërial belfry, high up above the stir and jar of the earth, there was a bell continually tolling," "'All are shadows!—all are passing!—all is past!'" The belfry is aerial — above the stir and jar — above, that is, the very industrial world Gaskell has spent so much effort making us inhabit.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The repetition of that tolling bell is extraordinary as a formal device — 'All are shadows!—all are passing!—all is past!' It's anaphora — the same grammatical structure struck three times — and the slight variation in the third clause, 'is past' rather than 'are passing,' performs the very exhaustion it describes. We move from plural present to singular perfect tense — from 'all are' to 'all is' — and in that grammatical shift, time itself contracts. Gaskell was writing under serialisation pressure — and yet she produced a sentence of that precision.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me historically about that passage is how precisely it encodes the self-made man's double bind. To explain himself, Thornton would have to disclose poverty — real poverty, the kind that carried stigma in mid-Victorian commercial culture. The origin story of a man who clawed his way up was not straightforwardly admirable in polite society — it was also a reminder of what he had been. She is, without knowing it, enacting the very class hierarchy that made men like Thornton permanently uncomfortable in drawing rooms.

What she gets right, remarkably right, is the epistemological problem at the heart of collective action. The workers don't fail because they are weak or cowardly — they fail because their model of the world is wrong. Gaskell is almost clinical about it. She writes that the workers' calculations were based, like too many of the masters', on false premises. Both sides in this dispute treat human beings as predictable units, and both sides are punished for it. The Irish workers are the shock that the model couldn't absorb — and Boucher is the human passion the Union forgot to account for.
