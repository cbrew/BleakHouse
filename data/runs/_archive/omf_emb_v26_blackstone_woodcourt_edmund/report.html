Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
One is grateful, as always, for a text that does the work of explaining itself — though Dickens, typically, takes his time arriving at the explanation. What strikes me most forcefully about this passage is its understanding of moral drift. John Harmon does not choose villainy. He lapses into his condition — and Dickens is precise about this — "as many a man lapses into many a condition, without perceiving the accumulative power of its separate circumstances." That word 'accumulative' is doing enormous work. It is not a single fall; it is a series of small concessions, each one making the next seem reasonable. This is very much a conservative moral psychology, I would suggest — an understanding that the self is not mastered by grand gestures of virtue, but imperilled by gradual, barely-noticed yielding. And behind it all, of course, is the father's money — that corrosive inheritance which Dickens identifies, quite brilliantly, as the root cause. The deception was conceived, Dickens tells us, in "the distrust engendered by his wretched childhood and the action for evil — never yet for good within his knowledge then — of his father and his father's wealth on all within their influence." That parenthetical — 'never yet for good within his knowledge then' — is among the most quietly devastating things in the novel.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
It makes a great deal of sense, and what's remarkable is how carefully Dickens has thought through the legal consequences, even if he doesn't parade them. A man presumed dead by the police, plastered across London walls as 'wanted' under the name Julius Handford, effectively has no legal identity at all. He cannot inherit, cannot testify, cannot contract. Dickens captures this with that marvellous triple identity — the oakum-headed sailor, Rokesmith the secretary, Julius Handford the wanted man — all being, as the text says, "as like that same lost wanted Mr Julius Handford, as never man was like another in this world." That repetition — 'as never man was like another in this world' — applied first to the sailor becoming Handford, then immediately to Rokesmith also becoming Handford — is Dickens showing us the legal trap through the form of the sentence itself. They are all the same man, and yet none of them can legally be that man. There is something genuinely chilling about the institutional thoroughness of his erasure — and Dickens understood that perfectly.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I would say more than 'almost.' There is something deeply — one might almost say liturgically — formal about this moment of self-reclamation. Harmon calls his own name three times, like a priest calling a soul back to the body: "This is John Harmon drowning! John Harmon, struggle for your life. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I don't want to puncture Edmund's theological reading, because it's a good one — but I'd add the social dimension. What Harmon resurfaces into is not merely life — it's a particular kind of social death-in-life. The Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 is the institutional backdrop to everything Dickens writes about poverty in this novel. Its principle was deterrence — relief was made so deliberately unpleasant that only the truly destitute would seek it. The workhouse test — you must enter the workhouse to receive any help — was designed to humiliate. And Dickens understood exactly what this produced — a system so degrading that the most self-respecting of the poor would rather die than submit to it.
