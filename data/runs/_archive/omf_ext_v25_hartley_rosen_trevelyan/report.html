Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes you first about this novel — and I mean the very first pages — is the smell. Not metaphorically. You are on the river, at night, in a boat, and something dead is being hauled toward you. Dickens puts you there in his first paragraph, and he never quite lets you leave. And then there's the moment — partway through the novel — when we learn whose body it actually was in the river. John Harmon, reading a placard at Whitehall, discovers that the corpse pulled from the Thames was dressed in his clothes — but it wasn't him. Dickens writes it like this: "I perceived that Radfoot had been murdered by some unknown hands for the money for which he would have murdered me, and that probably we had both been shot into the river from the same dark place into the same dark tide." Same dark place. Same dark tide. The symmetry of it is almost musical. The river doesn't distinguish between the murderer and his intended victim. It takes them both.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That repetition — 'same dark place, same dark tide' — is doing something very precise structurally. Dickens is writing a novel in which identity keeps dissolving and reforming — people are constantly being mistaken for other people, or hiding who they are. And the river is his great metaphor for that — it takes everything in, it obscures, it equalises. But what I find remarkable is how the novel insists that good acts have a permanence that evil simply doesn't. There's a passage — the narrator speaking about the Boffins — that Dickens almost seems to have written as the novel's moral theorem. He writes: "For, Evil often stops short at itself and dies with the doer of it; but Good, never." Eight words after that semicolon — 'but Good, never' — and somehow the whole novel is sitting inside them.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't want to quarrel with the beauty of that line — it is beautiful. But I think we need to sit with what the novel is actually showing us before we accept that formulation too comfortably. The Boffins are good people — genuinely, I think Dickens means it. But the narrator tells us that the 'hard wrathful and sordid nature' that wrung work from them for as little money as possible — still respected their moral straightness. That is a precise description of exploitation. The exploiter respects the virtue of the people he exploits — and goes right on exploiting them. Dickens sees this clearly. I'm not sure the novel fully reckons with what it means.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and what Dickens does in that passage is almost unbearably precise. We're ostensibly hearing Boffin reminisce, but Dickens is constructing a kind of origin myth for the entire novel's moral architecture. Notice the structural doubling: the child's scanty clothes, the scanty box — Dickens doesn't say 'poor' once. He just repeats that one adjective, and the repetition does the work of an entire paragraph of social commentary. Then there's this extraordinary sentence where Mrs Boffin kneels to warm her hands and rubs the child's cheeks. That outburst — 'the wide wide world' — is operatic, but Dickens earns it completely. Mrs Boffin is performing love in a world that treats this child as a line in a ledger, and the repetition in 'wide wide' breaks open the prose into something almost lyrical.

What strikes me, returning to the novel whole, is how structurally brilliant Dickens's satirical machinery really is. And what Dickens understands — better than almost any Victorian novelist — is that the performance of identity and the performance of virtue are the same mechanism. There was an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James’s, when not in use, to whom the Veneerings were a source of blind confusion. The name of this article was Twemlow. Being first cousin to Lord Snigsworth, he was in frequent requisition, and at many houses might be said to represent the dining-table in its normal state. Mr and Mrs Veneering, for example, arranging a dinner, habitually started with Twemlow, and then put leaves in him, or added guests to him. Sometimes, the table consisted of Twemlow and half a dozen leaves; sometimes, of Twemlow and a dozen leaves; sometimes, Twemlow was pulled out to his utmost extent of twenty leaves. Mr and Mrs Veneering on occasions of ceremony faced each other in the centre of the board, and thus the parallel still held; for, it always happened that the more Twemlow was pulled out, the further he found himself from the center, and nearer to the sideboard at one end of the room, or the window-curtains at the other.
