Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who sees Bleak House as a precise anatomy of class power — and who will not let us be comfortable about what the novel reveals. And Oliver Trevelyan — actor, writer, and the voice of more Dickens audiobooks than any living person — brings us the novel as it was always meant to be heard: performed, relished, alive. So — the fog. The novel opens with one of the most famous paragraphs in all of English fiction, and Dickens gives us not a character, not a plot — but weather. Eleanor — what is Dickens doing with that fog, and why does it work so brilliantly as an opening move?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to that opening is that Dickens makes the fog do double and triple duty simultaneously. On the surface it is pure atmosphere — Victorian London as sensation, as physical experience pressing in on you. But the fog is also Chancery — it is the law itself, spreading everywhere, obscuring everything, touching every life in the novel whether those lives know it or not. And then there is a third layer — a narratorial layer — because we are given this panorama by a narrator who is not yet named, not yet embodied. We don't yet know whose eyes we are looking through, and that uncertainty is itself a kind of fog. And then the dual narration arrives — Esther's first-person voice stepping in — and the two narratives never fully merge. That structural tension — the omniscient third person and Esther's intimate, sometimes unreliable first person — is the formal equivalent of the fog. You are never quite certain, in this novel, who sees clearly and who does not.
Host (host)
Daniel — you would add a political dimension to that fog, I suspect.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Absolutely — and I think it is inseparable from what Eleanor has identified as the structural dimension. The fog is ideological in the precise sense: it is the condition that makes the system invisible to the people it devours. Chancery doesn't need to announce itself as predatory — the fog does that work by making its operations seem as natural as weather. And what Dickens understood — more clearly than almost any novelist of his era — is that the people most harmed by the system are the people least able to see how it works. Think of Miss Flite — she has been inside Chancery so long she's become part of its décor, part of its performance. And yet she is also the one character who sees the truth of what Chancery does to new arrivals — sees it precisely because she has been destroyed by it. There is this extraordinary moment where she warns Esther about Richard, and Dickens gives her the clearest diagnosis in the whole novel. "I saw them beginning in our friend the ward in Jarndyce. Let some one hold him back. Or he'll be drawn to ruin." Three sentences. That is all. And it contains everything Dickens knows about what Chancery does to human beings.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What gets me about that passage — every time I read it, whether aloud or on the page — is the rhythm of those three short sentences. Dickens has been giving Miss Flite this wonderfully fractured, rambling manner throughout the novel — all those little asides and honorifics and digressions. And then she clears. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And this is where I think the architecture of the novel is genuinely audacious — because Dickens is running two things simultaneously the whole time. There is the grave, indicting voice of the third-person narrator who opens the novel — cold, ironic, furious — and then there is Esther. Esther who is warm, and modest, and who consistently notices when people are suffering in ways she cannot quite name or explain. And what I love is that Dickens uses this contrast to dramatise the very limits of each mode of seeing. The omniscient narrator can condemn Chancery — but cannot hold Charley's hand. Esther can hold hands — but she cannot quite see the system that is tightening around the people she loves.

Skimpole is one of those characters where reading him aloud for the first time you almost stop and put the book down — in delight. "You will excuse me; I really cannot attend to the shop! Every word is perfectly placed. The apology that is not an apology. The self-portrait as helpless aesthete. And the crucial little sting in the tail — 'somebody who doesn't want to look about him' — as if the working person's failing is a mere deficiency of imagination. Dickens is doing something very dangerous here — making a morally catastrophic position utterly, irresistibly funny.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that's precisely why Skimpole is so important to the novel's argument — because he is not just a comic turn. He is the ideology of the ruling class made flesh and given a witty tongue. This idea that the cultivated, feeling person is simply too refined for the grubby business of earning — that someone else must do the unpleasant work of sustaining them — is not an eccentricity. It is the operating principle of the class Dickens is describing. And the people who pay for it — and Dickens is careful to show us this — are always the Jos, always the people at the very bottom. Skimpole's charm is the novel's most sophisticated trap — and Esther almost falls into it, which is deeply uncomfortable to read.

What I find structurally brilliant is that Dickens places Skimpole and Miss Flite almost as a kind of bracket. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And notice what the paragraph in the newspapers amounts to — a paragraph. The collapse of inhabited houses is so routine in Tom-all-Alone's that it generates a single unit of copy and then disappears. The slum is the physical deposit of the suit — every year of legal delay is another year the houses are not repaired, not demolished, not redeemed. The law does not merely fail to help the poor — it is the active mechanism of their destruction.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I keep returning to is what Dickens does with that image of the gaps — the spaces left by collapsed houses becoming lodgings. That is the novel's central structural logic made physical: Chancery creates absences, and people are forced to live inside them. And what Dickens does with his narrators is exactly parallel. Esther's narration is full of what she does not quite say — of perceptions she registers and then politely retreats from. There is a moment with Charley Neckett — a child minding younger children after their father's death — that is structurally extraordinary.
