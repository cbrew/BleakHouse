James Blackstone (social_historian)
That switch in narrative voice also maps, I think, onto a social distinction that is crucial to the novel's architecture. The omniscient narrator describes the fog from above — a god's-eye view of the city as system. Esther experiences it from the inside — she is a person to whom things happen, not an observer who comprehends the whole. And that distinction — between systemic view and individual experience — is precisely the tension Dickens is dramatising when he introduces the case of Jarndyce and Jarndyce. He describes it with magnificent savagery — the case has been going on so long that no one alive can remember its origin: "Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. This scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means." Verified (100%) A scarecrow of a suit — that is not metaphor employed for decorative effect. That is the carcass of something that was once alive, now preserved only to frighten off anyone who might come too close.
