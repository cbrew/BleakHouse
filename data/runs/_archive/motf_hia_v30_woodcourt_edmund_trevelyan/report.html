Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I come back to these childhood chapters is how unsentimentally Eliot holds the comedy and the pain together. Take the moment when Maggie resolves to run away to the gypsies after Tom rejects her company. "No! she would run away and go to the gypsies, and Tom should never see her any more." It's absurd, of course — she is nine years old and she is running away to a commons she's never visited. But the feeling inside that absurdity is entirely real. Eliot never condescends to Maggie's misery just because it has a child's scale. That, I think, is what makes the opening of this novel unlike any other Victorian childhood in fiction.

And what a sentence to have to read aloud. "No!" — that single word, that exclamation mark — it's a stage direction, really. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I would add only that the comedy is inseparable from something more serious — a question about character that runs through the whole novel. Maggie's intentions, we are told at the outset of this passage, "were on a larger scale than Tom imagined." That is not merely comic observation — it is the defining tension of Maggie's entire existence. She is a soul whose appetite for feeling and significance exceeds every container her world offers her. Eliot states the tragedy in the very first comedic flourish — and that is the mark of a genuine moral novelist.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Ah — Tom praying for Latin. This is one of the finest things in the book, I think. The setup is rich: Tom has broken down in the supines of the third conjugation for the fifth time. Mr Stelling has lectured him gravely about seizing the "present golden opportunity of learning supines." And Tom — desperate, humiliated, and utterly without resources — turns to prayer. But here is the detail that makes it art rather than merely pathos: "and please to make me always remember my Latin." And then — the comedy returns — he pauses to consider how to pray about Euclid. Eliot holds those two things — the genuine piety and the utter bathos — perfectly level.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Caroline is quite right to fasten on that detail. There is a passage shortly afterwards — in a quite different key — that tests this sibling love with sudden violence. Tom has been showing off with Mr Stelling's sword, Maggie watching from the bed. The sword falls. Tom faints. Maggie believes him dead. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
We begin, I think, with the Latin Grammar. It is one of those passages that tells you, in miniature, everything about how this child's mind works. George Eliot writes that Maggie skips the rules in the Syntax — because the examples have become too absorbing. "These mysterious sentences, snatched from an unknown context, — like strange horns of beasts, and leaves of unknown plants, brought from some far-off region, — gave boundless scope to her imagination." Boundless scope. Maggie does not want to master Latin — she wants to be transported by it. She is drawn not to the complete and the systematic but to the fragment — because the fragment demands imagination to complete it. That is a portrait of a particular kind of mind — and a warning about what happens when such a mind finds no proper object.

The breath of the young, unwearied day, the delicious rhythmic dip of the oars, the fragmentary song of a passing bird heard now and then, as if it were only the overflowing of brimful gladness, the sweet solitude of a twofold consciousness that was mingled into one by that grave, untiring gaze which need not be averted,—what else could there be in their minds for the first hour? Some low, subdued, languid exclamation of love came from Stephen from time to time, as he went on rowing idly, half automatically; otherwise they spoke no word; for what could words have been but an inlet to thought? and thought did not belong to that enchanted haze in which they were enveloped,—it belonged to the past and the future that lay outside the haze. Maggie was only dimly conscious of the banks, as they passed them, and dwelt with no recognition on the villages; she knew there were several to be passed before they reached Luckreth, where they always stopped and left the boat. At all times she was so liable to fits of absence, that she was likely enough to let her waymarks pass unnoticed.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The reckoning, yes — and that is where the third passage comes in, and where, I think, Eliot shows us her full moral seriousness. Maggie wakes. The dream dissolves. And she is left with, as Eliot puts it, her own memory and her own dread. The sentence I keep returning to is spare, declarative, almost unbearably direct. "she had brought sorrow into the lives of others — into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love." "Knit up" — it's a domestic word, a homely word. And it carries all the weight of the novel's vision of human connectedness. We are not islands. We are woven into one another. And to tear that weaving is not merely regrettable — it is the deepest wrong Eliot can imagine.

She had fallen asleep before nine, and had been sleeping for six hours before the faintest hint of a midsummer daybreak was discernible. She awoke from that vivid dreaming which makes the margin of our deeper rest. She was in a boat on the wide water with Stephen, and in the gathering darkness something like a star appeared, that grew and grew till they saw it was the Virgin seated in St Ogg’s boat, and it came nearer and nearer, till they saw the Virgin was Lucy and the boatman was Philip,—no, not Philip, but her brother, who rowed past without looking at her; and she rose to stretch out her arms and call to him, and their own boat turned over with the movement, and they began to sink, till with one spasm of dread she seemed to awake, and find she was a child again in the parlour at evening twilight, and Tom was not really angry. From the soothed sense of that false waking she passed to the real waking,—to the plash of water against the vessel, and the sound of a footstep on the deck, and the awful starlit sky. There was a moment of utter bewilderment before her mind could get disentangled from the confused web of dreams; but soon the whole terrible truth urged itself upon her. Stephen was not by her now; she was alone with her own memory and her own dread. The irrevocable wrong that must blot her life had been committed; she had brought sorrow into the lives of others,—into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love. The feeling of a few short weeks had hurried her into the sins her nature had most recoiled from,—breach of faith and cruel selfishness; she had rent the ties that had given meaning to duty, and had made herself an outlawed soul, with no guide but the wayward choice of her own passion. And where would that lead her? Where had it led her now? She had said she would rather die than fall into that temptation. She felt it now,—now that the consequences of such a fall had come before the outward act was completed. There was at least this fruit from all her years of striving after the highest and best,—that her soul though betrayed, beguiled, ensnared, could never deliberately consent to a choice of the lower. And a choice of what? O God! not a choice of joy, but of conscious cruelty and hardness; for could she ever cease to see before her Lucy and Philip, with their murdered trust and hopes? Her life with Stephen could have no sacredness; she must forever sink and wander vaguely, driven by uncertain impulse; for she had let go the clue of life,—that clue which once in the far-off years her young need had clutched so strongly. She had renounced all delights then, before she knew them, before they had come within her reach. Philip had been right when he told her that she knew nothing of renunciation; she had thought it was quiet ecstasy; she saw it face to face now,—that sad, patient, loving strength which holds the clue of life,—and saw that the thorns were forever pressing on its brow. The yesterday, which could never be revoked,—if she could have changed it now for any length of inward silent endurance, she would have bowed beneath that cross with a sense of rest.

Edmund's right — and yet I want to hold on to what makes that passage so extraordinary, which is that it isn't only an indictment. The dream that precedes it — Tom rowing past without looking at her, the boat turning over — that is grief shaped as nightmare. Eliot writes: "she rose to stretch out her arms and call to him" — and that gesture, that reaching toward Tom even in the dream, tells you everything about what Maggie is losing. The condemnation and the compassion are indistinguishable in this prose.
