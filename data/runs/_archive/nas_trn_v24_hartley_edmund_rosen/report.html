Delighted to be here — this is a novel I find myself returning to almost every year, and it never gives up the same book twice. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is quite right about the indirection — and I think that restraint is the key to the whole novel's moral texture. Gaskell is not interested in exposing suffering — she is interested in the character that suffering either calls forth or destroys. Margaret's response to this overheard prayer is not self-pity and not protest — it is planning. She lies awake devising how to procure another servant so that her mother might have the personal attention she requires. That is duty enacted, not merely felt — and that distinction is what separates heroic character from mere sensibility. One rather wishes certain readers of this novel would attend to Margaret's example before reaching for their theoretical frameworks.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'll take that very courteous barb on the chin, Edmund, and note that Margaret's admirable planning is also a response to a material problem — the family's reduced economic circumstances in Milton. The sordid domestic worries Gaskell names are not abstract — they are the specific texture of genteel poverty, of people whose class position no longer matches their income. But what interests me even more is the moment a chapter later, where Margaret turns her critical eye on Thornton. She has just praised the one thing about him she found genuine — his account of starting as a shop-boy. "Everything else revolted me, from its hardness; but he spoke about himself so simply — with so little of the pretence that makes the vulgarity of shop-people, and with such tender respect for his mother." That word revolted — it's violent, it's physical — and she's applying it to his class pride, his boasting about Milton, his passive contempt for workers' improvidence. Gaskell gives Margaret a sharper diagnosis of industrial capitalism's moral failures than Thornton himself possesses at this stage — and she doesn't even know she's making it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd argue the price of cotton is precisely what makes that tension possible — but we'll get to that. What I want to flag now is a passage near the end of the novel — Chapter Forty-Six — that puts all these questions about change and loss and what the system produces into a different, rawer key. Margaret returns to Helstone — the southern pastoral idyll she's been holding in memory as an alternative to Milton — and finds it changed beyond recovery. Gaskell writes — and this is worth sitting with — "places were changed — a tree gone here, a bough there, bringing in a long ray of light where no light was before — a road was trimmed and narrowed, and the green straggling pathway by its side enclosed and cultivated." Enclosed and cultivated — that phrase is doing double work: pastoral improvement and enclosure of common land are the same gesture in this novel's world. The South is not exempt from what the North makes visible — it has just been doing it more quietly, for longer.

This is one of the passages I keep returning to, because Gaskell gives us something genuinely rare in Victorian fiction: a heroine in the act of self-indictment. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And from that devastation, Gaskell builds the resolution — and it is one of the most perfectly calibrated proposals in Victorian fiction. Because Thornton doesn't prostrate himself. He cedes power to Margaret — but the grammar is pure Thornton, still urgent, still hovering at the edge of presumption. He says — "Take care. — If you do not speak — I shall claim you as my own in some strange presumptuous way. — Send me away at once, if I must go; — Margaret! —" Every dash in that sentence is a man holding himself back by sheer will. It gives her the power — and then that single word, "Margaret," the only time in the novel he calls her by her name alone — breaks everything open.
