James Blackstone (social_historian)
What he's doing is something quite audacious for the opening of a novel — he refuses to give you a person, or a story. He gives you an environment, and he insists that the environment is the argument. The fog and mud are not backdrop — they are the condition of life in this city, in this legal system, in this novel. And then he does something wonderfully strange. "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A dinosaur on Holborn Hill. It's a joke, but it's a serious joke — this city is so primordial, so buried under accumulated filth, that geological time feels plausible. And the word 'accumulating at compound interest' arrives a few lines later — suddenly the mud is financial.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Both of those observations are exactly right — and I want to push them a little further. Notice who is in this fog — foot passengers losing their footing, jostling each other in 'a general infection of ill temper.' These are ordinary people, urban poor, doing what the city requires them to do — and the city punishes them for it. The mud accumulates 'at compound interest' — James noticed the financial language — but compound interest punishes the people who have least. The fog isn't neutral — it is the atmosphere that the powerful generate, and the powerless must breathe.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel is right about the asymmetry — and it connects directly to the Court of Chancery, which Dickens introduces almost immediately after. Chancery was not an abstract evil — it was a real institution that could hold an estate in litigation for decades while the people connected to it were slowly destroyed. That sentence lands like a verdict. Chancery doesn't just slow things down — it severs the human connections that make life liveable.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The coroner's inquest in this period was genuinely the primary mechanism for investigating unexplained deaths among the poor. And the quality of testimony Dickens depicts is not especially satirical — it's actually fairly accurate. Neighbourhood witnesses routinely gave evidence of this character: impressions, fears, rumours, third-hand reports. What Dickens adds is the comedy of the syntax — that extraordinary run-on sentence about Alexander James Piper, eighteen months old, whose teething troubles somehow become admissible evidence. The point is that the institution exists, it functions, it produces verdicts — but it cannot see the actual person at the centre of it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is the question Nemo keeps asking Jo afterward — whether he slept sound, how he bore cold and hunger, whether he ever wished to die. These are not the questions of charity. They're the questions of someone who knows the answers from the inside. Nemo is not distributing benevolence downward. He recognises Jo as an equal in suffering. The line — "I am as poor as you to-day, Jo" — is the one moment in the novel where a person of even marginal status addresses this invisible boy by name and as a peer. The system produces these two figures — the destitute anonymous man and the destitute anonymous boy — and the only solidarity available to them is the solidarity of shared nothing.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to hold on to that detail about Jo giving his evidence — because the inquest then dismisses it. A crossing-sweeper's testimony was worth almost nothing as formal evidence in this period. Children of Jo's class were routinely found incompetent to testify — they were assumed not to understand the oath, not to grasp the meaning of truth under legal obligation. And yet what Jo says — "He wos wery good to me, he wos!" — is the only true, the only human testimony in the entire proceeding. The law cannot hear him. That is Dickens's indictment — rendered as a weeping child wiping his eyes with a wretched sleeve.
