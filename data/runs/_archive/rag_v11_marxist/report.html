James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me every time I return to that opening is how carefully Dickens positions the fog before he gives us a single human being. We get the Thames, the meadows, the streets of London — and only then, buried in all of that murk, do we get the Lord Chancellor. And the fog is not metaphor yet — or not only metaphor. It's a physical description of the Court of Chancery's effect on everything around it. When we finally step inside the court itself, what we find is — and this is the thing — almost nothing happening. Dickens gives us this wonderful, terrible inventory of who is actually present in the Lord Chancellor's court on that murky afternoon. "There is the registrar below the judge, in wig and gown; and there are two or three maces, or petty-bags, or privy purses, or whatever they may be, in legal court suits. These are all yawning, for no crumb of amusement ever falls from Jarndyce and Jarndyce." "Whatever they may be" — that parenthetical is doing enormous work. Dickens is telling us that the court's own personnel are so arcane, so irrelevant to ordinary life, that even the narrator cannot be bothered to name them properly. And then we meet the court's real inhabitants — the ones who have been swallowed by it. There is the mad old woman with her reticule of paper matches. There is the prisoner who has been attempting to purge his contempt charge for what sounds like years. And there is the man from Shropshire — who comes periodically to address the Chancellor, and who simply cannot be made to understand that Chancery does not know he exists. That phrase — "having made his existence desolate for a quarter of a century" — is historically precise, not hyperbole. The real Chancery suits of the era could and did run for decades, consuming entire lifetimes, entire family fortunes. Dickens was not exaggerating. He was, if anything, being restrained.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What James is pointing to is exactly right, and I want to press on it a little — because there's something even more specific happening in that passage. The people in the court who are not destroyed by it — the lawyers, the clerks — are described as yawning, or lingering for sport. The ruined suitor from Shropshire is, in Dickens' own words, a source of potential "fun" for the lawyers' clerks watching him. That is the class relation laid bare: one man's destroyed life is another man's entertainment. But what I find most devastating is what Dickens shows us later — the physical legacy of Chancery written into the actual fabric of London. Jarndyce — one of the most decent men in the novel — describes a street of property nominally belonging to the suit. "It is a street of perishing blind houses, with their eyes stoned out, without a pane of glass, without so much as a window-frame, with the bare blank shutters tumbling from their hinges and falling asunder, the iron rails peeling away in flakes of rust, the chimneys sinking in, the stone steps to every door — and every door might be death's door — turning stagnant green." "Eyes stoned out." Dickens chose that phrase. Not boarded up. Not shuttered. Stoned out — as in, attacked, blinded, deliberately destroyed. And then the line that lands like a verdict: "These are the Great Seal's impressions, my dear, all over England — the children know them." The children know them. Not the lawyers. Not the judges. The children — who live in those streets, who grow up in that decay. Chancery does not merely fail the poor. It stamps its failure onto the physical world and then moves on.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I want to come back to something in what Daniel just read, because that passage from Jarndyce is one I've never managed to get through quickly. The sentence is structured as an accumulation — clause after clause piling up, each one a fresh detail of ruin. You cannot read it fast. The syntax itself slows you down, forces you to look at every broken thing in turn. And that, to me, is Dickens at his most technically brilliant — when the form enacts the content. There is a passage later in the novel that works the same way, but even more hauntingly for me — the description of Richard walking in Lincoln's Inn. By that point in the story, we already love Richard and we already dread what's happening to him. And then the narrator does something quite extraordinary. "On many such loungers have the speckled shadows of those trees often fallen; on the like bent head, the bitten nail, the lowering eye, the lingering step, the purposeless and dreamy air, the good consuming and consumed, the life turned sour." "The good consuming and consumed." That is six words. Six words that describe what Chancery does to a person who had genuine promise, genuine decency — Richard Carstone, specifically — but also every person who came before him. The active and passive form together — consuming and consumed — he is doing it to himself, and it is being done to him simultaneously. And then: "This lounger is not shabby yet, but that may come." "Yet." One word carrying an entire future the reader can already see and Richard cannot. That is what Dickens does better than almost anyone — he makes you grieve for someone who is still walking in sunshine.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Caroline's reading of that passage makes me want to press on the line that precedes it: "Chancery, which knows no wisdom but in precedent, is very rich in such precedents." That is not a throwaway clause. That is a precise legal critique. The Court of Chancery operated almost entirely on precedent — on past decisions — rather than on any fixed statutory code. Which meant that the system's greatest expertise was in reproducing the outcomes it had always produced — ruin, delay, cost. Dickens knew this. He was not summarising a legal textbook — he was describing a system he had watched operating on real families. "Why should one be different from ten thousand?" — the narrator asks about Richard, and the answer, historically speaking, is that there was no mechanism by which he could be. The system was not broken. It was working exactly as designed — for the benefit of those whose income depended on its perpetuation.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James has just said in one sentence what the novel takes eight hundred pages to demonstrate — and I mean that as a genuine compliment. "Working exactly as designed." That is the horror. Because the standard reading of Bleak House — the comfortable reading — is that Chancery is an aberration, a grotesque dysfunction that Dickens is holding up for reform. But Dickens keeps showing us who benefits, and it is never the suitors. It is always the lawyers. The fog at the novel's opening is not a symbol of confusion — it is a symbol of deliberate concealment. It hides a transfer of wealth — from the Jarnydces, from all the suitors, into the hands of Vholes and Tulkinghorn and every lawyer in the book. Dickens, I would argue, was more radical than he fully intended to be.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Dickens gives us in the opening pages of this novel is a portrait of Chancery that is, in its essentials, historically accurate — and that's what makes it so devastating. The Court of Chancery by the 1850s was genuinely, structurally incapable of resolving the cases before it. Cases could run for decades — generations — and the costs were borne entirely by the litigants, eaten alive by fees. And yet Dickens captures something even darker than the dysfunction — he captures the attitude of the profession toward that dysfunction. He gives us this extraordinary line very early on — let me read it because the rhythm is everything. "Jarndyce and Jarndyce has passed into a joke. That is the only good that has ever come of it. It has been death to many, but it is a joke in the profession." Death to many, but a joke in the profession. That pivot — that brutal little pivot — is the whole indictment in a sentence.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And notice what the joke is doing, institutionally. The laughter of the profession is not incidental — it is a mechanism of self-protection. If Jarndyce and Jarndyce is a joke, then it cannot be a crime. If every Chancellor has been in it, if every articled clerk has sharpened his wit on it — then it belongs to everyone, it is culture, it is tradition. And tradition cannot be dismantled. That is exactly the move Dickens shows Kenge making later with Vholes — the same sleight of hand, the same immunization through respectability.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
If I may — I think the sharpest formulation of what Dickens demands comes not from the grand satirical set-pieces, but from Krook's shop. There's a moment where Krook recounts the fate of Tom Jarndyce — a man consumed by the Chancery suit — and the language is almost clinical in its horror. "It's being ground to bits in a slow mill; it's being roasted at a slow fire; it's being stung to death by single bees; it's being drowned by drops; it's going mad by grains." Five separate images of incremental destruction. Not a catastrophe — a process. And that is precisely how the Victorian legal system worked — not through spectacular cruelty, but through attrition. Fees accumulating, years passing, will to live gradually surrendered. Tom Jarndyce ends up shooting himself in a coffee house near Chancery Lane. Dickens' demand, as I read it, is that we refuse the comfort of calling this historical.

The grinding by grains — that's the novel's central political image. It's not the villain with a face. It's the system that has no face — that processes human beings as though they were raw material. The view from my Lady Dedlock’s own windows is alternately a lead-coloured view and a view in Indian ink.
