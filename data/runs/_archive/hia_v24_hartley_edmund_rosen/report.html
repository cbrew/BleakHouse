Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What he's doing is something almost unprecedented in the Victorian novel. He's refusing to give us a character, a drawing room, a family — any of the usual points of entry. Instead he gives us a city — a condition. The opening paragraph is a catalogue of damage. Mud that has accumulated, as Dickens writes, at what he calls compound interest. Listen to the scale of it: "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A prehistoric monster, waddling through the modern city. Dickens is telling us: this city has not evolved; it has merely accumulated. The mud is not picturesque — it is the residue of a system that deposits misery the way a river deposits silt.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That phrase — 'compound interest' — is doing so much work, and I think we should sit with it for a moment. Dickens borrows the language of finance to describe physical filth — and that slippage is entirely deliberate. What's architecturally brilliant about this opening is the complete absence of a main verb for the first several sentences. 'London. Michaelmas term lately over. Implacable November weather.' — just nouns and fragments. The world is presented to us as a series of arrested facts — as if action itself has been suspended. And notice the smoke: 'making a soft black drizzle, with flakes of soot in it as big as full-grown snowflakes — gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun.' The sun has died. The novel opens with a world in which natural light has been extinguished. That is your structural keynote for everything that follows.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I wouldn't quarrel with either of those observations, but I'd want to draw attention to something rather different. It is the word 'implacable' that has always arrested me. Implacable November weather — weather that cannot be appeased, that will not relent. Dickens is invoking something ancient here — a universe morally indifferent to human suffering. And the fog and mud are not, I think, primarily symbols of capitalism — they are the condition in which moral choices must be made. The question Dickens presses upon his characters from the very first page is this: given this world, what will you do?

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd say those two things are not easily separable, Sir Edmund. The conditions in which moral choices are made are themselves produced by particular arrangements of power. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That stagecraft extends, I would argue, to his handling of the comic characters — and there is nothing accidental about their placement. The fog of suspicion and misinterpretation is just as thick in a Holborn parlour as on the London streets. Consider Mrs. Snagsby. She has assembled an elaborate theory of her husband's infidelity from the flimsiest of circumstantial evidence. And Dickens gives her this triumphant inner revelation: "Who told him not to come? Who? Ha, ha! Mrs. Snagsby sees it all." She sees nothing, of course. But her blindness is the same blindness as the fog — it is what happens when people cannot perceive reality clearly, and mistake their own projections for truth. Dickens the moralist and Dickens the satirist are not in conflict here — they are the same hand.

Host (host)
Welcome back to Bleak House Unpacked. We turn now to one of the novel's darkest and most urgent preoccupations: the machinery of injustice. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Thank you — and I think that word, anatomy, is exactly right. Dickens isn't simply sketching scenes of poverty in Bleak House. He is constructing a systematic argument about what the institutions of Victorian England actually do. Take the scene in Tom-all-Alone's. Snagsby is taken through the slum by Bucket with a bull's-eye lamp — as if the light itself were an accusation. Dickens gives us this extraordinary sentence — let me read it precisely as he wrote it. "a villainous street, undrained, unventilated, deep in black mud and corrupt water — though the roads are dry elsewhere — and reeking with such smells and sights that he, who has lived in London all his life, can scarce believe his senses." That parenthesis — though the roads are dry elsewhere — is doing enormous ideological work. It insists that this deprivation is not natural, not inevitable. It is produced. It is chosen, by those with the power to choose differently. And the streets branch outward — Dickens writes — into courts so infamous that Snagsby feels as if he is going deeper into an infernal gulf.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is the precision of the narrative point of view. Snagsby is a man who has lived in London all his life. He is not naive. He knows the city. And yet Dickens uses him precisely because his incredulity indicts the reader along with him. I don't dispute the tenderness — in fact, it's precisely the tenderness that makes the next passage so important. Esther watches the two women — coarse, shabby, beaten — and she reflects on something that Dickens, through her, insists upon as a kind of epistemological scandal. That invisibility is itself a form of violence.
