Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately, even in these early chapters, is how much Gaskell is doing through physical description — and how quietly she does it. The passage where Margaret compares her father and Mr. Thornton is one of the most architecturally precise pieces of characterisation in the novel. Gaskell is running two faces in parallel — and the contrast isn't merely physical, it's almost philosophical. Listen to how she describes Mr. Hale's eyes — the eyelids are 'large and arched, giving to the eyes a peculiar languid beauty which was almost feminine.' That word 'feminine' is doing immense cultural work in 1855 — it codes him as interior, spiritual, ineffectual in the world of commerce. And then she turns to Thornton, and the prose hardens — literally hardens — with this: 'The straight brows fell low over the clear, deep-set, earnest eyes, which without being unpleasantly sharp, seemed intent enough to penetrate into the very heart and core of what he was looking at.' The syntax itself becomes more angular — fewer curves, more load-bearing.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Eleanor is identifying in the prose mirrors something historically precise about how these two men would have been understood by contemporary readers. Mr. Hale is a clergyman who has relinquished his living — a man of the old educated class who exists outside the market entirely. Thornton is a manufacturer — which in the eighteen-fifties placed him somewhere rather uncomfortable in the social taxonomy. Respectable enough to be received in drawing rooms, but not quite a gentleman — not in the inherited, landed sense that still carried the most social weight. So Gaskell's physical contrast — soft, dreaming clergyman versus 'firm, carved in marble' manufacturer — is also a map of competing social orders. The old England and the new one, sitting in the same room — which is the whole novel in miniature, really.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto that image — two men absorbed in conversation while the women in the room do needlework and notice things. Margaret's observation is triggered by — and I love this detail — 'some trivial, low-spoken remark of her mother's.' The men theorise. The women are recalled to the present by the trivial. But Gaskell is also showing us that this marginal position — watching rather than speaking — produces a sharper analytical intelligence. Margaret reads these two men more perceptively from her needlework than either of them reads each other. The novel keeps returning to that dynamic — the people the system pushes to the edge are the ones who see it most clearly.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the context Gaskell gives for that suffering is worth pausing on — because it isn't abstract. She says the air itself is 'deprived of all revivifying principle' — and that is not a metaphor. That was the literal medical understanding of smoky industrial air in the 1850s. Milton is making Mrs. Hale ill in ways that are entirely specific to class displacement — and Gaskell is precise about the mechanism.

What strikes me first about that early confrontation between Margaret and Thornton is the very specific staging of her emotion. Gaskell gives us Margaret roused — which is a beautifully chosen word — roused to a fond vehemence of defence. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right that Margaret's observation is sharp, but I'd want to press on what she's seeing and what she's not. Her diagnosis — a sullen sense of injustice — is accurate as social observation goes. Industrial workers in the 1850s had genuine grievances rooted in wages, hours, safety, and a legal system that treated combinations of workers as criminal conspiracy. But Margaret frames it as a contrast with Southern poverty, and that framing is ideologically convenient for her. Southern rural poverty was miserable in its own specific ways — tied labour, tied cottages, no union movement, no combination at all. The Southern poor didn't have that sullen expression of injustice because they had fewer channels through which to perceive and articulate it — not because they suffered less.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James is putting his finger on exactly what the novel has to work through — and doesn't fully resolve. Margaret's error is the classic error of the humanitarian observer: she sees suffering as a visible, countable thing. She sees men ground down — and that seeing is real — but she doesn't yet have a structural account of why they are ground down. That account comes later, and it comes from Nicholas Higgins, not from any comfortable drawing-room conversation. Higgins gives her the historical dimension — and it's worth hearing it in his voice. "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and th' chidren's teeth are set on edge... It's a withstanding of injustice, past, present, or to come." He's giving her — and us — a theory of inherited structural oppression dressed in scripture. The union isn't a crime — it's the answer to a crime that came first.

It appeared to Mr. Thornton that all these graceful cares were habitual to the family; and especially of a piece with Margaret. She stood by the tea-table in a light-coloured muslin gown, which had a good deal of pink about it. She looked as if she was not attending to the conversation, but solely busy with the tea-cups, among which her round ivory hands moved with pretty, noiseless, daintiness. She had a bracelet on one taper arm, which would fall down over her round wrist. Mr. Thornton watched the replacing of this troublesome ornament with far more attention than he listened to her father. It seemed as if it fascinated him to see her push it up impatiently until it tightened her soft flesh; and then to mark the loosening—the fall. He could almost have exclaimed—“There it goes again!” There was so little left to be done after he arrived at the preparation for tea, that he was almost sorry the obligation of eating and drinking came so soon to prevent him watching Margaret. She handed him his cup of tea with the proud air of an unwilling slave; but her eye caught the moment when he was ready for another cup; and he almost longed to ask her to do for him what he saw her compelled to do for her father, who took her little finger and thumb in his masculine hand, and made them serve as sugar-tongues. Mr. Thornton saw her beautiful eyes lifted to her father, full of light, half-laughter, and half-love, as this bit of pantomime went on between the two, unobserved as they fancied, by any. Margaret’s head still ached, as the paleness of her complexion, and her silence might have testified; but she was resolved to throw herself into the breach, if there was any long untoward pause, rather than that her father’s friend, pupil, and guest should have cause to think himself in any way neglected. But the conversation went on; and Margaret drew into a corner, near her mother, with her work, after the tea-things were taken away; and felt that she might let her thoughts roam, without fear of being suddenly wanted to fill up a gap.
