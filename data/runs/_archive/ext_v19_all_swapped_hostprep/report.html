Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver, that was magnificent — and you've identified the thing I want to push on. The fog does not fall equally. Listen to what the passage actually maps: 'green aits and meadows' on one side, 'waterside pollutions of a great and dirty city' on the other. The fog is cruelest to the boy — the one with no name, no age, no story — because that is where the system deposits its cruelty. So yes — it is a physical thing. It is also a geography of power. Those two things are not in competition.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I will confess, I find Daniel's geography persuasive up to a point — but the passage does something that defeats it. It ends with 'chance people on the bridges peeping over the parapets into a nether sky of fog, with fog all round them, as if they were up in a balloon and hanging in the misty clouds.' I want to stay with this question of conscience versus structure — but via the Megalosaurus, if you'll permit me. And yet — Dickens cannot leave institutions alone. He keeps returning to the law, specifically. There is a passage later in the novel — the defence of Mr. Vholes — that I think is the funniest and most lethal thing Dickens ever wrote about professional respectability. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The passage is extraordinarily beautiful — I don't think anyone would dispute that. But beauty and eulogy are not the same thing, and I think it's important that we don't let the prose seduce us into misreading what Dickens is actually doing. Listen to what Dickens writes, and listen carefully to its structure. "So young, so beautiful, so full of hope and promise, they went on lightly through the sunlight as their own happy thoughts might then be traversing the years to come and making them all years of brightness." That is not a description of doom — that is a description of self-delusion rendered in gorgeous language. The light is theirs — it belongs to their own happy thoughts. Dickens is showing us what they imagine, not what awaits them. And then — with absolute economy — the sun clouds over and the room darkens. That is moral judgment delivered through image.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'm not trying to make anyone feel grubby. I'm trying to make them see the architecture of the emotion more clearly. What Dickens gives us in that scene is a handkerchief passed from Esther to a dead child, and then carried — we learn — by a living woman in flight. The object travels through the class structure — from the respectable household of Bleak House into the slums and onto the body of a fugitive. And Dickens renders that journey as spiritual revelation — as if the motion of the handkerchief sanctifies everyone it touches. What the novel does NOT render is any account of why the child died, who is responsible for the conditions of that death, or what might prevent the next one. Individual compassion becomes the answer to a structural question — and the novel is so beautiful about it that we don't notice the substitution.

I've been thinking about this for twenty years and I still can't give you a clean answer — which is probably the right response. What I can say is this: Dickens builds the trap and he shows us the trap and he springs the trap on us — all in the same gesture. The handkerchief moves us. We should be moved. And the movement does nothing to change the conditions in the brick field where the child died. If Dickens were purely complicit, he would not have written the fog, the mud, the crossing-sweeper dying on the street. The system is too visible in this novel to be accidental. I think the framing of 'complicity' requires a theory of literature as primarily a political instrument, which I resist — but I will say this. Dickens knew — deeply, viscerally, from his own childhood — what it costs to have no Jarndyce. The 'unquiet bosom,' the chain of flowers that might become a chain of lead — these are images of a man who never forgot that happiness is fragile and mostly undeserved. What he gives us — in Jarndyce's generosity, in Esther's watching compassion, in even the absurdity of Boythorn — is not a solution.
