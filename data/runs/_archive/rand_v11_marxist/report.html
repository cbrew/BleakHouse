Caroline's point about tenderness is well-taken — and I think it's important not to read my argument as cold. Dickens is furious on behalf of people like Esther precisely because he loves them. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That observation cuts to something historians find again and again — the reform of manner is not the reform of structure. Bucket is gentler than a magistrate's constable would have been. But the inquiry is the same inquiry. Dickens was writing at the precise moment when Victorian institutions were being reformed in exactly this way — humanised on the surface, preserved in their essentials. The Poor Law of 1834 was sold as compassionate rationalisation. It was, in practice, a system of deliberate cruelty dressed in the language of improvement.

I keep coming back to Caddy Jellyby — of all people — as the figure who captures something the grander arguments sometimes miss. She's a minor figure by most measures, but there's this flash of anger very early on — she accuses Esther of lying, of not saying what she actually sees. She says — with real heat — "You're speaking now. You know you are. Don't tell stories, Miss Summerson." It's a tiny scene. But there's something in it about the cost of politeness — of the stories the respectable tell themselves to keep functioning. Esther's whole narrative is, in a sense, a long effort not to tell stories — to see clearly, report honestly, and somehow survive the clarity. Caddy calls her on it at the start.
