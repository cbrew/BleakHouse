James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me first, coming to Bleak House from a historical direction, is that Dickens sets his fog to do double work from the very opening pages. It is atmosphere, yes — but it is also a precise description of what London's air actually was in the 1850s: coal smoke, river damp, the accumulated filth of an unventilated city. And then he takes you somewhere the fog has solidified into something even worse. When Bucket leads Snagsby into Tom-all-Alone's, Dickens gives us this: "a villainous street, undrained, unventilated, deep in black mud and corrupt water — though the roads are dry elsewhere — and reeking with such smells and sights that he, who has lived in London all his life, can scarce believe his senses." That parenthetical — though the roads are dry elsewhere — is doing something forensic. Dickens will not let you think this is weather or bad luck. This is a condition maintained by specific choices, by specific people.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think we should be careful not to dissolve the gothic into the sociological quite so quickly. What Dickens gives us in that terrace scene is something older and — I would argue — more powerful than social critique. It is the drama of a will that refuses to be broken by circumstance — a woman who converts her suffering into a terrible kind of dignity. Listen to what she actually says in the moment her husband lifts her from the pavement: "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled." That is not political programme. That is the concentrated force of a human soul refusing extinction. Dickens understood — as Burke understood — that institutions are haunted by the characters that formed them, and the characters they have broken.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I don't dispute the moral force of that — it is an extraordinary speech. But the pride she's cursing is not abstract. The Dedlock family pride is maintained through a legal and financial apparatus — entailed estates, arranged alliances, the machinery of aristocratic self-perpetuation. And when that apparatus turns against a woman — as Tulkinghorn later uses it against Lady Dedlock — the soul Edmund describes has very little room to manoeuvre. Tulkinghorn's threat to Lady Dedlock is a masterclass in institutional power deployed without heat. He tells her, as she reaches for the door: "Lady Dedlock, have the goodness to stop and hear me, or before you reach the staircase I shall ring the alarm-bell and rouse the house." He doesn't raise his voice. He doesn't move a limb. The power is entirely in the structure — and he knows it, and she knows it.

That stillness is one of the most frightening things Dickens ever wrote, technically speaking. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that self-knowledge makes the Chancery obsession even more insidious, because it gives Richard something to stand by — that phrase he uses himself. He finds his way into Kenge's law office, ostensibly to read law — but listen to how he describes his real purpose: "If I went into Kenge's office... In the real legal world of the eighteen-fifties, Chancery suits of this kind could run for decades. They consumed young men's lives the way Richard's is being consumed — not by violence but by distraction, by perpetual deferred expectation.
