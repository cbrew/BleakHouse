Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Gaskell does in that opening domestic scene is quietly devastating — she sets up a whole world of self-deception in a single paragraph. Mrs. Shaw is performing maternal feeling — performing it with tremendous conviction, to herself as much as to her guests. And then Gaskell inserts this perfectly balanced clause that punctures the whole edifice. "Mrs. Shaw said that her only child should marry for love,—and sighed emphatically, as if love had not been her motive for marrying the General." That em dash before the sigh — that is architecture. The pause mimics the sigh itself, and the irony lands in the silence. Gaskell could have been withering about these people — instead she's almost affectionate, which makes the comedy sharper.

One agrees that the comedy is exquisitely done — but I'd resist the temptation to read it merely as satire. Mrs. Shaw is a weak woman, not a wicked one. And the scene establishes something quietly essential about Margaret — she is watching all of this, and she is not taken in. We are told the very parts that made Edith shudder made Margaret glow. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The debate scenes are where Gaskell is at her most daring as a novelist — because argument is notoriously hard to make dramatic. And yet these conversations crackle. Margaret's defence of the South in Chapter Ten is a perfect example — she's roused to genuine passion, and Gaskell doesn't soften her. The phrase is — she's roused by what she feels is an aspersion on her beloved South. "I see men here going about in the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care—who are not only sufferers but haters." That word — haters — arrives like a stone dropped into still water. It's not a genteel word, and Margaret is not in a genteel mood. Gaskell makes her argue well — which is the greatest compliment a novelist can pay a character.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
She does argue well — and yet one notices that she is also, in this scene, wrong in a rather instructive way. She is speaking from attachment — from love of the South — not from knowledge of the North. And Thornton, for all his autocratic swagger, is speaking from experience. His position is not comfortable — but it is not cynical either. He believes in authority because he believes in order — and order, for Thornton, is a moral condition. He says, quite plainly — and I confess I find this passage fascinating rather than repellent — "I maintain that despotism is the best kind of government for them; so that in the hours in which I come in contact with them I must necessarily be an autocrat." That is not a comfortable thing to say — but it is an honest one. And Gaskell does not let us dismiss it cheaply.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that tension extends to her view of the South, which is not simply nostalgic. There's a remarkable passage later in the novel — Chapter Thirty-Seven — where Margaret tries to dissuade Higgins from going South to find work. And the portrait she paints of Southern rural labour is devastating — not romantic at all. She tells him, with real urgency — "Those that have lived there all their lives, are used to soaking in the stagnant waters. They labour on from day to day, in the great solitude of steaming fields—never speaking or lifting up their poor, bent, downcast heads." The South she loved as a girl is not the South she can offer Higgins as a refuge. By this point she has been changed by Milton — even as she still loves the South. Gaskell builds that transformation so gradually you almost don't notice it — and then a passage like this lands, and you see how far she's come.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me most forcefully about that passage is not the social context at all, but the moral weight of a single threshold. Mr. Hale stands in the doorway — and that doorway is everything. He is a man who has broken faith with his calling, and now cannot quite bring himself to re-enter his own home. Gaskell's description of him is devastating in its quietness. "His face was gray and pale; he had a timid fearful look in his eyes; something almost pitiful to see in a man's face." That word 'pitiful' is doing an enormous amount of work. It is not contempt — Gaskell never allows us contempt — but it is a reckoning with a kind of failure that has its own dignity. And it is Mrs. Hale who rises to meet it — not Margaret, who has carried the day's burden. That is Gaskell's moral imagination at its most precise: the person we least expect finds, in the moment, a grace she cannot always sustain.
