Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Thank you — yes, and I think this is the essential entry point into the novel's moral universe. Dickens gives us the Veneerings — this family conjured from nowhere, whose entire social existence is pure performance — and then he gives us Lady Tippins, who is the performance's most self-aware cheerleader. She clatters around town drumming up votes for Veneering's parliamentary campaign, and she does it while openly, gleefully, confessing the entire thing is a charade. Nobody knows who these Veneerings are — she says it herself, in the middle of campaigning for them. This is Dickens's portrait of a ruling class that has abandoned even the pretence of legitimacy — and what's terrifying is that the system works anyway.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver puts his finger on something I want to hold for a moment — because the novel isn't only comic. Running alongside this glittering social satire is another story entirely — a story about a man pulled under the Thames and fighting his way back to the surface. John Harmon's recovery of consciousness is one of the most viscerally written passages in the novel. Dickens writes it as a kind of interior command — the self ordering itself to survive. "This is John Harmon drowning! John Harmon, struggle for your life. John Harmon, call on Heaven and save yourself!" Three times the name — like a desperate act of self-reconstitution. And this matters enormously because the entire plot turns on whether John Harmon can remain himself — or whether the world's demand that he perform an identity will swallow him whole.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about the John Harmon soliloquy is how Dickens frames an act of profound paternalism as though it were entirely reasonable. Harmon recounts his calculation — and notice that word, 'calculation' — with the careful logic of a man who has already decided. The sentence that reveals everything is this one — listen to its structure: 'the thought entered my head of turning the danger I had passed through, to the account of being for some time supposed to have disappeared mysteriously, and of proving Bella.' 'Proving Bella.' Two words at the end of a very long sentence — and they carry the whole moral weight of the novel's central plot. She is to be tested. She doesn't know she's being tested. And the tester has appointed himself with no authority other than inheritance and desire. Dickens gives us the logic in Harmon's own voice — and it's seductive logic — but the architecture of that sentence quietly indicts it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor is absolutely right about the indictment — and I'd push it further. What Harmon calls 'moral timidity' is in fact the ideology of property speaking through him. The 'fate' he fears — that his father's riches 'should lead to nothing but evil' — is displaced entirely onto Bella's character. The system that produced those riches, the labour that created them, the river that yields and destroys — none of that is examined. A woman must be refined and tested so that the money may be enjoyed with a clear conscience. That is the transaction. And Dickens — to his immense credit — makes the machinery visible, even when his plot ultimately rewards Harmon for running it.

What strikes me about that scene — and about the whole novel's ending — is the cost that the novel is willing to acknowledge even as it resolves. The Boffins are kind people — genuinely kind — but they are, finally, the inheritors of a dust fortune, a fortune built on waste and labour. And then Dickens turns the whole tension of class and appearance into pure comedy with Sloppy's suit. Which sounds like a tonal lurch — but it isn't, because Dickens earns it.
