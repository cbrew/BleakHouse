Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close reading of Charles Dickens's last completed novel. Our Mutual Friend is a book about money — about what it does to people, what it makes them pretend to be, and occasionally what it helps them escape. It is also a book about dust heaps — vast, reeking mounds of Victorian London's waste — that happen to be worth a fortune. And it is, underneath everything else, a book about resurrection — the recurring possibility that people and relationships can be remade. To work through this extraordinary novel, I'm joined by three remarkable readers. He is sceptical of politicised readings, and I suspect he will keep us honest. Professor James Blackstone is a legal and social historian who knows the institutions of Victorian England from the inside out — the workhouses, the courts, the dust economy. When the novel touches the real world, James will tell us exactly how real it was. And Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who first read this novel as a teenager and has been re-reading it ever since. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The atmosphere of this novel is established very early through a single, perfectly observed room. When Silas Wegg first visits the Bower — the Boffins' home — Dickens gives us a space that is a compressed social history of sudden wealth. The description is worth reading closely, because Dickens is doing something quite precise here. "It was the queerest of rooms, fitted and furnished more like a luxurious amateur tap-room than anything else within the ken of Silas Wegg." A tap-room — a public house saloon — that is luxurious. That conjunction is the whole joke — and the whole tragedy — of the Boffins' position. They have money enough for drawing-room furniture, but their instincts are still the settle and the sawdust. And Dickens doesn't condemn them for it — which is the really interesting choice.

There were two wooden settles by the fire, one on either side of it, with a corresponding table before each. On the hob, a kettle steamed; on the hearth, a cat reposed. Facing the fire between the settles, a sofa, a footstool, and a little table, formed a centrepiece devoted to Mrs Boffin. They were garish in taste and colour, but were expensive articles of drawing-room furniture that had a very odd look beside the settles and the flaring gaslight pendent from the ceiling. There was a flowery carpet on the floor; but, instead of reaching to the fireside, its glowing vegetation stopped short at Mrs Boffin’s footstool, and gave place to a region of sand and sawdust. Mr Wegg also noticed, with admiring eyes, that, while the flowery land displayed such hollow ornamentation as stuffed birds and waxen fruits under glass-shades, there were, in the territory where vegetation ceased, compensatory shelves on which the best part of a large pie and likewise of a cold joint were plainly discernible among other solids. The room itself was large, though low; and the heavy frames of its old-fashioned windows, and the heavy beams in its crooked ceiling, seemed to indicate that it had once been a house of some mark standing alone in the country.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Precisely — and one notices that the carpet tells the whole story in miniature. The flowery carpet advances so far and then — stops. It gives way, as Dickens puts it, to a region of sand and sawdust. "its glowing vegetation stopped short at Mrs Boffin's footstool, and gave place to a region of sand and sawdust." That phrase — glowing vegetation — is doing remarkable work. It is faintly absurd — carpet as jungle — and yet it conveys the Boffins' genuine aspiration with complete sympathy. Dickens understood that character is revealed in the objects people surround themselves with — and that bad taste is not the same as bad character.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I love about that room is the food. On the fashionable side of the carpet, you have stuffed birds and waxen fruit under glass — hollow ornament, as Dickens calls it. But on the sand-and-sawdust side, there is — and this detail is wonderful — the best part of a large pie and likewise of a cold joint. Dickens is already telling us that the Boffins' instincts are better than their aspirations.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That contrast between real sustenance and mere display runs all the way through the novel's treatment of wealth. Consider where the Boffins' fortune actually comes from — the dust heaps, the accumulated refuse of a city. Dickens places enormous moral weight on who handles that inheritance honestly and who is corrupted by it. And the novel's portrait of failed moral inheritance is perhaps most devastating in the Lammles. There's a passage near the end of the novel — their final exit — that Dickens strips down to almost nothing. "They walked arm-in-arm, showily enough, but without appearing to interchange a syllable." Showily enough — that is the whole biography of a fraudulent marriage in two words. And then Dickens delivers the sentence that I think is one of his finest endings for a character. "In turning the street corner they might have turned out of this world, for anything Mr and Mrs Boffin ever saw of them to the contrary." They vanish — not into death, not into ruin — simply out of the world's moral sight.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
That sentence has the quality of a judgment delivered by the universe rather than the narrator. Dickens doesn't need to say that they are damned. He simply removes them from the ledger of human existence. I think of Burke's phrase about the contract of the generations — the idea that the living, the dead, and the unborn are all party to civilisation. The Lammles have broken that contract utterly — contributed nothing, taken what they could, and departed without leaving a trace. The novel sets that emptiness against the Boffins' generous, slightly absurd, genuine warmth — and the contrast is withering.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
But before we leave the question of women in this world — there's a passage I keep returning to, which offers something quite different from both the Bower and the Lammles. Lizzie Hexam and Jenny Wren — the dolls' dressmaker — spend an evening together, and Dickens gives us a scene of such quiet intimacy that it almost stops the novel's momentum. Lizzie brushes out Jenny's long fair hair, and this is what Dickens makes of that simple act. "it fell in a beautiful shower over the poor shoulders that were much in need of such adorning rain." Adorning rain. It's tender, it's physical, it's unsentimental — those poor shoulders deserve the beauty that falls on them. The friendship between Lizzie and Jenny is one of the truest things in the novel, and this passage is its emblem.

The passage that opens all of this — the Secretary reading the letters — is one of the great comic set-pieces in the novel. Dickens is doing something wonderfully precise with scale. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me is that the comedy never loses its moral precision. The Duke's letter is 'lithographed by the hundred' — a form letter, personalised only in quite another hand. The condescension is systematic, industrial, and entirely unconscious. One thinks of Burke's observation that manners are of more importance than laws — and here the manners have entirely collapsed. The postscript — 'The Steward's fee is limited to three Guineas' — that is the masterstroke. Dickens calls it 'thoughtful in the postscript,' which is about as withering a compliment as one can administer.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The institutional context here is entirely accurate, and that's part of what makes it sting. Victorian charity dinners really did work this way — noblemen lending their names, stewards paying fees, the whole apparatus generating a thin slick of respectability over what was essentially a social transaction. Boffin is being invited to buy his way into a class that will never fully admit him. Three guineas at a time.

And then there are the individual beggars — which is, I think, an even finer piece of writing. These are the corporate beggars. But there are, besides, the individual beggars; and how does the heart of the Secretary fail him when he has to cope with them! And they must be coped with to some extent, because they all enclose documents (they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf), the non-return of which would be their ruin. That is to say, they are utterly ruined now, but they would be more utterly ruined then. Among these correspondents are several daughters of general officers, long accustomed to every luxury of life (except spelling), who little thought, when their gallant fathers waged war in the Peninsula, that they would ever have to appeal to those whom Providence, in its inscrutable wisdom, has blessed with untold gold, and from among whom they select the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, for a maiden effort in this wise, understanding that he has such a heart as never was. The Secretary learns, too, that confidence between man and wife would seem to obtain but rarely when virtue is in distress, so numerous are the wives who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted husbands, who would never permit it; while, on the other hand, so numerous are the husbands who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted wives, who would instantly go out of their senses if they had the least suspicion of the circumstance. There are the inspired beggars, too. These were sitting, only yesterday evening, musing over a fragment of candle which must soon go out and leave them in the dark for the rest of their nights, when surely some Angel whispered the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, to their souls, imparting rays of hope, nay confidence, to which they had long been strangers! Akin to these are the suggestively-befriended beggars. They were partaking of a cold potato and water by the flickering and gloomy light of a lucifer-match, in their lodgings (rent considerably in arrear, and heartless landlady threatening expulsion ‘like a dog’ into the streets), when a gifted friend happening to look in, said, ‘Write immediately to Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire,’ and would take no denial. There are the nobly independent beggars too. These, in the days of their abundance, ever regarded gold as dross, and have not yet got over that only impediment in the way of their amassing wealth, but they want no dross from Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire; No, Mr Boffin; the world may term it pride, paltry pride if you will, but they wouldn’t take it if you offered it; a loan, sir—for fourteen weeks to the day, interest calculated at the rate of five per cent per annum, to be bestowed upon any charitable institution you may name—is all they want of you, and if you have the meanness to refuse it, count on being despised by these great spirits. There are the beggars of punctual business-habits too. These will make an end of themselves at a quarter to one P.M. on Tuesday, if no Post-office order is in the interim received from Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire; arriving after a quarter to one P.M. on Tuesday, it need not be sent, as they will then (having made an exact memorandum of the heartless circumstances) be ‘cold in death.’ There are the beggars on horseback too, in another sense from the sense of the proverb. These are mounted and ready to start on the highway to affluence. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Yes, and Twemlow is the perfect place to begin, because he is the novel's purest emblem of a man who has been entirely absorbed by the social machinery around him. Dickens introduces him not as a person but as an object. Listen to the precision of the metaphor — and to what it costs Twemlow personally. "Being first cousin to Lord Snigsworth, he was in frequent requisition, and at many houses might be said to represent the dining-table in its normal state." The dining-table in its normal state — that is to say, empty. Twemlow exists to be extended — and the more the Veneerings extend him, the further he finds himself from the centre. That is not satire of a class system, precisely — it is a portrait of a man who has consented to his own erasure. The moral question Dickens poses is always: what is a man willing to become?

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
With the simplest thing in the book, I think — and therefore, perhaps, the most profound. We meet the Boffins early on, and Dickens gives us this exchange between husband and wife. 'Yes; and it's pleasant to know that you are Mrs Boffin, and it's been a pleasant thing to know this many and many a year!' And then Dickens adds — with that perfectly placed satirical stab — that this warmth was, as he puts it, ruin to Mrs Boffin's aspirations. They sit side by side, he tells us, a hopelessly Unfashionable pair. That capital U is doing extraordinary work — the whole comedy of social ambition punctured in a single adjective. And yet the passage is tender, not mocking — that is Dickens's most difficult trick, and he brings it off beautifully.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Which is exactly what the dead old miser perceived — and what makes his will so extraordinary a document. Dickens takes us inside the mind of old John Harmon — this dead jailer, as he calls him — and what he gives us is almost a confession from beyond the grave. The passage is worth attending to carefully. 'He had perceived the powerlessness of all his wealth to buy them if he had addressed himself to the attempt.' Powerlessness — applied to wealth — is a word that would have startled Dickens's first readers. And yet the logic is absolute: there are people who simply cannot be purchased. The miser knows it — and hates them for it — and trusts them entirely.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That legal structure is worth pausing on, because it is genuinely remarkable. Old Harmon is a man of absolute legal power over his household — and yet he inscribes that power's limits into his will. He cannot compel the Boffins' loyalty in life, so he bequeaths them the means to exercise it after his death. There is something almost — not quite, but almost — admirable in that final acknowledgement. Dickens is careful to say the miser mistrusted all who bore any resemblance to himself — which is a devastating psychological aside.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And then, in the sharpest possible contrast, there is Bella. The scene where Rokesmith rushes into the counting-house is one of the great comic-romantic moments in the whole novel. Dickens piles on the breathless syntax — that triple repetition of 'not only' — until it becomes almost farcical. And then he reads to us what Rokesmith cries out. 'My dear, dear girl; my gallant, generous, disinterested, courageous, noble girl!' Six adjectives — six! — and the man is barely across the threshold. But then Bella lifts her head and lays it on his breast — as if that were her head's chosen and lasting resting-place — and the comedy becomes something else entirely. It becomes a kind of homing — a life finding its right place at last.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Bella's arc is, I think, the moral centre of the novel — more so, even, than the inheritance plot. She begins as a creature of vanity — not a vicious vanity, but an unreflective one — and earns her way, through suffering, to genuine generosity. Rokesmith's litany — gallant, generous, disinterested, courageous, noble — is not sentimental exaggeration. By the time we reach that scene, she has earned each word.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Which makes Bradley Headstone's fate all the more harrowing by contrast. Headstone is a man who has clawed his way up from the working poor through sheer will and discipline — and it destroys him. The system rewarded him with respectability and gave him no instrument for managing what lay beneath it. And then we get that passage — Bradley sitting through the night, Riderhood watching. 'Rigid before the fire, as if it were a charmed flame that was turning him old, he sat, with the dark lines deepening in his face, its stare becoming more and more haggard.' The fire that should warm him instead ages him — it is consuming him from the inside. That image of being overspread with ashes — it is not a metaphor Dickens reaches for carelessly.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The sentence does something extraordinary at the level of pure sound. 'The very texture and colour of his hair degenerating' — that word, degenerating, placed at the very end, falls like a closing door. Bradley Headstone is not merely ruined — he is being unmade. And what Dickens implies, I think, is that the self-made man carries within him the mechanism of his own unmaking. It is one of the most morally serious passages in the book — all the more so for its silence.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Not one other word did Bradley utter all that night — Dickens tells us that, too. The silence is its own kind of eloquence — a man who has lost all language for what he has become. What strikes me, holding these four passages together, is how Dickens insists that character is destiny — but he means it in absolutely opposite ways for different people. For the Boffins, their character is their salvation — their incorruptible simplicity protects them from everything the novel throws at them. For Bella, character is something she must discover and then choose — and the choosing is the whole story. For Bradley, character is the trap — the thing he built to survive that finally turns and devours him.
