Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What it does — even now, even knowing the story — is make the hair on my arms stand up. The setup is this: there is a crippled lady, long dead, who swore a curse on the Dedlock house. She had walked the terrace every day despite her failing body — up and down, up and down, with greater difficulty every day. And when her husband finally found her fallen on the pavement, she did not weep or plead. She looked at him, Dickens says, fixedly and coldly — and spoke these words: "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" Four sentences — and they land like hammer blows. The rhythm escalates — each sentence a little more terrible than the last — and that final imperative, let the Dedlocks listen, is chilling every single time.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And Dickens compounds it, you see — he doesn't just give us the origin legend and leave it there. He gives us the housekeeper, Mrs. Rouncewell, passing this story on — and the moment she does so is genuinely theatrical. Before we move on, I do want to mention one more passage — because it is the comic obverse of everything we've just said, and Dickens needs both registers to be Dickens. The Vholes passages are, for my money, among the most technically accomplished satirical writing in the English novel. The target is not merely a corrupt lawyer — it is the institutional logic that makes corruption respectable.
