Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It is, isn't it — and what's so striking is that it comes from Boffin, who is not a figure you expect to break your heart. He's warm, he's comic, he's the Golden Dustman — and yet here he carries this memory with a gravity that is absolutely earned. What Dickens is doing structurally is using Boffin's plain vernacular voice to deliver something almost unbearable. The passage builds through accumulation — the little scanty clothes, the little scanty box, the shivering wind. That repetition of 'little scanty' is doing enormous work — it's not just poverty, it's the specific poverty of a child who has been given the bare minimum by a father who will not spare sixpence for coach-money. And then Dickens gives us this extraordinary moment — let me read it, because you really have to hear it. "Mrs Boffin, then quite a young woman and pictur of a full-blown rose, stands him by her, kneels down at the fire, warms her two open hands, and falls to rubbing his cheeks; but seeing the tears come into the child's eyes, the tears come fast into her own, and she holds him round the neck, like as if she was protecting him, and cries to me, 'I'd give the wide wide world, I would, to run away with him!'" The syntax itself enacts the gesture — it's one long movement, each clause folding into the next, like her arms folding around that child.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is quite right about the syntax, and I'd go further: that passage earns its place in the novel's moral architecture precisely because it is not ironic. Dickens is often accused of sentimentality, and not always unfairly. But what he is doing here is something altogether more serious — he is showing us goodness, genuine goodness, lodged in the heart of a woman who has no social or literary pretension whatsoever. Mrs Boffin's impulse to protect — to shield the child against a world that will not shelter him — is the novel's moral centre declared early and without equivocation. And notice what Boffin says at the end of the memory — that it 'gently wore out, as many things do.' "So of a night, when it was very cold, or when the wind roared, or the rain dripped heavy, she would wake sobbing, and call out in a fluster, 'Don't you see the poor child's face? O shelter the poor child!'—till in course of years it gently wore out, as many things do." That final clause is the truest thing in the passage — grief does wear out, and Dickens does not flinch from saying so, which saves the whole thing from becoming mawkish.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund's word 'shelter' lands very precisely, because what Dickens is describing here is not merely emotional deprivation — it is the literal condition of a child with no legal protection from a monstrous father. Old Harmon's refusal to allow sixpence for coach-money is not comic miserliness — in the context of Victorian parental authority, it is a small demonstration of near-absolute power. A father could send a child of seven to a foreign school, alone, in the cold, with a scanty box — and the law regarded that as entirely his prerogative. Boffin and Mrs Boffin are servants — they can warm the child's cheeks, but they cannot stop the steamboat. And the whole novel, in a sense, is haunted by that helplessness — by what it costs to stand next to suffering and be powerless to alter it.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And yet Dickens also shows us that powerlessness becoming, over time, a kind of purposefulness — because that memory is exactly what motivates the Boffins when the inheritance arrives. The past doesn't just haunt in this novel — it shapes action. Which brings me to a passage later in the novel that I find almost impossibly charming — the supper scene in Chapter Forty-Nine, which seems to arrive from a completely different emotional register. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and Bradley is the novel's most terrifying argument about what the world does to ambition when it has nowhere clean to go. The passage from Chapter Sixty-Five is brief — almost surgical — and it delivers its verdict on Bradley with a cruelty that I think rivals anything in the book. Bradley has tried to murder Eugene Wrayburn to prevent him from marrying Lizzie Hexam — and the irony of what he has actually achieved is laid out with clinical precision. Dickens writes — "For, then he saw that through his desperate attempt to separate those two for ever, he had been made the means of uniting them." And then comes the line that I find almost unbearable in its compression — "That he had dipped his hands in blood, to mark himself a miserable fool and tool." Fool and tool — the rhyme is not accidental, and it is not cheap. It reduces a man of desperate passion to an instrument — something used by fate, or Providence, or whatever we want to call the novel's governing irony.

What I keep coming back to is the structural irony Dickens has constructed around Bella's transformation.
