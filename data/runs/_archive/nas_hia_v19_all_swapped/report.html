Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't disagree with either of you on that passage — it's one of the most startling moments in Victorian fiction. But I want to press on what that line reveals structurally, not just emotionally. Higgins is a union man, a striker, a man the mill system has ground very hard — and yet his most intimate moment is addressed not to an equal but to the clergyman's daughter from the South. The novel keeps placing its most radical feeling across a class line — and I think Gaskell knows that is the problem she is writing about. Because later in the novel, Thornton himself arrives at a conviction that I find astonishing coming from a mill-master. He says — and this is Gaskell giving him his most serious speech — "No mere institutions, however wise, can attach class to class as they should be attached, unless the working out of such institutions bring the individuals of the different classes into actual personal contact." He is dismantling the very logic of top-down reform — the idea that you can legislate your way to human connection. That is a more radical position than most of Thornton's contemporaries would have dared to articulate.

Chapter 51
“Because I believe them to be such. I am not sure of the consequences that may result from them. But I am sure they ought to be tried. I have arrived at the conviction that no mere institutions, however wise, and however much thought may have been required to organise and arrange them, can attach class to class as they should be attached, unless the working out of such institutions bring the individuals of the different classes into actual personal contact. Such intercourse is the very breath of life. A working man can hardly be made to feel and know how much his employer may have laboured in his study at plans for the benefit of his workpeople. A complete plan emerges like a piece of machinery, apparently fitted for every emergency. But the hands accept it as they do machinery, without understanding the intense mental labour and forethought required to bring it to such perfection. But I would take an idea, the working out of which would necessitate personal intercourse; it might not go well at first, but at every hitch interest would be felt by an increasing number of men, and at last its success in working come to be desired by all, as all had borne a part in the formation of the plan; and even then I am sure that it would lose its vitality, cease to be living, as soon as it was no longer carried on by that sort of common interest which invariably makes people find means and ways of seeing each other, and becoming acquainted with each others’ characters and persons, and even tricks of temper and modes of speech. We should understand each other better, and I’ll venture to say we should like each other more.”

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And Gaskell earns that speech by showing us the practice of it first — which is what I love about her architecture. Before Thornton makes his grand argument, we see him actually doing the thing — going into the workers' dining room, accepting the invitation to eat. And the detail that slays me is the hot-pot. The men don't invite him to a formal conference — they say, "Master, there's hot-pot for dinner to-day, win yo' come in?" Lancashire dialect, entirely unsentimental, the most domestic possible invitation — and it is the hinge on which a whole class relationship turns. Gaskell is the great novelist of the small gesture that contains the large argument.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right — and this is where I part company with those who read the novel as primarily a tract. Thornton's self-discipline in that dining room episode — his care not to intrude, to follow rather than lead — is a portrait of a very specific kind of English virtue. Gaskell notes that he was scrupulous to leave the men free — not to impose his own judgment. That is not a political programme. That is a man learning to be good in a new situation. And that, I would submit, is the deepest subject of this novel — not class, but character.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right to linger on the comedy — but I think what makes this scene genuinely fine is the moral seriousness underneath it. Margaret has a headache. She does not wish to be there. She finds Thornton presumptuous. And yet — here is the thing — she performs her duty with absolute fidelity. Gaskell gives us that marvellous line about the cup of tea — let me find it precisely — "She handed him his cup of tea with the proud air of an unwilling slave." An unwilling slave — who nonetheless watches for the exact moment he requires a second cup. That is a study in the discipline of character — of self-mastery in spite of feeling. Gaskell is not satirising duty here. She is honouring it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
It is one of the finest pieces of physiognomy in Victorian fiction — and I use that word descriptively, not as criticism. Gaskell constructs Mr. Hale as all undulation and tremor — eyelids languid, lines soft and waving, almost feminine in their expressiveness. And then she gives us Thornton — carved in marble. She writes of those eyes — and I want to give this its full weight — "The straight brows fell low over the clear, deep-set, earnest eyes, which without being unpleasantly sharp, seemed intent enough to penetrate into the very heart and core of what he was looking at." That phrase — "the very heart and core" — is Gaskell telling us that Thornton's gaze is not brutal; it is truthful. He sees clearly. He demands that the world be equally clear-sighted. That is both his virtue and, for now, his limitation.

Gaskell has tremendous fun with Mrs. Shaw — I don't think one should sentimentalise that or pretend it's something graver. But what strikes me is that the comedy is doing serious moral work. The passage gives us a woman who has organised her life around a comfortable untruth — that she married without love, and suffers for it — and then built her daughter's life on a romantic ideal she cannot herself sustain. Edmund is right about the force of that word — but I want to note what Mrs. Thornton is actually defending.
