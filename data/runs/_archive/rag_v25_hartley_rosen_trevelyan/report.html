Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The opening of Bleak House is one of the great acts of literary nerve in the nineteenth century. Dickens doesn't open with a character, or a plot, or even a scene in any conventional sense. He opens with a condition — fog, mud, and the Court of Chancery — as if the atmosphere itself is the protagonist. And then, almost as a structural counterweight, he gives us Esther Summerson — warm, domestic, first-person. The dual narration is the novel's governing architectural choice, and it's breathtaking in its ambition. Esther, for instance, has this extraordinary habit of talking to her own reflection — literally addressing herself in the mirror. "When you are mistress of Bleak House, you are to be as cheerful as a bird." She's coaching herself — performing duty to her own face — and it's comic and heartbreaking at the same time. That double register — the cheerfulness that has to be willed into existence — tells us everything about Esther's situation before Dickens has to explain a word of it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me is how early Dickens establishes that the fog isn't metaphor — it's mechanism. Chancery produces fog the way a factory produces smoke — as a by-product of what it actually does, which is consume people. And the people it consumes are not random — they are always the ones with the least power to resist. Take Sir Leicester Dedlock — the system's great beneficiary and its most eloquent sleepwalker. Dickens gives us that magnificent, savage line about his dependents: "He supposes all his dependents to be utterly bereft of individual characters, intentions, or opinions." That is a portrait of class ideology rendered in a single sentence. Sir Leicester isn't cruel — he's worse than cruel. He is perfectly sincere, and his sincerity is the sincerity of a man who has never once had to imagine another person as fully real.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And the secrets are never merely personal — that's the point Dickens keeps pressing. Power, in Bleak House, is always informational power — the power to know things about people who cannot afford to have them known. Tulkinghorn is not an aberration in this world — he is its logical product.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That criticism of Esther as passive never survives a close reading, I'm afraid. Dickens has engineered her narration to be almost perversely modest — and that modesty is itself a form of power. Look at how she describes Charley learning to write. "In whose hand every pen appeared to become perversely animated, and to go wrong and crooked, and to stop, and splash, and sidle into corners like a saddle-donkey." That is a comic sentence of real precision — the rhythm of accumulation, those verbs stacking up, and then the saddle-donkey landing with a little thud of delight. Esther is not passive — she is observant to a degree that borders on the uncanny. And then Dickens turns it: "It was very odd to see what old letters Charley's young hand had made, they so wrinkled, and shrivelled, and tottering, it so plump and round." That juxtaposition — old letters, young hand — is doing something quite dark underneath the comedy. It's Dickens reminding us that Charley has been aged by poverty, by service, before she's had a childhood.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that's what the dual narration gives Dickens that a single voice never could. Esther's chapters create intimacy, warmth, attachment — you live inside her perception. Look at how she describes Bleak House itself when they first arrive. She gives us room by room — Ada's flowers, the surprised birds on the sitting-room walls, the oval engravings of the months. And then the sentence opens up into something almost breathless. "With its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw." The semicolons are doing structural work there — each clause is a new sense impression, arriving in waves. Dickens is building the feeling of home incrementally, each clause adding to the warmth until it becomes overwhelming. And then the omniscient narrator swoops in and shows you Chancery — and the contrast is devastating.
