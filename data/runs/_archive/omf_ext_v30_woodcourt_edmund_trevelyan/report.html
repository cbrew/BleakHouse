Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes one immediately about this novel — and what one never quite recovers from — is the sheer comprehensiveness of its satirical vision. Dickens is not content to satirise the wealthy or the corrupt in isolation. He insists on showing us the whole ecosystem — every human variety that money, and the hope of money, calls into being. There is a passage — assigned to me with I think considerable mischief — in which the Secretary catalogues the beggars who write to Mr Boffin after his inheritance becomes known. And Dickens anatomises them with a taxonomist's precision and a comedian's relish. The detail that arrests me — and I confess I laughed aloud when I first read it — is his description of their enclosures. He writes: "they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf." Minced veal to a calf. The simile is grotesque, precise, and utterly devastating — and it does in seven words what a lesser writer would need a paragraph to achieve. Now, one could read this passage as social polemic — an indictment of poverty and desperation. I think that reading is, frankly, too earnest. This is Dickens delighting in human variety — in the extraordinary ingenuity with which people construct their fictions of entitlement.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Sir Edmund, I am completely with you on the comedy — but I think the comedy and the tragedy are inseparable here, and that's precisely what makes it so extraordinary to read aloud. Because when you perform that passage — and I have performed it — you feel the double register operating in real time. The 'beggars of punctual business-habits' who announce they will make an end of themselves at a quarter to one P.M. on Tuesday — that is comic writing of the highest order. The specificity of the time is the joke — the absurd precision of the desperate. But then you pause, and you think: these are people threatening suicide in their letters. And the novel holds both things simultaneously — the ridiculous and the truly wretched — without resolving the tension. That is not something many novelists can do.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Caroline touches on something I think is central to the architecture of the whole novel. The fortune itself — the Harmon fortune — has been constructed from dust. And the will that governs its disposal is, in its own way, as satirical a document as any of those begging letters. There is a second will — discovered by the villainous Wegg — and its terms, as Wegg reads them aloud, are almost a parody of testamentary language. John Harmon's own words, as ventriloquised by Wegg: "Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown." 'Quite enough for him.' The contempt in that phrase is extraordinary — a man disposing of a vast fortune as though distributing leftovers, and doing so with the air of conferring a favour. The elder Harmon was, by every account in the novel, a thoroughly unpleasant man — and his will is written in his own image.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Oliver, that connection is exactly right — and it points to something the novel does which I find almost uncanny. Every character in this book, at some level, is performing a version of themselves for an audience of money. And yet Dickens keeps interrupting this human comedy with these extraordinary passages of pure, almost impersonal description. There's a passage — one I return to again and again — where the novel simply gives us the train crossing the Thames. No characters. No plot. Just the thing itself. "the train rattled among the house-tops, and among the ragged sides of houses torn down to make way for it, and over the swarming streets, and under the fruitful earth, until it shot across the river: bursting over the quiet surface like a bomb-shell, and gone again as if it had exploded in the rush of smoke and steam and glare." Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The Boffins present Dickens at his most morally precise, and I think that precision is often misread as sentimentality. Consider the passage where Boffin addresses his wife — there is no grand gesture, no eloquence, only simple and absolute loyalty. He says — and this deserves to be savoured — 'Yes; and it's pleasant to know that you are Mrs Boffin, and it's been a pleasant thing to know this many and many a year!' Dickens then delivers the sting with perfect economy: it was ruin to Mrs Boffin's aspirations, and they sat side by side, a hopelessly Unfashionable pair. Note the capital U on Unfashionable — Dickens's quiet mockery of a world that would regard this happiness as failure. What Dickens is saying, I believe, is that goodness is its own sufficiency — and that no fortune can improve upon it.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
And what that passage reveals, I think, is something Burke would have recognised: the tyranny of an inheritance poorly conceived. Harmon's father has not merely left money — he has left a moral condition, a kind of corruption that radiates outward. The novel makes it very clear, in Harmon's own words, that he knew 'nothing but wretchedness that my father's wealth had ever brought about.' 'Knowing of nothing but wretchedness that my father's wealth had ever brought about.' This is not a critique of capitalism — it is a critique of a particular man's failure of paternal love. The money is merely the instrument of a cold and loveless will.

And there is one further layer of disguise in this novel that we cannot quite leave unexamined. Welcome back to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked. We've been talking about the novel's preoccupation with money, with the river, with the weight of the Harmon inheritance. Now we want to turn to something equally central: the performance of identity. In this novel, almost nobody is quite who they appear to be — and Dickens milks that, for comedy, for pathos, and for something genuinely unsettling.
