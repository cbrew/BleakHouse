James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me, coming at this as a legal historian rather than a literary critic, is quite how accurate that fog metaphor is as a description of how Chancery actually functioned. The Court of Chancery in the 1850s was a genuine catastrophe — cases that had been running for decades, costs that devoured estates long before any judgment arrived. Dickens wasn't exaggerating for comic or satirical effect — if anything, he was being conservative. And this matters enormously for how we read the novel's famous opening claim — that Jarndyce and Jarndyce has become a cause so complicated that no man alive knows what it means. Dickens puts it with wonderful deadpan precision — "Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. This scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means." James Blackstone (social_historian)
I think Edmund is right to focus on the temporal dimension — the way Richard lives in a permanent, deferred future. And as a legal historian I have to say — the Court of Chancery was specifically structured to produce exactly that effect. Chancery suits in the nineteenth century could run for decades — fifty years was not unusual. There was no mechanism for expedition, no incentive to settle — only the fee income of the court's own officers. So when Dickens writes, and he does write it with surgical fury, that Chancery has its decaying houses and blighted lands in every shire — he is not exaggerating for effect. He is describing a documented institutional pathology. What happens to Richard is what Chancery was — structurally, systematically — designed to do to people like him.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And there's a class dimension here that I think we have to name. Richard is a ward in Chancery — which means he is a young man whose claim to property is entirely mediated by the law. He has no trade, no profession he can commit to — because the suit promises to render all that unnecessary. He tries medicine, he tries the law, he tries the army — and Dickens is precise about this, each trial shorter and more erratic than the last. The suit has colonised his imagination, and the result is that he cannot form what we might call a relationship to productive labour. Esther captures it when she observes — and this is one of those moments where Dickens hides an indictment inside a gentle sentence — 'He had been educated in no habits of application and concentration.'
