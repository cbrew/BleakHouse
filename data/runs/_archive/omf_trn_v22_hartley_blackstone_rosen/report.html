Thank you — and yes, the Poor Law passage is, I think, one of the most remarkable moments of authorial intrusion in Victorian fiction. Dickens steps entirely out of the story and addresses the reader directly — almost as a pamphleteer — to state his position on legislation that was destroying people's lives. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is how it closes: "the illegality is quite equal to the inhumanity — and known language could say no more of their lawlessness." Dickens is refusing the separation that apologists always reach for — the idea that a law can be cruel but still legitimate. He's saying: the cruelty is the lawlessness. And notice who he's arguing with — the "Circumlocutional champions," the defenders of the system who dismiss any complaint as an impossible demand for turtle soup from gold spoons. It's a very precise image of how the powerful deflect accountability — by caricaturing the demand for basic human dignity as some kind of outrageous luxury.

The Sprodgkin passage is interesting precisely because Dickens is gentle with the Milveys but still locates the absurdity. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That phrase — "a floor of grief" — is exactly right, and it brings me to the Bella and Boffin passage, which is the passage I find most structurally audacious in this group. Dickens has spent many chapters showing us Mr Boffin apparently corrupted by his inheritance — miserly, cold, dismissive of Bella. And now, in a single scene, he reveals it was a performance — a moral education staged for Bella's benefit. What's remarkable is that Bella herself articulates the logic — she speaks it aloud, almost as a theorem, holding him by the coat. "This shallow creature would never work the truth out of her own weak soul, if she had a hundred years to do it in; but a glaring instance kept before her may open even her eyes and set her thinking." She is quoting, in her own excited rush of comprehension, what she imagines Boffin said to himself about her. It's a double ventriloquism — Dickens, through Bella, speaking Boffin's imagined interior — and it lands with extraordinary tenderness.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Thank you — and yes, this is the novel where I think Dickens most consciously plays the author as trickster. There's a remarkable moment — and Dickens puts it right there in the body of the text, not in a preface — where he breaks open the fourth wall entirely. He writes, and this is verbatim: "I foresaw the likelihood that a class of readers and commentators would suppose that I was at great pains to conceal exactly what I was at great pains to suggest: namely, that Mr John Harmon was not slain, and that Mr John Rokesmith was he." What's so thrilling about that sentence is the chiasmus at its heart — conceal and suggest, inverted, mirroring each other. The technique of the novel and the technique of the sentence are performing the same trick simultaneously. And then he does something even bolder — he asks the reader to trust him, to grant the artist patience.
