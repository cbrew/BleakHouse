James Blackstone (social_historian)
The England Gaskell is writing about in the early eighteen-fifties is one in the middle of a seismic shift in how power is organised. The old hierarchies — land, church, gentry — are being elbowed aside by men who made their money with machinery and will. And Gaskell, to her credit, refuses to caricature either side. Take Margaret's very first real assessment of Thornton. She has just met this northern manufacturer, and her verdict is this: With such an expression of resolution and power, no face, however plain in feature, could be either vulgar or common. I should not like to have to bargain with him; he looks very inflexible. Altogether a man who seems made for his niche, mamma; sagacious, and strong, as becomes a great tradesman. Notice how much weight she gives to that word — tradesman. It is admiring, and it is also, just faintly, condescending. That ambivalence is Gaskell's whole subject.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me is how quickly that ambivalence gets replaced by something far grimmer. Because the industrial world Gaskell is building around Margaret is not just interesting and alien — it is brutal. There's a passage later in the novel where Margaret is trying to stop Higgins — a mill worker, a union man — from fleeing south to agricultural labour. And her description of what that southern life would do to him is devastating. She says: Those that have lived there all their lives, are used to soaking in the stagnant waters. They labour on from day to day, in the great solitude of steaming fields — never speaking or lifting up their poor, bent, downcast heads. That image — the bent, downcast head — is one of the most quietly heartbreaking in all of Victorian fiction. Gaskell is not romanticising the rural south. She is indicting it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that indictment carries enormous historical weight when you understand the actual conditions of agricultural labour in mid-Victorian England. The wages Margaret mentions — ten shillings — were real. For a family of the size Higgins is responsible for, that represented subsistence at best, destitution in a bad week. But there's a further irony in all this that I find extraordinary. The very man who dissuades Higgins from emigrating — Thornton — later refuses to give him work on explicitly moral grounds. Higgins has offered to accept lower wages than the going rate, out of care for another man's children. And Thornton turns him down with this: You'd be a knobstick. You'd be taking less wages than the other labourers — all for the sake of another man's children. Think how you'd abuse any poor fellow who was willing to take what he could get to keep his own children. You and your Union would soon be down upon him. Thornton is forcing Higgins to confront the logic of collective labour principles. You cannot benefit from solidarity and then undercut it when it suits you. Gaskell understood labour economics more clearly than most of her contemporaries, and she puts that understanding into the mouth of a mill-owner.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to add a layer of material reality to what both of you have said. Gaskell is precise about why Mrs. Hale is declining: the air itself, the domestic worries in a new and sordid form. Milton's atmosphere in the eighteen-fifties was genuinely, measurably poisonous — cotton fluff, coal smoke, effluent. When Gaskell writes that the air is 'deprived of all revivifying principle,' that is not poetic license — it is public health observation. And note what Margaret's response is: she lies awake planning how to hire another servant. She can do nothing about the industrial air. She can do something about the domestic labour. That is a very Gaskellian form of agency — constrained, practical, and quietly radical.

Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
The line 'the vulgarity of shop-people' is magnificent, because it exposes Margaret's own class snobbery in the very act of praising Thornton for not having it. The Bessy scenes are where Gaskell allows herself to be openly biblical, and it works because she earns it.
