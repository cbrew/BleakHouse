Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes, and it's one of those moments where you feel Eliot's hand most clearly on the wheel. She's describing Tom coming home from school — which on the surface is a small, domestic detail. But then the narrator lifts completely out of the scene and into something much larger. The sentence she gives us is one of the great formulations in Victorian fiction — listen to how it works. "There is no sense of ease like the ease we felt in those scenes where we were born, where objects became dear to us before we had known the labour of choice." "The labour of choice" — that phrase is doing enormous work. Home is the one place we didn't choose — it chose us — and that prior-ness is exactly what makes it irreplaceable. The whole novel is, in some sense, a meditation on that one idea.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is quite right, and I'd press the point even further. What Eliot is describing is not mere sentimentality about furniture and hearths — though she's shrewd enough to acknowledge that that furniture might look shabby at auction. She is describing the very mechanism by which human beings are constituted as moral beings — rooted in particular loves, particular places. Burke understood this, of course — the little platoon before the grand abstraction. And Eliot gives it this perfectly judged formulation about the elderberry bush. "There is no better reason for preferring this elderberry bush than that it stirs an early memory... it is the long companion of my existence, that wove itself into my joys when joys were vivid." "When joys were vivid." That final clause is absolutely devastating in its simplicity. It implies, without stating, that joys are no longer vivid — that adulthood is precisely the loss of that intensity.

What strikes me — as someone who has read this novel aloud more times than I can count — is that passage is written entirely for the voice. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
It is one of the most quietly harrowing passages in the whole novel. Mrs Tulliver is not a woman of great intelligence or tragic dignity in the conventional sense — Eliot is completely clear-eyed about her limitations. And yet when she faces the loss of her linen, we feel the full weight of a life dismantled. She says, with devastating plainness — "To think o' these cloths as I spun myself... and Job Haxey wove 'em, and brought the piece home on his back, as I remember standing at the door and seeing him come, before I ever thought o' marrying your father!" The whole social fabric of her life is in those cloths — the labour, the courtship, the expectation of passing things on to her children. And the thing that undoes her entirely is that line — "You'll never have one of 'em, my boy... I meant 'em for you." The future she had planned — the future that gave the labour its meaning — has simply ceased to exist.

What I find so precise about that scene is the detail Eliot chooses to end on. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What I find essential here is the moral complexity Eliot refuses to simplify. Maggie cannot achieve simple penitence — not because she is bad, but because her sense of justice is acute. Her resentment presents itself to her — Eliot's phrase is exact — "as a just indignation." She cannot submit because submission would require her to agree that Tom was right — and Tom was not right. This is not weak will — it is the terrible price of an honest conscience. And Eliot never lets us look away from how much that costs Maggie.

“Well, well, do as you like, Bessy,” said Mr Tulliver, taking up his hat and walking out to the mill. Few wives were more submissive than Mrs Tulliver on all points unconnected with her family relations; but she had been a Miss Dodson, and the Dodsons were a very respectable family indeed,—as much looked up to as any in their own parish, or the next to it. The Miss Dodsons had always been thought to hold up their heads very high, and no one was surprised the two eldest had married so well,—not at an early age, for that was not the practice of the Dodson family. There were particular ways of doing everything in that family: particular ways of bleaching the linen, of making the cowslip wine, curing the hams, and keeping the bottled gooseberries; so that no daughter of that house could be indifferent to the privilege of having been born a Dodson, rather than a Gibson or a Watson. Funerals were always conducted with peculiar propriety in the Dodson family: the hat-bands were never of a blue shade, the gloves never split at the thumb, everybody was a mourner who ought to be, and there were always scarfs for the bearers. When one of the family was in trouble or sickness, all the rest went to visit the unfortunate member, usually at the same time, and did not shrink from uttering the most disagreeable truths that correct family feeling dictated; if the illness or trouble was the sufferer’s own fault, it was not in the practice of the Dodson family to shrink from saying so. I've read that speech aloud more times than I can count, and it never loses its power — because it's written for a voice, not a page. 'Poor little wench' — that's not literary dialect, that is a man talking to himself in the only language he has for love. But I want to bring Tom into this too, because there's a scene later that I find genuinely moving — and it involves money, of all things. Tom has been quietly trading — borrowed a little money from his uncle Glegg, said nothing to anyone, worked in secret. And then he comes to his father and says, quite simply — "I have three hundred and twenty pounds in the bank." The plainness of it is everything — no speech, no drama, just a number that means the debt is paid and the world is right again. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right — and that moment brings us to the final reckoning between Tulliver and the world that has beaten him. There is a line near the end of Mr Tulliver's life that I consider among the most honest sentences in Victorian fiction. He has asked Tom to say no more — he is exhausted by argument, by the long humiliation of failure — and he turns to his wife and says — "Give me a kiss, Bessy, and let us bear one another no ill-will; we shall never be young again — this world's been too many for me." 'This world's been too many for me' — that is not self-pity, it is the plain truth of a man who tried and was overcome. It is what Johnson called a man's honest reckoning with Providence — and Eliot honours it completely.

What I find extraordinary in that farewell speech is how Eliot gives him the imperative — 'let us bear one another no ill-will' — as if he's issuing a quiet moral instruction to everyone in the room.
