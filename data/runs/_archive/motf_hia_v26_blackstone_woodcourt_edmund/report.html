Edmund is right to stress the structural point, and I'd press it further from a legal standpoint. Caroline puts her finger on something crucial about Tom. He is not unfeeling — Eliot is at pains to make that clear throughout — but he has no vocabulary for his feeling. That image of extinguishment lands because Eliot has already, pages earlier, sketched the systemic failure that produced this outcome. The education passage — the one about Mr Stelling and the dark ages of schooling — is often treated as a digression. It is not a digression. She's doing something quite precise, and it rewards close attention. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That contrast between the siblings is where the novel's social argument and its moral argument come together most powerfully, I think. Tom's code is external — procedural, public, legible to the community. Maggie's struggle is entirely interior, invisible, almost impossible to adjudicate by any social standard. And the narrator makes quite explicit what the stakes of that interior struggle are. "She might as well hope to enjoy walking by maiming her feet, as hope to enjoy an existence in which she set out by maiming the faith and sympathy that were the best organs of her soul." That image of maiming is quite startling — self-betrayal conceived not as moral failure but as a kind of self-mutilation. Tom's society has no language for what Maggie is preserving — and that, I think, is Eliot's most searching indictment of that world.

She could have informed you that there was such a word as “polygamy,” and being also acquainted with “polysyllable,” she had deduced the conclusion that “poly” mean “many”; but she had had no idea that gypsies were not well supplied with groceries, and her thoughts generally were the oddest mixture of clear-eyed acumen and blind dreams.

And the flood, when it finally comes, doesn't discriminate between the clear-sighted and the blind. That's one of the things that makes the ending so genuinely disturbing. The river the children had loved — their own little river, the Ripple, and the great Floss beyond it — becomes the instrument of destruction. Nature in this novel is not consoling. It is indifferent — and occasionally, with the Eagre, almost malevolent. The children in that early passage sense this — they call the Eagre a hungry monster — but they cannot yet connect it to their own fate.

It was one of their happy mornings. They trotted along and sat down together, with no thought that life would ever change much for them; they would only get bigger and not go to school, and it would always be like the holidays; they would always live together and be fond of each other. And the mill with its booming; the great chestnut-tree under which they played at houses; their own little river, the Ripple, where the banks seemed like home, and Tom was always seeing the water-rats, while Maggie gathered the purple plumy tops of the reeds, which she forgot and dropped afterward; above all, the great Floss, along which they wandered with a sense of travel, to see the rushing spring-tide, the awful Eagre, come up like a hungry monster, or to see the Great Ash which had once wailed and groaned like a man, these things would always be just the same to them. Tom thought people were at a disadvantage who lived on any other spot of the globe; and Maggie, when she read about Christiana passing “the river over which there is no bridge,” always saw the Floss between the green pastures by the Great Ash.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
There is something almost Greek in that structure. The very things a character loves most become, in the end, the agents of their destruction. Maggie associates the Floss with Bunyan's river over which there is no bridge — and she is right, though not in the way she means as a child. The Floss is precisely the river she will not be able to cross — except by drowning in it. That is George Eliot at the height of her powers: prophecy rendered invisible in pastoral.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I want to end by saying something simple, which is that this novel breaks my heart every time. Not because it is tragic — many novels are tragic — but because the loss feels so specific and so unnecessary. Maggie is not undone by a flaw in her character, or not only that. She is undone by the gap between what she is and what the world she was born into could possibly make room for. And that gap — clear-eyed acumen and blind dreams, as Eliot says — that gap has never closed.

Edmund, James, Caroline — that is a remarkable note on which to close. A novel that begins with two children beside a river, believing the world will never change — and ends with the river proving them catastrophically wrong. What George Eliot gives us in The Mill on the Floss is not just a story about one family, or one woman, or one provincial town. It is a reckoning with what we lose when the world moves on and leaves certain kinds of people — certain kinds of hunger and feeling — without a place to stand.
