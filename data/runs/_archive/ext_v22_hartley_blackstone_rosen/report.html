Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Thank you — and yes, let's start with narration, because it's the strangest and most brilliant structural decision Dickens makes in this book. Bleak House has two narrators who never speak to each other. The omniscient narrator — cold, ironic, present tense, panoramic — and Esther Summerson, writing in the first person, past tense, warm and searching and quietly devastating. And what that double narration does is extraordinary — it creates a world that nobody can see whole. Esther is always catching up, always slightly inside events, limited by what she knows and what she admits she knows. The omniscient narrator sees the pattern — but tells it with this biting, almost theatrical irony that keeps the reader slightly off-balance. I want to look at a passage from Esther's narration — from Chapter Twenty-Three — because it shows you exactly what Dickens does when he puts a character under pressure through Esther's eyes. Richard Carstone is unravelling — slowly, steadily — and he half-knows it. He says to Esther, in his frankest and most feeling manner: "I understand you, and I wish to heaven I were a more constant sort of fellow...If I were a more constant sort of fellow, I should have held on either to Badger or to Kenge and Carboy like grim death, and should have begun to be steady and systematic by this time, and shouldn't be in debt, and—" That broken dash at the end — that's not just a rhetorical pause, that's a life sentence trailing off before it's finished. Dickens builds Richard's tragedy into the grammar itself. He can't finish the sentence because he can't finish the thought — and he can't finish the thought because Chancery has already begun to consume the man who could have finished it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and I want to dwell on that curse for a moment, because it's easy to read it as gothic decoration and miss what Dickens is actually doing. The Dedlocks are the hereditary aristocracy — a family whose identity is entirely invested in the permanence of their name, their house, their unbroken lineage. And Dickens reaches back into that history and plants a figure — a crippled woman, walking the terrace day by day, whose husband never gave her justice — and he makes her the novel's first ghost. Listen to what she says when she finally breaks her silence. "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" She has said nothing — not to her husband, not to anyone — since the night he wronged her. And when she does speak, it is to attach herself permanently — as a haunting — to the very stones of the place that has swallowed her. Now — historically — the great houses of England were built on exactly this kind of suppression: of women, of dependants, of anyone who had no legal standing to demand redress. Dickens makes her silence — and then her curse — the moral debt that the Dedlock name has been carrying all along.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James puts it exactly right — and I'd push one step further. The genius of that curse is the form it takes: not vengeance from without, but haunting from within. She doesn't threaten to destroy the house from outside — she says she will never leave it. The system that crushed her becomes the very ground she walks, literally and figuratively, for eternity. And that, I think, is Dickens's deepest insight about how class power perpetuates itself: it doesn't just exploit you — it makes you part of the structure. When we get to Tulkinghorn — and Eleanor's passage about Lady Dedlock is absolutely central here — we see the same mechanism working in a much colder, more deliberate way. Tulkinghorn says to Lady Dedlock — and this is extraordinary language for a lawyer: "I would as soon have hoped to root up by means of my own strength and my own hands the oldest tree on this estate as to shake your hold upon Sir Leicester." He is admiring the very fortress he intends to demolish. The image of the tree, the estate, the deep roots — this is the ideological language of aristocratic permanence. And Tulkinghorn wields it precisely because he understands that Sir Leicester's entire self-conception is rooted in that language. Knowledge is leverage — and in this novel, it is always the lawyers and the institutions who hold the knowledge, and therefore the power.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that deferral was not a literary metaphor — it was the lived experience of thousands of Chancery suitors in Dickens's own time. Cases in the Court of Chancery could run for decades — sometimes for generations. Costs accumulated, parties died, original documents were lost, and the lawyers — who were paid by the process, not the outcome — had no particular incentive to resolve anything. Richard is not a weak character who has made bad choices — he is a person the system is designed to destroy.
