Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
One always feels, returning to the opening of Bleak House, that Dickens has somehow changed the weather of English fiction permanently. Before those first pages, fog was something that happened to characters. After them, it is a character. And yet I'd urge some caution against reading the fog purely as a symbol of systemic failure — of capitalism, or bureaucratic oppression, or whatever the current critical fashion requires. Dickens was, above all, a novelist of the particular. Consider how perfectly the fog announces itself through individual bewilderment — through Esther's eyes, specifically. She arrives in London and simply asks whether there is a great fire anywhere. "He was very obliging, and as he handed me into a fly after superintending the removal of my boxes, I asked him whether there was a great fire anywhere? James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right that Dickens works through the particular — and that's precisely what makes the historical record so illuminating here. The fog was real. London in the early 1850s burned coal on a scale that made the air genuinely lethal — especially in November, especially near the river. So when Esther looks out of that fly and sees dense brown smoke, she is describing an actual atmospheric condition that killed several thousand Londoners a year. Dickens doesn't have to choose between the literal and the symbolic — the literal is already damning enough. And Chancery itself had a similarly lethal quality — not metaphorically, but materially. Suits in Chancery could run for sixty years. Jarndyce and Jarndyce was based on a real case — Jennings v. Jennings — which consumed its litigants across three generations. When Esther asks innocently whether there is a great fire, the irony runs very deep indeed. There is always a great fire. It simply burns people rather than buildings.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver's instinct there is exactly right — and it illustrates precisely why I resist purely sociological readings of this novel. The fog in Bleak House is not only a critique of institutions. It is a condition of human perception — of what we can and cannot see about ourselves. Richard cannot see what he is becoming. Lady Dedlock cannot see — or refuses to see — what her past is about to do to her present. And Esther — our clearest-eyed narrator — asks whether there is a great fire, and is told nothing. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There is a moment I keep returning to — Esther trying to reach Mrs. Jellyby, which is its own kind of fog, of course. James Blackstone (social_historian)
He's escaping from the reality of the system that has already swallowed Gridley, that is swallowing Richard Carstone, and that hovers over almost every character in the novel with quiet menace. Weevle is, of course, Tony Jobling — a Chancery hanger-on, a young man of no prospects waiting, like so many in this world, for something to turn up. And what strikes me as the historian is this: fashion journalism was genuinely the opiate of the middling sort in the 1850s. The illustrated papers — the picture galleries — offered precisely the same intoxication of glamour that Weevle is consuming with his borrowed newspaper. Now consider what sits behind Weevle in this same chapter — Smallweed, Grandfather Smallweed, with his money and his ledgers and his perfectly preserved grievances. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And what makes that scene historically significant as well as dramatically powerful is what it reveals about obligation and honour among the working poor. The Bagnets — Quebec, Malta, Woolwich — they are not people of means. Their security is relational, not financial. And then we have the passage about Rouncewell's townsman — the ironmaster's connection — which operates on a completely different class register. This is the scene where the working-class father pulls his daughter away from Lady Dedlock's patronage — and Dickens' framing of it is genuinely startling. I find that passage more complex than Oliver suggests — not that he is wrong, but I think Dickens is genuinely uncertain here, as he rarely is. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund's reading is generous to the father, and I understand it — but I'd push back, slightly. The legal and social historian in me notes that 'condescension' in the Victorian sense was a technical as well as a social term. For the aristocracy to extend patronage was to assert the naturalness of hierarchy — to say: I can raise you, because it is my right to do so. The father's refusal is therefore not merely pride — it is a refusal of the entire social grammar that makes such condescension possible. Rouncewell the ironmaster, of course, enacts the same refusal at Chesney Wold — and Dickens clearly regards him as a figure of the future.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a Snagsby parallel worth drawing here — different register entirely, but the same mechanism. Snagsby, you'll recall, gives half a crown to Jo — a small, genuine act of human decency. But when he tells the story, he omits it — again and again, he suppresses the half-crown fact. Dickens gives us the exact phrasing twice: suppressing the half-crown fact. "And Mr. Snagsby again relates his experience, again suppressing the half-crown fact." The repetition is the story — the compulsive, anxious, self-protective suppression. Snagsby fears his wife. But the novel is asking us to consider what it costs — to a man and to a child — when small decencies are hidden because the social cost of owning them is too high.

The half-crown is one of those Dickensian details that seems trivial and is absolutely not. Snagsby has a good heart — Dickens never lets us forget that.
