Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close, unhurried look at what many consider the greatest of Dickens's novels. Our Mutual Friend was published in monthly parts between 1864 and 1865 — the last completed novel Dickens would write. It is a novel about money — about what money does to people, what people do for money, and who gets buried under the weight of it all. It opens on a river, with a body being fished from the Thames — and it never quite leaves that dark water. Joining me today are three extraordinary readers who bring very different lenses to this remarkable book. Oliver Trevelyan has narrated more classic novels on audio than perhaps anyone alive. He comes to Dickens as a performer first — hearing the rhythms, the comic timing, the voices that were always written to be heard rather than simply read. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don — a traditionalist who reads novels as moral investigations of character under pressure. He will keep us honest about what the novel is actually doing with its people — as opposed to what we might wish it were doing. And Dr Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads every novel as an anatomy of class and power. In a novel built on dust heaps and inheritance and the labour of the forgotten, his perspective is — I'd argue — indispensable. We begin today with the novel's strange foundations — its dust, its money, and the question of what goodness looks like in a world built on filth.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me, returning to this novel after many years, is how persistently Dickens insists that goodness is real — not a social construct, not a performance, but a force. And he makes a quite extraordinary claim about that goodness — that it cannot ultimately be suppressed, even by the worst circumstances.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to begin with exactly the passage Edmund is gesturing at, because I think it contains one of the most remarkable sentences in the whole novel. Dickens is describing the Boffins — these two people whom their former master had wrung dry of work for as little money as he could pay. He describes their master as — and this is the text — 'the hard wrathful and sordid nature that had wrung as much work out of them as could be got in their best days, for as little money as could be paid.' 'For as little money as could be paid to hurry on their worst.' That is a description of exploitation — precise, unflinching, and delivered without any softening. And then he writes this — listen to it — 'For, Evil often stops short at itself and dies with the doer of it; but Good, never.' That is not a sentimental claim — it is a theological one, and Dickens means every word of it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I respect the distinction, but I think the novel itself refuses it. Look at what Dickens actually gives us in the chapter that follows — and this is Harmon reconstructing the logic of his own deception. He doesn't begin with individual conscience — he begins with a system: 'the distrust engendered by his wretched childhood and the action for evil — never yet for good within his knowledge then — of his father and his father's wealth on all within their influence.' 'All within their influence.' This isn't one man's private failing. The wealth acts on everyone it touches — it is structural, not personal. Harmon's 'moral timidity,' as Dickens calls it, is the product of that system, not a flaw in his character that he must overcome by individual willpower.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'm not sure 'tentative' exonerates the plan, Edmund. Harmon's scheme still treats Bella as an object to be evaluated — a specimen in a test he has designed entirely without her consent. The class dimension matters too: he dresses as a common sailor to observe a woman who is herself economically powerless. What the novel does brilliantly — and this is where Dickens earns his radicalism — is show Bella becoming aware of exactly this power imbalance, from the inside. There's a moment when she's with Lizzie Hexam — a woman who has given up everything for love without prospect of return — and Bella's self-awareness breaks through. Dickens renders it as comedy — but it is devastating comedy: 'There, you little mercenary wretch! Do you hear that? Ain't you ashamed of yourself?' Oliver is quite right that the comedy is doing serious moral work — though I'd press a little further on what precedes that self-examination. Before Bella retreats to her room, Dickens gives us one of those moments of gothic economy that he does better than almost any other novelist in the language. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund's reading is elegant, but I think it lets the novel off a hook it has deliberately impaled itself on. If this were simply about individual moral character, Dickens would have no need of Lizzie Hexam — and yet Lizzie is the novel's most searching test of what redemption actually costs. Lizzie confides to Bella about Eugene — the man she loves across an unbridgeable class divide — and what she says is devastating in its precision. 'I would not have the light of them taken out of my life, for anything my life can give me.' She is holding onto the light of something she has already decided she cannot have. That is not redemption freely given — that is what the novel's social structure permits a woman of her class: not union, but the memory of light. Dickens gives Bella her comic self-reckoning in the mirror because Bella has options the system allows her. Lizzie gets something more austere — the grace of having confided it, once, and then carrying it forward alone.

Daniel has hit on something I find almost unbearably moving about that passage — Lizzie says 'my mind changed' the moment Bella walks in.
