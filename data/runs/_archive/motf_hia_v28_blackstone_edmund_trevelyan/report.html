Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
One of the first things that strikes the attentive reader is how thoroughly George Eliot earns her tragedy before a single catastrophe has occurred. The mill, the river, the flat Midland landscape — these are not mere backdrop. They are the moral weather of the book. The Floss is not a symbol of capitalism or progress or any fashionable abstraction. It is simply the condition of existence for these people — indifferent, powerful, and ultimately fatal. Burke wrote of the partnership between the dead, the living, and the yet unborn. The mill is precisely that kind of institution — and George Eliot understands that its loss is not merely financial.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right that the loss is not merely financial — though I'd press a little harder on what that financial reality actually looked like. When Tulliver loses the mill, he loses his entire social standing, his children's prospects, his wife's sanity — the lot. This was precisely how rural insolvency worked in the early nineteenth century — total, public, humiliating. And what I find remarkable is how George Eliot shows us that the children absorb this catastrophe into their very characters. Tom's relentless drive to repay the debts, Maggie's desperate need for love alongside her impossible moral conscience — these are not born in a vacuum. There's a passage I keep returning to — a small, almost throwaway moment about Tom's time at Mr Stelling's school, but it reveals something quite precise about these two children. George Eliot tells us that after Maggie's visit, Tom had actually come to a dim understanding of the fact that there had once been people upon the earth who were so fortunate as to know Latin without learning it through the medium of the Eton Grammar. That phrase — 'dim understanding' — is doing enormous work. Tom is not stupid, but his education has been designed to keep him dim — and Maggie, simply by asking questions, has let in a crack of light.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That 'Eton Grammar' line is pure Eliot comedy — deadpan, precise, and quietly devastating. The joke is not really on Tom — the joke is on the entire edifice of classical education as it was applied to boys who had no earthly use for it. Tom's acquirements, we're told, were 'otherwise confined to an epitomised history of the Jews' — I mean, that is a perfect sentence. And what strikes me as a performer — the moment when Maggie is 'fetched away in the gig by Luke' and the study becomes 'once more quite lonely for Tom.' That 'once more' carries such weight — the loneliness was always there; Maggie temporarily expelled it. And this is what George Eliot does better than almost any novelist — she understands that comedy and grief are not opposites. They are the same emotion seen from different angles, and sometimes from the same angle at once.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver puts his finger on something that I think critics of a more sociological bent tend to miss. This novel is, at its deepest level, a study of two souls — not two social positions. Tom and Maggie are bound together by love and by a kind of mutual incomprehension that is itself a form of love. The tragedy is moral, not structural — it arises from character, not from the failures of Victorian educational policy. Which is precisely why the novel still speaks to us — because human nature has not been reformed out of existence by any subsequent legislation.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I won't entirely concede the structural point — but I'll grant Edmund this: the novel's power is not reducible to its social argument. What George Eliot does — and this becomes fully visible in the later sections — is show us how the structural and the moral are the same crisis experienced from the inside. There is a passage — one of the most extraordinary in the book — where Maggie wakes on the boat, having drifted away with Stephen Guest, and the full weight of what she has done crashes in upon her. She had brought sorrow into the lives of others — into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love. That phrase — 'knit up with hers by trust and love' — is not the language of social position. It is the language of moral obligation — the web of human connection that Maggie has always understood and always, at the crucial moment, struggled to honour. And the tragedy is that her very capacity for feeling — the thing that makes her remarkable — is also what makes her most vulnerable to catastrophic failure.

And George Eliot doesn't let Maggie off easily — not for a single page. There's a passage just after that awakening scene that I think is among the most brutally honest things she ever wrote about the interior life. She asks — did Maggie lie down that night with her will bent unwaveringly on the path of penitent sacrifice? The great struggles of life are not so easy as that; the great problems of life are not so clear. And then — and this is the passage that really stays with me — the love she had renounced came back upon her with a cruel charm; she felt herself opening her arms to receive it once more; and then it seemed to slip away and fade and vanish, leaving only the dying sound of a deep, thrilling voice that said, 'Gone, forever gone.' James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund puts his finger on something important, and I want to push it a little further. Maggie doesn't simply want to be appreciated — she wants to be a teacher. And in the real social world of the 1830s, that was one of the very few roles in which a woman's intelligence might be at least nominally sanctioned. The tragedy is that even that cramped outlet isn't available to her at home — Tom rejects the gypsy scheme with contempt, and Eliot records his objections with devastating precision. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I think we begin with character — specifically with the question of what kind of man George Eliot has made Mr Tulliver. He is, the narrator tells us, a strictly honest man, and proud of being honest. And yet he holds a philosophy of law that is — to put it charitably — entirely unprincipled. Eliot states it with magnificent economy — listen to this formulation. "Law was a sort of cock-fight, in which it was the business of injured honesty to get a game bird with the best pluck and the strongest spurs." There is something almost Johnsonian in the compression of that sentence. The whole tragedy of Mr Tulliver's situation is contained in it: a man of genuine integrity who cannot see that his means contradict his ends. That is not a satire of law so much as a portrait of a particular kind of moral blindness — wilful, touching, and ultimately ruinous.

Oliver is right about the technique, but I want to stay with the substance of that catalogue for a moment. James makes an important point, and I don't wish to minimise the structural pressures on Tulliver. But Eliot is equally concerned with the domestic vectors of this disaster — and Mrs Tulliver's role is rendered with extraordinary subtlety. The narrator offers what appears to be a gentle defence of Mrs Tulliver's influence, and then immediately complicates it. "It might even be comparable to that proverbial feather which has the credit or discredit of breaking the camel's back." But notice the equivocation that follows — the blame, the narrator says, ought rather to lie with the previous weight of feathers. Eliot is refusing to assign simple causation to a woman who is herself a victim of the marriage she inhabits. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Which brings us to the education question — because Tulliver's ruin is not solely a legal disaster. It is also the consequence of a system in which anxious fathers of modest means were systematically fleeced by the educational establishment. Eliot's narrator is genuinely angry here — and the anger is directed at a very specific institutional absurdity. "There was but one way of doing this; any of those low callings in which men are obliged to do good work at a low price were forbidden to clergymen; was it their fault if their only resource was to turn out very poor work at a high price?" The mock-exculpatory question — was it their fault? — is devastating in its irony. Tulliver is not simply an imprudent man — he is a man without access to reliable institutions, making decisions in conditions of near-total information poverty. Eliot is asking us to hold both judgements simultaneously — the personal failure and the structural betrayal.
