Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Dickens does in that opening scene is something I find almost audacious in its compression. We don't need to be told what Gaffer Hexam does for a living, or what kind of man he is. Dickens gives us one gesture, and it tells us everything. Gaffer pulls himself back into the boat, washes his arms over the side — and then washes what he's holding too. "He chinked it once, and he blew upon it once, and he spat upon it once,—'for luck,' he hoarsely said—before he put it in his pocket." That triple rhythm — chinked, blew, spat — is pure incantation. It's the ritual of a man who has made a religion out of dead men's money, and Dickens captures it in a single sentence.

What gets me — and I've read that passage aloud more than once — is the word 'hoarsely.' He doesn't just say 'for luck.' He says it hoarsely. James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's worth being precise about what Gaffer is actually doing, because Victorian readers would have understood it immediately and we tend to soften it. He is a mudlark — or more precisely, a body-finder — someone who searched the Thames for the drowned and was legally entitled to whatever was found on the corpse. This was an actual, recognized occupation in nineteenth-century London. The Thames was, among other things, an economy of the dead. So that coin Gaffer washes isn't metaphorical corruption — it is quite literally money taken from a dead man's pocket. And the spitting for luck is borrowed superstition — the river folk had rituals around the dead that stretched back centuries.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What's remarkable is that the novel never lets Gaffer's profession remain simply gothic — it keeps interrogating what money means and where moral worth actually lives. Later, when we meet the Boffins — the Golden Dustman and his wife, people everyone dismisses as ignorant and unpolished — Dickens does something quietly extraordinary. He gives us this almost polemical narrator's aside about the nature of goodness itself. "For, Evil often stops short at itself and dies with the doer of it; but Good, never." That is the novel's moral counter-argument to everything Gaffer represents. Evil is finite, self-consuming; good propagates beyond itself. Structurally, Dickens plants that idea early and spends the entire novel testing it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Gaffer's death is one of the novel's great dark ironies, and Dickens times it with almost brutal precision. The man who made his living pulling the dead from the Thames ends up being pulled from it himself. And the discovery is made by Riderhood — his own former partner, who had been hunting him. Riderhood's reaction is just three words: 'Gaffer's done me. It's Gaffer!' Not grief. Not even shock. Just the complaint of a man who has been cheated out of his revenge. In three words Dickens tells you everything you need to know about Riderhood's moral universe.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about these charity-letter passages is the structural bravado of the thing. Dickens doesn't give us one comic appeal — he gives us a taxonomy. Fifty-seven churches, forty-two parsonage houses, seven-and-twenty organs — the accumulation is the joke, but it's also the argument. He is showing us that notoriety functions like a magnet for every form of organized extraction. And then, in the middle of all that comic cataloguing, he drops in the Duke of Linseed's letter — verbatim, mock-solemn, perfectly devastating. The postscript is the masterpiece: 'The Steward's fee is limited to three Guineas.' Limited. As if this is the duke's concession to Boffin's dignity.

And the target is entirely real and entirely recognizable to Dickens's readers. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Which connects directly to the next passage — the individual beggars — where the satire gets darker and more uncomfortable. Dickens gives the narrator this extraordinary simile for their letters, their so-called documents. 'They call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf.' That is a grotesquely specific image — and it's meant to sting, but it also captures something true about the gap between claim and reality. The form of the document confers dignity; the content dissolves it.

These are the corporate beggars. But there are, besides, the individual beggars; and how does the heart of the Secretary fail him when he has to cope with them! And they must be coped with to some extent, because they all enclose documents (they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf), the non-return of which would be their ruin. Among these correspondents are several daughters of general officers, long accustomed to every luxury of life (except spelling), who little thought, when their gallant fathers waged war in the Peninsula, that they would ever have to appeal to those whom Providence, in its inscrutable wisdom, has blessed with untold gold, and from among whom they select the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, for a maiden effort in this wise, understanding that he has such a heart as never was. The Secretary learns, too, that confidence between man and wife would seem to obtain but rarely when virtue is in distress, so numerous are the wives who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted husbands, who would never permit it; while, on the other hand, so numerous are the husbands who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted wives, who would instantly go out of their senses if they had the least suspicion of the circumstance. There are the inspired beggars, too. The poor child clings to her for awhile, as she clings to him, and then, when the old man calls, he says “I must go! God bless you!” and for a moment rests his heart against her bosom, and looks up at both of us, as if it was in pain—in agony. Such a look! I went aboard with him (I gave him first what little treat I thought he’d like), and I left him when he had fallen asleep in his berth, and I came back to Mrs Boffin. But tell her what I would of how I had left him, it all went for nothing, for, according to her thoughts, he never changed that look that he had looked up at us two. But it did one piece of good. Mrs Boffin and me had no child of our own, and had sometimes wished that how we had one. But not now. “We might both of us die,” says Mrs Boffin, “and other eyes might see that lonely look in our child.” So of a night, when it was very cold, or when the wind roared, or the rain dripped heavy, she would wake sobbing, and call out in a fluster, “Don’t you see the poor child’s face? O shelter the poor child!”—till in course of years it gently wore out, as many things do.’
