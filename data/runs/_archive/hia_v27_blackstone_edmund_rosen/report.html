What strikes me every time I return to Bleak House is how precisely Dickens locates his characters within their material conditions — their houses, their rooms, the very furniture around them. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I take your point about the room, Blackstone, and it is well observed — but I'd resist making Skimpole merely an economic phenomenon. Dickens is doing something subtler there — exposing a particular moral failure, a willed irresponsibility dressed up as charm. Skimpole is not oppressed by the system — he exploits the goodwill of decent people and calls it innocence. That is a failure of individual character, and Dickens sees it with cold clarity. The shabby luxury of that room is, in the end, a portrait of a man who has refused to become fully human.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I have enormous respect for that reading — but I think it lets the system off rather lightly. Consider the Smallweeds, who represent the precise opposite of Skimpole — a family that has annihilated every human quality in the pursuit of capital accumulation. Dickens describes how the Smallweed family tree, going out early and marrying late across generations, produced something remarkable. "Hence the gratifying fact that it has had no child born to it and that the complete little men and women whom it has produced have been observed to bear a likeness to old monkeys with something depressing on their minds." No child born to it — because childhood itself, with all its levity and imagination, has been systematically expelled. Dickens is not describing a moral failure here — he is describing what a particular set of economic imperatives does to human beings across generations. The Smallweeds are monstrous not because they chose badly, but because the system has made their monstrousness rational.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel is right that the Smallweed portrait goes beyond individual character — and the family structure he describes is historically recognisable. The moneylending, the early entry into commerce, the late marriage — these were deliberate strategies of capital preservation in a world without inheritance safety nets. But I want to turn to something that strikes me as the novel's darkest atmosphere — and it comes from an earlier passage altogether. The ghost of the crippled lady at Chesney Wold — the curse she pronounces as she falls on the pavement. "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" That is not a ghost story in any cheap sense — it is a curse that functions as structural prophecy within the novel. The pride of the Dedlocks will be humbled — and Dickens ensures that the reader feels the weight of that promise from very early on.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
That passage is, I think, one of the finest examples of Dickens's Gothic imagination — and it is doing very precise narrative work. She had been, we're told, a lady of a handsome figure and a noble carriage — and she never complained. The silence she keeps with her husband — that utter refusal of communication — is as much a part of her character as the curse itself. What Dickens gives us here is pride as a moral absolute — something that can survive physical ruin and transform into spiritual power. One can disagree with her — one should, perhaps — and yet one cannot quite dismiss her.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The Gothic is never merely decorative in Dickens — and that curse confirms it. The crippled lady walked the terrace day after day, going up and down, up and down — what Dickens renders is a body being destroyed by a refusal to yield. But I want to connect this to another kind of interior life the novel gives us — one that is equally harrowing, and entirely private. Esther, describing her situation of knowing Lady Dedlock is her mother, says something that should stop every reader cold. "Knowing that my mere existence as a living creature was an unforeseen danger in her way, I could not always conquer that terror of myself which had seized me when I first knew the secret." Terror of herself — not of the secret, not of exposure, but of her own existence as a living creature. That is what the system of illegitimacy and secrecy does — it teaches a person to experience themselves as a danger, as a problem to be managed. Dickens understood that — and it is one of the things that makes this novel so devastating.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, reading these early passages, is the sheer clarity of Jarndyce's moral counsel — and how little Richard actually hears it. Dickens gives us Jarndyce as something very close to a figure of practical wisdom — not a saint, but a man who has looked at the world steadily and understood it. His advice to Richard is remarkable in its directness. "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner." There is Burke in that sentence, I think — the conviction that effort and providence are not alternatives but partners. The tragedy is not that Richard is wicked — it is that he is constitutionally incapable of the sustained, unglamorous effort that Jarndyce describes. He prefers the possibility of fortune to the discipline of earning it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd agree with that reading of Jarndyce — and I'd add that it makes him almost unique in the novel's world. Most of the adults surrounding Richard are either indifferent to his fate or actively invested in prolonging the Chancery suit. Jarndyce alone tries to give the boy a conceptual framework for living independently of it. And the way Jarndyce frames this is quite beautiful — he refuses to be condescending about the young love. "I will assume that a few years hence you will be in your hearts to one another what you are to-day." He takes them seriously as moral agents — which is both generous and, in Richard's case, ultimately a wager Jarndyce will lose. The historical point worth making is that Richard's situation was not at all unusual for a young man of the period with expectations tied up in Chancery. Such suits could run for decades — the Jarndyce case is a caricature, but only just — and young men routinely deferred adult life while waiting for a windfall that never came.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Both of those observations are right, and I want to push them a step further. What Dickens is staging in Chapter Thirteen is a scene of ideological formation — the moment a young man is inducted into the ideology of self-help and individual effort. Jarndyce's counsel is wise and well-intentioned — I don't dispute that — but it places the entire burden of success on Richard's individual character. Esther captures the tenderness of that scene beautifully. "They went on in their own wild way for a little while — I never stopped them; I enjoyed it too much myself." But that warmth is precisely the problem — it makes the systemic trap Richard is already inside invisible. No amount of constancy and fortitude was going to reform the Court of Chancery.

To understand what Dickens is doing with Jo, you need to understand what the inquest system actually was in mid-Victorian England. A coroner's inquest was convened to establish the cause of death — legally, it was the mechanism by which the state acknowledged that a death had occurred and assigned it a cause. For that mechanism to function, it required witnesses — people who could testify to what they had seen or known. That phrase — 'I hesitate. Not that he could doubt' — is the most chilling bit of lawyerly syntax in the novel. He is not hesitating out of mercy — he is hesitating because the timing of maximum damage hasn't quite arrived yet. And that, I think, is what the novel ultimately teaches us about institutions — whether legal, philanthropic, or social. They do not fail by accident. They operate exactly as designed, for exactly the people they were designed to serve.

London. Michaelmas term lately over, and the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln’s Inn Hall. Implacable November weather. As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill. Smoke lowering down from chimney-pots, making a soft black drizzle, with flakes of soot in it as big as full-grown snowflakes—gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun. Dogs, undistinguishable in mire. Horses, scarcely better; splashed to their very blinkers. Foot passengers, jostling one another’s umbrellas in a general infection of ill temper, and losing their foot-hold at street-corners, where tens of thousands of other foot passengers have been slipping and sliding since the day broke (if this day ever broke), adding new deposits to the crust upon crust of mud, sticking at those points tenaciously to the pavement, and accumulating at compound interest.
London in November: fog and mud pervade the entire city, creating a primordial, apocalyptic atmosphere where even extinct creatures seem plausible.
