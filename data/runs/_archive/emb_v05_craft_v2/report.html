Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The first word of the novel is 'Fog.' Just that — one word, a full stop, and a paragraph break. Dickens doesn't ease you in. He doesn't say 'It was a foggy afternoon in London.' He simply declares the condition of the world. And then he does something structurally extraordinary — he builds the fog outward, in waves, like a great rhetorical tide. Listen to how it moves — upstream and downstream simultaneously, then out to the marshes and the heights, then inward into the most intimate human spaces. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." 'A great and dirty city' — that parenthetical aside, that little '(and dirty),' is almost a whisper of contempt inserted into an otherwise grand clause. By the time we reach the shivering apprentice boy on deck, the fog has moved from geography into human suffering — and it's done it in a single, sustained paragraph.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me, reading that passage with a historian's eye, is how precise the geography of misery is. Greenwich pensioners, collier-brigs, the apprentice boy — these are not decorative details. These are the actual inhabitants of the Thames corridor in the 1850s. The fog Dickens describes was real — the great London particular, as it was known — coal smoke trapped by cold air, lethal to the young and old alike. But of course he sends us straight from the river into Lincoln's Inn, which is where the fog's real source lies, in his view. There's a wonderful passage — and I use 'wonderful' with some irony — where Dickens places the Lord High Chancellor at the centre of all this murk. "The raw afternoon is rawest, and the dense fog is densest, and the muddy streets are muddiest near that leaden-headed old obstruction, appropriate ornament for the threshold of a leaden-headed old corporation, Temple Bar." 'Leaden-headed old obstruction.' As a legal historian, I find that almost unbearably apt. Temple Bar was the gateway between the City of London and Westminster — it was literally a place where things got stuck. And the Lord High Chancellor sat just beyond it, at the heart of an institution designed, in theory, for equity — for fairness where common law failed. The gap between that theory and the reality Dickens describes is where the novel's anger lives.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline, that's exactly right — and it prepares us for the Chancery passage, which works by the same logic but at much greater scale. Dickens builds a satirical tableau — the wigs, the fog, the interminable brief — and then releases this extraordinary rhetorical charge at the end. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." 'Every shire... every madhouse... every churchyard.' That triple anaphora is a prosecution. Dickens isn't describing one corrupt institution — he's mapping its damage across the entire national body. And structurally, the fog from page one has been preparing us for this: obscurity is not accidental — it is the Court's primary product.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The warning at the end of that passage is, to me, one of the most devastating lines in Victorian literature — and it's historically accurate. Dickens quotes — paraphrases, really — a sentiment that legal reformers had been expressing for decades. The advice he attributes to honourable practitioners — 'Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here' — that was the lived reality. Chancery suits could run for generations. The case Dickens fictionalises as Jarndyce and Jarndyce had real prototypes — cases lasting forty, fifty years. By the time a case was resolved — if it was resolved — the estate being fought over was frequently consumed entirely by legal costs. So when Dickens says the Court 'gives to monied might the means abundantly of wearying out the right,' he is not exaggerating for effect. He is simply describing procedure.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Dickens is doing in that passage is quite precise, and it's worth being precise back at him. He isn't simply saying the lawyers are corrupt in the ordinary sense — taking bribes, fixing verdicts. He's describing something more insidious: the moral deformation that comes from living inside a system that never resolves. The Court of Chancery in the early nineteenth century was, genuinely, a byword for catastrophic delay. Cases ran for decades — occasionally for generations — while costs accumulated and the original estate was consumed by legal fees. Dickens had seen this at close quarters — he'd worked as a clerk in a Chancery solicitor's office as a young man. So when he lists the corrupted figures — the master, the copying-clerk, the solicitors' boys, the receiver — he knew exactly who those people were. The line that really stops me is this one — let me read it as he wrote it. "In trickery, evasion, procrastination, spoliation, botheration, under false pretences of all sorts, there are influences that can never come to good." That list — trickery, evasion, procrastination, spoliation — is doing something beyond enumeration. Each word is slightly worse than the last, and that final ugly compound — botheration — deliberately deflates the legal gravity with low comedy. He's saying: the law produces not dramatic villainy but petty, creeping moral shabbiness.

That point about deflation is exactly right, and I want to pick up the craft thread there. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and what's remarkable about that passage is the way the Gothic mode does actual historical work. Lincoln's Inn was the geographical heart of Chancery practice — the physical buildings, the staircase offices, the conveyancers' chambers. Dickens knew that world intimately, and he renders it here as something between a prison and a haunted house. The key phrase for me is almost buried in the opening sentence. "The perplexed and troublous valley of the shadow of the law, where suitors generally find but little day." He's rewriting the Twenty-Third Psalm — the valley of the shadow of death becomes the valley of the shadow of the law. That substitution is not accidental: the law has become a religion, and a deadly one. And then those lamps — clogged lamps like the eyes of Equity — bleared Argus, with a pocket for every eye. Equity was the formal jurisdiction of Chancery — the court that was supposed to deliver fairness beyond the letter of the law. Dickens gives it many-eyed, blind, pocket-stuffing monster form.

The historical record bears Miss Flite out. There are documented cases from the real Chancery — families destroyed over two or three generations by a single piece of disputed property. The costs were not incidental — they were structural. Every procedural step generated a fee; every delay generated another step; the machine fed on itself. What Dickens understood — and this is his particular genius — is that the violence of the system was invisible precisely because it was legal. Nobody went to prison for what happened to Miss Flite's family.

The legal dimension of that passage is worth pressing on. Jo was barred from giving evidence as a witness at the inquest — not because of his character, but because he could not demonstrate knowledge of an oath. Illiteracy, in Victorian legal practice, was treated as a species of moral deficiency. The system that had failed to educate Jo then used his lack of education to exclude him from its protection. And Dickens sees that circular logic with absolute clarity. When Jo moves on through the novel — always moved on, never allowed to stop — the phrase acquires its full legal meaning. In Chapter Thirty-One, he describes it himself, and it's impossible to read without anger. "I have been moved on, and moved on, more nor ever I was afore, since the t'other one give me the sov'ring. Mrs. Snagsby, she's always a-watching, and a-driving of me — what have I done to her?" What have I done to her. The question that can't be answered — because it isn't about anything Jo has done. It's about what he is.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And then Dickens takes that question — what has Jo done — and extends it into a passage of sustained, terrible irony in Chapter Forty-Seven. The passage about Mrs. Pardiggle's Indians and Mrs. Jellyby's Africans is one of the sharpest satirical passages Dickens ever wrote. He's attacking the culture of philanthropic exoticism — charity that travels ten thousand miles rather than cross the street. But what makes the passage brilliant is the formal conceit. "He is not a genuine foreign-grown savage; he is the ordinary home-made article." Home-made article — the language of manufacturing, of commodity, applied to a human child. It's the language of trade used against a system that trades on distant suffering while ignoring the suffering at home. And then the final line of that passage — "from the sole of thy foot to the crown of thy head, there is nothing interesting about thee" — is written in a register that is simultaneously contemptuous and heartbroken. Dickens is performing the dismissal to condemn it.

The image of the cart draws on something very specific in the Victorian social imagination. Jo is a crossing-sweeper — one of the invisible poor, the children who kept London's streets marginally passable for their betters. The Poor Law of 1834 had essentially institutionalised neglect — if you were not in a workhouse, you were not the state's problem. Jo has no workhouse. He has the street. He moves on when told to move on — "move along, Jo" — a refrain Dickens repeats with deliberate cruelty. And then Dickens turns that constant dispersal — that lifelong being-moved-on — into the metaphor of a cart dragging itself to its end. The genius of the image is that it refuses pity on sentimental terms. It demands something harder: accountability.

This is where Dickens' satire cuts most finely, I think — and most accurately. "The story goes that Sir Leicester paid some who could have spoken out to hold their peace." In Victorian England — and, let us be honest, in many subsequent eras — this is simply how scandal died among the gentry. Money did not buy innocence — it bought silence. And silence, given long enough, became the accepted version of events.
