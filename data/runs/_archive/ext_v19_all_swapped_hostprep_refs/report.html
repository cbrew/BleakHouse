Edmund Leigh
Dickens does not believe in his own remedies. The three passages, read as a moral sequence, reveal that the people are not the innocent victims of corrupt institutions but the co-producers of the conditions in which injustice thrives — and Dickens has embedded that uncomfortable claim in the syntax of a dying man's last sentence and in the cheerful entertainments of a London court, perhaps against his own pamphleteer's intentions.
Key points:
  • The three passages form a single moral argument in three movements tracing the degeneration from bystander to accomplice to mourner, structured on Aristotelian lines about habituated vice.
  • Dickens's central concern is not institutional critique but the corruption of spectatorship: ordinary people repeatedly choosing entertainment over conscience, cultivating pleasurable disengagement as a social art.
  • The Gridley passage enacts its ethical argument through grammar itself — Chancery is the grammatical subject that 'breaks,' reducing Gridley from agent to patient, from poiein to paschein.
  • The justice-to-entertainment ratio across the two crowd passages (1:5 in c11:p60 to 0:6 in c39:p58) represents a measurable moral deterioration of the community, the precise downward habituation Aristotle describes when hedone governs action.
Passages they want to quote:
  • "Of all my old associations, of all my old pursuits and hopes, of all the living and the dead world, this one poor soul alone comes natural to me, and I am fit for. There is a tie of many suffering years between us two, and it is the only tie I ever had on earth that Chancery has not broken!"
  • "the public immediately refers to innumerable people who can tell nothing whatever"
Disagreement angles:
  • Against a Foucauldian or new-historicist reading of the crowd as disciplined subjects of institutional power: the crowd in c11:p60 is not oppressed into ignorance but has cultivated irrelevance as a social art, chosen freely because not-knowing is delightfully communal — this is a moral diagnosis, not a structural one, and institutional reform cannot cure a cultivated vice.
  • Against a reformist or Whig-historical reading that judges the novel by its political consequences (the Judicature Acts of 1873-75): the moral structure of the novel quietly contradicts its surface politics, since the people who would carry out reform are themselves complicit in the conditions being reformed — closer to Burke's view in the Reflections than to Victorian liberal optimism.
Oliver Trevelyan
Dickens understood that satire is not the genre of critique — comedy is. Satire lets you feel superior; comedy makes you the joke. The passages that seem lightest, c11 and c39, are the ones where the reader is most thoroughly indicted, while the tragedy of c24 is the easiest to bear precisely because bearing it is all it asks. This only becomes visible when you perform the passages aloud and feel your own face doing what Dickens has set it up to do.
Key points:
  • The three passages occupy completely different acoustic worlds — c11 is farce with music-hall deflation, c24 is a formal elegy with long suspended phrases, and c39 is a comedy of obsession — and Dickens is scoring three different registers of emotional response, not making three versions of the same argument about injustice.
  • The relationship between the passages is symphonic, not argumentative: Dickens modulates affect across them, moving the reader from laughing at others (c11), to grieving with Gridley (c24), to enjoying gossip and thereby becoming complicit (c39).
  • The comedy passages (c11 and c39) are structurally more damning than the tragedy of c24, because satire allows the reader to feel superior while comedy makes the reader the joke — in c39, the reader draws pleasure from the Fortunatus' purse of gossip just as the court does.
  • Dickens encodes theatrical performance directions — gear-changes, breath points, auditory layering — directly into the syntax of the prose, visible only when the passages are read aloud; the public readings technique is already present in the novel eight years before those readings began.

Daniel Rosen
Dickens's novel is not, at its deepest level, a critique of Chancery. It is a critique of inheritance. Chancery exists to manage property transfers between generations, and what Dickens shows is that the machinery of inheritance does not merely fail to transfer property fairly — it actively consumes the lives of those who seek their share. The most radical thing you can say about Bleak House is that Dickens is not asking for a better Court of Chancery; he is showing what any institution built to adjudicate the passage of property from the dead to the living will inevitably do to those who enter it without power.
Key points:
  • The three passages share a single economic structure: the conversion of human life and human death into objects that circulate inside institutions serving no one who enters them. Each passage shows a different register of that conversion — Nemo's death carrying no economic weight, Gridley's life expropriated into legal fees, the court community generating a fantasy economy of gossip from exclusion.
  • The grammar and passive voice of c11:p60 are not stylistic choices but grammatical enactments of what Chancery does to agency: no one acts, everyone is acted upon. The beadle's failure to find witnesses is the rational response of a community where a pauper's death carries no economic weight, not civic apathy.
  • Gridley's speech in c24:p154 is not merely pathos but a property ledger of destroyed assets — 'old associations, old pursuits and hopes' mapping directly onto Victorian political economy's three sources of individual economic capacity: social capital, productive capacity, and future earnings. Chancery has liquidated all three.
  • The 'Fortunatus' purse of gossip' in c39:p58 describes a substitution economy: a community excluded from real property relations generates an imaginative economy of pure speculation, which is also exactly what Chancery itself runs on. The actual contested value in Jarndyce is over a thousand times the annual income of anyone in the scene.

Disagreement angles:
  • Against Trevelyan's likely literary-historical reading: the beadle is not primarily a comic type in a satirical tradition going back to Fielding — he is a labour position, a minor state functionary whose ineffectuality is the structural condition of his role, not a personality defect or aesthetic device.
  • Against Leigh's likely psychological reading of Gridley: the devastation is real but downstream of an economic fact. Gridley went to Chancery because he had no money, no social capital, no lawyer friend — people with connections resolve inheritance disputes without going to court. The psychology is produced by the structure, not the reverse.
  • Against both panelists' likely shared assumption: the law in Bleak House is not the primary villain. The law is the mechanism. The primary structure is property — the system by which some people's claims on assets are enforceable and others' are not. Chancery is the machine, but property is the fuel.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_cultural_historian)
The Jarndyce case has been running for generations because the contested property cannot be distributed until the court decides who rightfully owns it. But here is the thing — the longer it runs, the more the property is consumed by legal costs. Chancery is not a broken mechanism for distributing inheritance — it is the mechanism by which inheritance destroys itself, and everyone connected to it. And what I find so devastating in the passages we've been given is that Dickens shows us three different registers of that destruction — the comic, the bureaucratic, the tragic — and each one reveals the same structural truth. Look at the beadle in Chapter Eleven. He is, in Dickens's mordant phrasing, made 'more imbecile by being constantly informed that Mrs. Green's son was a law-writer his-self and knowed him better than anybody.' The information that might help is always located precisely where it cannot be reached — aboard a vessel bound for China, accessible only by telegraph to the Lords of the Admiralty. This is not a joke about bureaucratic incompetence — it is a joke about how property and inheritance make knowledge itself inaccessible to the people who need it most.

Edmund Leigh (moral_philosopher)
That is a very elegant structural reading, and I would not wish to quarrel with the observation itself. But I think it elides something important — the degree to which the people in these scenes are freely choosing their behaviour. The policeman in Chapter Eleven is a particularly uncomfortable figure for Dan's account. Dickens describes him as 'inflexible' — that stiff stock, that stout belt — and the description is admiring in its way. And then we are told he is there to deal with anything 'between a lost child and a murder.' That range — from the most innocent vulnerability to the most extreme violence — is the entire moral spectrum, and he is positioned to respond to all of it with identical indifference. Now, I think Dan would say the structure produces the indifference. I would say the indifference is a cultivated vice — a habit of not looking, chosen and practised until it becomes second nature. And that distinction matters enormously for how we read the novel's moral weight.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_cultural_historian)
But Edmund, where does the habit come from? The policeman is not paid to look — he is paid to maintain order, which is an entirely different thing. A Metropolitan Police constable in the 1850s earned around nineteen shillings a week — barely a subsistence wage. His entire livelihood depends on not seeing anything that his superiors have not sanctioned him to see. The psychology Edmund is describing — that cultivated indifference — is produced by an employment relationship. And Dickens knows this — which is why the smile the policeman exchanges with the potboy is so lethal. That smile is solidarity between two men at the bottom of the hierarchy — watching the official machinery make a fool of itself, but knowing better than to say so out loud.

Oliver Trevelyan (actor_and_narrator)
Right, so Fortunatus' purse — for those who need it — is a mediaeval legend about a magical bag that never runs empty. And Dickens deploys it with this wonderful, almost casual grandeur. 'Never since it has been a court has it had such a Fortunatus' purse of gossip as in the proceedings at the rag and bottle shop.' Now, when you read that aloud, there is a luxurious quality to the cadence — it practically invites you to purr. And that is the trap Dickens has set. Because what is the court doing? It is 'maddened,' it is 'in delirium,' imagining 'guineas pouring out of tea-pots, crown-pieces overflowing punch-bowls.' The community has been driven into collective delusion by the fantasy of hidden wealth — the fantasy that underpins Chancery itself. And we laughed. We laughed at the people imagining guineas in tea-pots — and in doing so, we were doing exactly what they were doing: enjoying the spectacle of other people's desperate hope.

Edmund Leigh (moral_philosopher)
That is beautifully put, and I think it connects to something I notice in all three passages — the people are always watching something they cannot influence. The court watches Smallweed rummage. The crowd watches the beadle. Esther watches Richard leave, carrying Gridley's words inside him like a wound. Edmund Leigh
Dickens has written a novel in which the man who knows himself and cannot act (Richard) is sympathetic, and the man who acts without self-knowledge (Vholes) is damnable — and the grammar proves it: Richard's speech of roughly 120 words contains zero future tense constructions and five conditional or subjunctive ones, because the future tense is the tense of resolve, and akrasia is precisely the inability to connect moral knowledge to practical intention.
Key points:
  • Richard Carstone's confession in c23:p37 is a textbook case of Aristotelian akrasia — he possesses full moral self-knowledge (demonstrated by his use of the indicative mood) yet cannot form intentions to act on it (demonstrated by the complete absence of future tense constructions in his speech), with five conditional or subjunctive constructions and zero future tense constructions.
  • The Vholes passage (c60:p94) illustrates what MacIntyre calls the characteristic moral failure of modernity: the evacuation of virtue language from substantive teleological content, leaving only the form of obligation. Vholes is not lying or hypocritical in a simple sense — he has perfectly systematized his parasitism into a language of professional duty so that vice and virtue are structurally indistinguishable to him.

The plot of Bleak House actually vindicates Tulkinghorn's function as keeper of secrets: when he is destroyed, the social fabric unravels, confirming Burke's argument about the necessity of productive opacity in civilized life.

Daniel Rosen
Tulkinghorn is not the villain of Bleak House — he is its most honest economist. Every character in the novel deals in information asymmetry and non-disclosure; Tulkinghorn alone is transparent about the structure, never invoking duty to disguise extraction. He is property relations made flesh: the answer to the question of what the Victorian legal-property system looks like when it becomes a person. And the corollary that should make any reader uncomfortable is that we, consuming Lady Dedlock's secret for entertainment, are positioned structurally exactly where Tulkinghorn is — hungry for the disclosure.
Key points:
  • These passages are not primarily about secrets but about the ownership of information as a form of capital — information behaves exactly like Victorian real property: it can be held, transferred, withheld, leveraged, and used to extract rent.
  • Tulkinghorn's annual income from the Dedlock estate is likely 25–30 times what he paid Nemo to copy the documents that generate it; Nemo is structurally invisible before he is literally dead, enacting Poovey's argument that the social body is constituted by rendering certain kinds of labour invisible.
  • Richard Carstone's 'inconstancy' is not a character flaw but a rational response to structural impossibility: a young gentleman with no capital whose only 'asset' is a Chancery suit that costs more to pursue than it can yield; Vholes is not a monster but a lower-middle-class professional doing exactly what the market requires — the horror is systemic, not personal.
  • In pre-1870 English property law Lady Dedlock owns nothing in her own right as a feme covert; the staring scene in c12:p114 is not a psychological drama but a description of actual power relations in which the family solicitor, holding deeds, wills, settlements, and secrets, has become more powerful than the family itself.

Jarndyce's advice in c13:p118 is not just correct — it is the ethical centre of the novel. But Dickens has structured the novel to make us feel that its failure is tragic rather than deserved, and in doing so he inadvertently makes the sentimental case against personal moral responsibility that his own moral architecture ought to resist. The novel knows more than its author intends to argue.
Key points:
  • Richard's ruin is a case of akrasia — weakness of will — not wickedness or ignorance; every adult in these passages performs their moral duty correctly, and the failure belongs entirely to Richard's character
  • Jarndyce's counsel in c13:p118 is grammatically and morally precise — a chiasm pairing Providence with personal effort, refusing both Calvinist fatalism and Pelagian self-sufficiency — yet it accomplishes nothing, exposing the gap between moral knowledge and moral action
  • Mrs. Badger's diagnosis in c17:p19 is the most clinically exact passage: Richard is 'languid' because he pursues external goods (the suit's resolution) rather than the internal goods of any actual practice, mapping onto MacIntyre's distinction in After Virtue
  • Dickens the moralist holds Richard responsible while Dickens the sentimentalist solicits sympathy for Richard as Chancery's victim — this unresolved tension between institutional blame and individual moral responsibility is the novel's most interesting irresolution

These are not comic relief passages — they are the main event. The critical tradition has it exactly backwards: Dickens's humor is not a pressure valve before returning to dark material. The comedy IS the dark material. The form enacts the content, and the laugh is the mechanism of ambush — how the audience is brought close enough to be hurt.
Key points:
  • These passages contain 'scored comedy' — the humor is encoded in delivery infrastructure (rhythm, timing, physical register) rather than existing on the page as described comedy. Dickens writes stage directions into his sentence structures.
  • The four passages form a taxonomy of comic survival strategies: Miss Flite survives through grandiose ritual, the charity women through collective self-righteous delusion, Vholes through total depersonalization, and Bucket through cheerful aggression disguised as sociability — and each character's technique is visible in the sentence structure itself.
  • Esther's narrative neutrality in c30:p87 is not unconscious naïveté but disciplined restraint — a form of tact raised to technique. When performed aloud, her rhythm subtly shifts around the charity women, revealing control rather than obliviousness.
  • These passages are not comic relief from the dark material — they ARE the dark material. Miss Flite's fragmented sentences are the sound of a broken and rebuilt mind; Vholes's depersonalized politeness is legal violence; Bucket's cheerful raillery is coercion. The comedy is the trauma, refracted.
