Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I should say at the outset that I have always found the opening of Bleak House to be one of the great set pieces in English prose — and I use that phrase with full deliberation. There is a tendency among certain readers — well-intentioned, naturally — to treat that famous fog as an allegory for institutional corruption, for the smothering machinery of the State. And while I do not wish to be uncharitable to that reading, I think it mistakes the nature of Dickens's genius. The fog is not a symbol deployed to carry a political argument. It is a world made vivid — made palpable — through the exercise of an extraordinary moral and literary imagination. What truly interests me in the early chapters is the way Dickens establishes the human stakes — the damage done to individual souls by a system of law that has lost all connection to justice. And here I am drawn to a passage in the later chapters — when Ada speaks to Jarndyce with a tenderness that I find, frankly, rather moving. She says to him, on his shoulder — and the detail of the shoulder matters enormously — she says: That is not a line written to illustrate a sociological thesis. That is Dickens writing about the human capacity for love — the way affection re-makes the family when the family itself has been broken. That, to my mind, is where the novel lives.
