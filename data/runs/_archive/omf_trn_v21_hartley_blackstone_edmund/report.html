Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Thank you — and yes, I think that moment is one of Dickens's most quietly devastating strokes in the entire novel. We are very early in the book, and the Boffins have just come into the Harmon fortune — the great dust-heap inheritance. And Mrs Boffin, this warm, plain, almost comically straightforward woman, proposes something that stops the reader cold. She wants to adopt an orphan boy and name him after the young heir who drowned — to use the fortune for something redemptive. The exact words are worth hearing, because Dickens loads so much into them. She says: 'Now that he is past all benefit of the money, and it's come to us, I should like to find some orphan child, and take the boy and adopt him and give him John's name, and provide for him.' That accumulation of verbs — find, take, adopt, give, provide — it reads like a liturgy of maternal care. And then she says it would 'make her easier' — as if the weight of the dead boy sits on her conscience, though she bears no guilt at all. Dickens establishes from the very start that the Harmon money cannot be held without some reckoning with the Harmon death.

That is exactly right, and I think it points to something central in Dickens's moral imagination throughout this novel. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I find that reading genuinely moving — but I want to add a layer of friction to it. The Harmon money is, at its origins, filth money — made from the collection and sale of London's waste. And it has arrived at the Boffins only because the legitimate heir — that same young John — was found drowned in the Thames. So when Mrs Boffin speaks of what would 'make her easier,' there is an unconscious acknowledgement that the money carries a kind of contamination. The gesture toward adoption is generous and genuine — but it is also a form of moral laundering. Dickens understood, at a structural level, that inherited wealth can never be wholly clean.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That word 'laundering' is interesting — because the novel gives us, in Wegg, a grotesque parody of exactly that impulse. Where Mrs Boffin wants to do something good with what the money permits, Wegg wants to seize it entirely — through blackmail, through what the novel calls moral bondage. His scheme, laid out with that magnificently pompous rhetoric Dickens gives him, is breathtaking in its brazenness. Dickens describes his plan in that rolling, parodic reported speech — all those 'That's and 'comrade, brother, and partner's. And the climax of his proposition is this: 'he should be kept in a state of abject moral bondage and slavery until the time when they should see fit to permit him to purchase his freedom at the price of half his possessions.' The language of slavery applied to a wealthy man in his own home — it's almost operatic in its villainy. And yet it's also comic, because Wegg is ridiculous — which is how Dickens defangs the truly dangerous while still letting us feel the horror.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And then, in absolute contrast to both Mrs Boffin and Wegg, there is Bradley Headstone. Here the novel moves from comedy and tenderness into something genuinely disturbing — a portrait of a mind that cannot repent and cannot stop. Dickens gives us a passage on the psychology of the unremorseful murderer that reads almost like a clinical paper — and yet it is pure art. The narrator tells us, with absolute precision: 'He had no remorse; but the evildoer who can hold that avenger at bay, cannot escape the slower torture of incessantly doing the evil deed again and doing it more efficiently.' That phrase — doing it more efficiently — is chilling because it describes the criminal's compulsion as a kind of deranged perfectionism. He cannot undo the murder, so he rehearses it endlessly, improving the technique — punishing himself with his own competence. There is no novelist writing in 1865, and possibly very few since, who understood the dark mechanics of guilt this clearly.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me most in that passage is the theological precision — even without theology. Remorse, properly understood, is not merely suffering — it is suffering that opens toward amendment and eventually toward grace. Headstone is cursed with something worse than remorse — a punishment that forecloses redemption entirely. He is not suffering toward anything — he is simply suffering, in perpetuity, in a hell of his own cognition. I think Eleanor is right to highlight the defensive note, though I'd put it slightly differently. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to hold onto that word 'inheritance' for a moment, because the legal and social stakes here are rather more precarious than the novel sometimes lets us feel. Old Harmon's will is the engine of this entire plot, and it is an engine powered by spite as much as by affection. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The Headstone passage is devastating precisely because it is so interior — Dickens grants him full consciousness of his own ruin. He realises that his violence did not destroy Eugene and Lizzie — it united them. The sentence that captures it is almost cruel in its economy: "That he had dipped his hands in blood, to mark himself a miserable fool and tool." "Fool and tool" — that rhyme is not accidental, and it is not cheap. It says: he was used by his own worst nature, and used by Providence, and in the end he was used by both simultaneously. That is the structural irony Dickens has been building toward since Gaffer chinking that coin on the opening page.

And notice the word "Providence" in that passage — Dickens is careful not to let the theodicy off the hook. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I find that honesty bracing rather than bleak — because set against Headstone we have Mrs Boffin at the fireside. The novel does not claim that goodness is rewarded in any neat providential economy — it claims only that goodness is possible. Mrs Boffin kneels at the fire and warms her open hands and rubs the child's cold cheeks — and she does this because she can. That act costs her nothing except the rest of her night's sleep for many years — and she pays it without complaint. That, for me, is what Our Mutual Friend is finally about — not dust and money and the river, but the capacity for that gesture.
