Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I think both of those readings are right, and I want to add a dimension that doesn't contradict them but makes the stakes clearer. What the children believe will always be theirs — the mill, the river, the home — is property. And the novel's entire tragic engine is the mechanism by which that property is taken from them through debt, litigation, and the callousness of kin. The pastoral beauty of this opening passage is inseparable from what it means to lose it. Tom thought, we're told, that "people were at a disadvantage who lived on any other spot of the globe." That is not merely a child's provincialism — that is a class identity embedded in land. When the mill is lost, Tom doesn't just lose a building — he loses the ground on which his entire sense of self was constructed.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And what Eliot does with the siblings' relationship across the whole arc of the novel is equally precise as craft. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That contrast between Maggie's informal, rapacious curiosity and the deadening Latin drills Tom is subjected to is one of the novel's sharpest indictments. Tom is being ground through a curriculum designed to produce a certain kind of gentleman — not to produce thought. Her education happens sideways: through questions, through books she smuggles in, through sheer hunger. The irony Eleanor identifies in that passage cuts in two directions at once — gently mocking Tom's limited horizon, while showing us that Maggie is already the more alive intelligence in the room.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The gypsy episode is where Eliot is most nakedly honest about what the social order produces in a child who can't fit it. Maggie has been told she is 'like a gypsy' and 'half wild' — that language is a punishment, a diminishment. And Maggie, with the logic of a child who has been handed no better tools, decides that if she belongs nowhere respectable, she will belong there instead. Listen to how the narrator renders her resolution. "No! she would run away and go to the gypsies, and Tom should never see her any more." That 'No!' — the exclamation, the defiance — is rendered in free indirect style, Maggie's own voice surging up through the narrator's prose. And then what breaks your heart is the image of her father — she'll send him a letter by a small gypsy, she decides, 'just to let him know that she was well and happy.' She is running away from everyone who constrains her — except the one person she loves without ambivalence.

Daniel is right that the structural facts are damning — and from a legal and historical standpoint, non-resident landlordism was one of the defining crises of early-nineteenth-century England. Rack-renting, absentee ownership, the complete absence of investment in the land or its people — these were not incidental failures but structural features of the system.
