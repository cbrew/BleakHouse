Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
She does — and it's one of the most technically daring things in the whole novel. What Gaskell gives us in this passage is a character conducting a public argument against herself, in her own head, using her own best weapons. Margaret is precise, rational, morally serious — and she turns all of that against herself with devastating efficiency. Listen to how Gaskell sets it up — the prose itself mimics the rhythm of self-prosecution. "I, who hate scenes — I, who have despised people for showing emotion — who have thought them wanting in self-control — I went down and must needs throw myself into the melée, like a romantic fool!" That triple repetition — 'I, who' — is a rhetorical structure borrowed from classical oratory, and Gaskell uses it to show us a mind that is, in this moment, its own most merciless judge. And then — the body intervenes, beautifully, catastrophically — her palms wet with scalding tears before she even understands what she feels.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to come at this from a slightly different angle — because I think the pride that Margaret is flagellating herself for is not simply a personal flaw. It is a class formation. Margaret has been raised in the southern gentry — Helstone, the vicarage, a certain way of holding oneself in the world. And Milton confronts that formation with something it was never designed to accommodate — industrial reality, physical danger, collective action. What Gaskell is charting in Margaret's self-examination is not just a love story — it's the dissolution of a class identity under pressure. And the novel is honest enough to show that process as painful and humiliating — not triumphant.

That reading deepens everything — because it makes the structural parallel with Mrs. Thornton almost unbearably poignant. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
This is one of the passages I return to again and again — because Higgins does something that Victorian fiction almost never permits a working man to do. He makes a systematic historical argument. Not an emotional plea — an argument. And he does it through scripture — which is the one intellectual weapon the ruling class cannot simply dismiss. "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and th' chidren's teeth are set on edge... It's a withstanding of injustice, past, present, or to come." He is not asking for sympathy. He is making a case — that the union is not a sin but a historical necessity, forged in response to historical crime. 'It may be like war; along wi' it come crimes; but I think it were a greater crime to let it alone.' That is a genuinely radical ethical position.

The restraint here is almost shocking — given everything that has come before. What she's doing is constructing one of the cleverest double-perspective scenes in the novel. We inhabit both minds simultaneously — Margaret's exhaustion and social obligation, Thornton's admiration and wounded pride — without either character knowing what the other is actually feeling.
