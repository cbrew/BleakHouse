Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take Oliver's point entirely — the comedy is real and it matters. But I want to observe that what Gaskell is also doing here is establishing a moral geometry between these two characters. She goes to considerable lengths to describe the physical contrast between Hale and Thornton — the soft, wavering lines of the scholar set against the firm, carved features of the manufacturer. She writes of Thornton's eyes in terms that are really rather extraordinary for a first meeting. "The straight brows fell low over the clear, deep-set, earnest eyes, which without being unpleasantly sharp, seemed intent enough to penetrate into the very heart and core of what he was looking at." This is a man of absolute seriousness of purpose — and Gaskell is asking us to measure Margaret against him, not merely to observe them. The moral test of the novel begins here, in this drawing room, before a single argument has been had.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Edmund, yes — and the argument, when it comes, is one of the great set-pieces in Victorian fiction. Margaret is defending the South — she's been stung by Thornton's apparent complacency about Northern trade — and the passion Gaskell gives her is almost shocking. The narration tells us she is roused to "fond vehemence of defence" — that phrase alone is doing so much work. Then Margaret delivers this observation — which is not a polemic, it's a portrait — and it is devastating. "I see men here going about in the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care — who are not only sufferers but haters." "Not only sufferers but haters" — that is the line of a writer who understands poverty as a moral condition, not merely an economic one. And Gaskell gives that insight to a girl of twenty, fresh from the Hampshire countryside, who has simply been paying attention.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
When I read that speech aloud — and I have, many times — there's a moment just before the end where Gaskell pulls the camera back. Margaret finishes her great argument and then — and this is the stroke of comic genius — she collapses into what Gaskell calls "a determined silence," and is "angry with herself for having said so much." She has just delivered what is effectively a speech in Parliament and her immediate response is embarrassment. Gaskell never lets her heroines be merely heroic — they're always also human, in the most specific and slightly mortifying way. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That muttered aside is, I think, one of the finest things in the novel. Because it is not ironic at his expense — or not only that. Thornton is genuinely wounded. He came to that house prepared to admire — the omniscient narrator has made Thornton's fascination perfectly clear — and he has been met with scorn. The novel is, among other things, a study in injured pride — in both of them — and it begins precisely here. Gaskell understands that the most durable human feeling is not love or hatred but wounded dignity — and she builds her entire plot on that foundation.

The cost of transformation — yes, and Gaskell is ruthless about making us feel it in the body. I would add only this: that Gaskell is equally unsparing about the cost borne by characters of the opposite kind — characters who have built themselves up by will and by iron self-discipline. And we haven't even spoken about Margaret's own transformation — her willingness, finally, to set aside the pride that she began with.
