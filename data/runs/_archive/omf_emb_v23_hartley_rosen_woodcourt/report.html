Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor is right that it's not a simple unmasking. But I want to press on what that repeated phrase actually contains. "Lost" and "wanted" — those are police words. Those are words from a missing-persons notice on a London wall. Harmon's real name is already the property of the state — defined by what the law is searching for. And why is he in this position at all? Because his father's fortune has turned him into a commodity before he could speak for himself. Dickens makes this explicit in the analysis of his first deception. He writes of "the distrust engendered by his wretched childhood and the action for evil — never yet for good within his knowledge then — of his father and his father's wealth on all within their influence." The disguise doesn't come from cunning. It comes from a psyche deformed by inherited money and a loveless childhood. That is the novel's claim: the system that produces great fortunes also produces people who cannot trust themselves to exist without a mask.

Caroline's point about the third person is structurally crucial, because Dickens is doing the same thing at the narrative level. He actually steps into the text himself to explain his design — which is almost unheard of for him at this point in his career. He writes, in the postscript that functions almost as a manifesto: "I foresaw the likelihood that a class of readers and commentators would suppose that I was at great pains to conceal exactly what I was at great pains to suggest: namely, that Mr John Harmon was not slain, and that Mr John Rokesmith was he." He's not apologising for the ambiguity. He's taking credit for it. And yet there is something I find morally uncomfortable in the scheme itself — separate from Dickens's narrative craft. Consider the plot as Harmon himself describes it in that remarkable confession. "The plot was made out of our getting common sailors' dresses, and throwing ourselves in Bella Wilfer's neighbourhood, and trying to put ourselves in her way." A man of wealth and legal claim over a woman disguises himself as a working-class sailor to surveil her without her knowledge. The novel asks us to find this romantic. But it is also a portrait of power — of someone who has the luxury of choosing his own identity while Bella has no such freedom. He tests her. She cannot test him. The asymmetry is the point — and Dickens, to his credit, does not entirely let Harmon off the hook for it.
