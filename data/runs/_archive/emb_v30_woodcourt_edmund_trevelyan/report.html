Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The first word is London. Just that — one word, a full stop, and then the world begins to dissolve. What Dickens does in this opening passage is so strange and so confident that you almost miss how strange it is. He gives us a sentence that shouldn't work — and it's the best sentence in the chapter. Here it is: "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A dinosaur waddling up Holborn Hill — and it's absolutely right. Because by the time you've read those three words of preamble — London, November, fog — a Megalosaurus doesn't feel out of place at all. That's the genius of it — Dickens has already made London prehistoric before he names the creature.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I read those opening pages is the rhythm — or rather the deliberate absence of it. Dickens gives us fragments — nouns without verbs, images without resolution. "Dogs, undistinguishable in mire. Horses, scarcely better." Everything is half-obscured, half-named — as if the fog has got into the grammar itself. And then there's that extraordinary phrase — the sun has died, and the soot is in mourning for it. "Gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun." That's a sentence I've stopped and re-read every single time. There's comedy in it, and there's something genuinely desolate in it, and Dickens holds both at once.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
One ought to resist the temptation to make the fog do too much work. Critics have been known to treat it as a kind of one-word manifesto — fog equals obscurantism, fog equals the law, fog equals the ruling class. It is, in the first instance, simply fog — and Dickens renders it with the precision of a man who knew London streets and London winters. But what he then does with it is move it indoors — and that is the masterstroke. He sets up this passage about the Court of Chancery — and I ask you to hear what the prose is actually doing: "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." That accumulation — every shire, every madhouse, every churchyard — is not rhetoric for its own sake. It is the novel's moral architecture announced in a single periodic sentence. Dickens is telling us what the book is about — not allegorically, but quite literally.

Edmund is right that it's architectural — but what I love is what comes just before that great denunciation. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And the fog passage — the second great paragraph — I think it's one of the most hypnotic things Dickens ever wrote. He builds it like a piece of music — the same word repeated, each time landing somewhere new. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That parenthetical — "a great (and dirty) city" — is so unexpected, so dry. It punctures any grandeur before it can form. But then the fog reaches the most defenceless people — the shivering apprentice boy on deck — and it stops being comic at all. "Fog cruelly pinching the toes and fingers of his shivering little 'prentice boy on deck." That word cruelly — placed right at the front — it's the whole novel's moral compass in one adverb.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Miss Woodcourt is quite right to stop there. That boy — unnamed, shivering, at the bottom of every hierarchy — is the first of Dickens's innocents in this novel. He anticipates Jo, the crossing-sweeper, who will bear the weight of the novel's moral argument on his thin shoulders. Dickens understood — as Burke understood — that the first duty of a society is to its most vulnerable members. And the Chancery passage says it with a ferocity that I think has never been bettered in English prose. The warning at the end of that passage — "Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here" — is the epigraph of the entire novel. Everything that follows is an elaboration of that sentence.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Yes — and what strikes me most forcibly is that Dickens makes the diagnosis before the disease has properly declared itself. Esther observes Richard in his full attractive vigour and yet she perceives, with remarkable clarity, the flaw at the root. She says, with that characteristic quiet Dickensian precision, that his education had addressed him in exactly the same manner as it had addressed hundreds of other boys. "The system which had addressed him in exactly the same manner as it had addressed hundreds of other boys, all varying in character and capacity, had enabled him to dash through his tasks." That word — dash — does considerable work. It conveys brilliance, yes — but also superficiality, a skimming of surfaces. And then comes the sentence I consider one of the finest moral formulations in the entire novel. "They were good qualities, without which no high place can be meritoriously won, but like fire and water, though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." Fire and water — excellent servants, very bad masters. That is not a metaphor about systems — it is a maxim about the moral life, of precisely the kind Burke or Johnson would have recognized and applauded.
