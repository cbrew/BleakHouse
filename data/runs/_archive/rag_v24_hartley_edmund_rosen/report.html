Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Precisely so, and I think one must resist the temptation to read that passage as straightforward mockery. Dickens is a far more complicated moralist than his admirers on the left tend to allow. He gives us Sir Leicester as a figure of genuine honour — honourable, obstinate, truthful — the list is quite serious before it turns. "He is an honourable, obstinate, truthful, high-spirited, intensely prejudiced, perfectly unreasonable man." Six adjectives, and the last two detonate the first four — it is a sentence of extraordinary controlled irony. But notice what Dickens does not do — he does not make Sir Leicester a villain. He makes him a type — and within that type, there is something Dickens regards as genuinely admirable. The man would die rather than compromise his integrity — that is not nothing, even if his notion of integrity is, shall we say, somewhat parochial.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is how Dickens manages the tone across a single paragraph — it is genuinely virtuosic. He opens in the register of a peerage entry — "Sir Leicester Dedlock is only a baronet, but there is no mightier baronet than he" — almost heraldic. And then he slides the rug out, gently, with that deadpan qualifier about nature being "a little low, perhaps, when not enclosed with a park-fence." The comedy is in the speed — Dickens never slows down to signal that he's being funny. And that is also, I think, what makes the accumulated adjectives at the end so devastating — the rhythm never breaks. Honourable, obstinate, truthful, high-spirited — you almost agree — and then: intensely prejudiced, perfectly unreasonable.

There is also the matter of Tulkinghorn — a figure I find far more troubling than Sir Leicester, who is at least transparent in his prejudices. And this is where the dual narration becomes so important — Esther's voice exists partly to offer an alternative moral atmosphere to all of this. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel, I'll grant you the word system — it's there, and Dickens means it — but I think you're in danger of letting Richard off rather lightly. The sentence Dickens writes next is the crucial one, and it is a sentence of almost classical moral precision. "They were good qualities, without which no high place can be meritoriously won, but like fire and water, though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." That construction — excellent servants, very bad masters — is not an observation about educational policy. It is a statement about the fundamental condition of moral agency — the ancient question of whether a man governs his gifts or is governed by them. Dr. Johnson would have recognised it instantly. Aristotle would have recognised it. The system may have failed Richard — but in the end, Richard fails himself.

It is one of Jarndyce's finest moments — and Jarndyce is, for me, the moral centre of the entire novel.
