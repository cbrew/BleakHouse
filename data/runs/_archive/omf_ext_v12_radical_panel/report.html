Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me immediately about Our Mutual Friend is that Dickens understands money not as a backdrop but as the animating substance of every relationship in the book. The Wilfer family scenes are where that understanding becomes most acute — and most moving — because here we watch characters actively choosing poverty over corruption. R. Wilfer — the cherub, as Dickens calls him — delivers one of the most remarkable speeches in the novel when Bella comes back to him. He has earned the right to say what he says, because he has been ground down by genteel poverty his entire life, and he knows what it costs. Listen to what he tells Rokesmith about his daughter's choice. 'The mercenary young person distantly related to myself, did well! The mercenary young person distantly related to myself, did not trust to me in vain!' 'I admire this mercenary young person distantly related to myself, more in this dress than if she had come to me in China silks, Cashmere shawls, and Golconda diamonds.' That triple repetition of 'mercenary young person distantly related to myself' — it's incantatory. Wilfer is satirising the very language the novel's society uses to condemn Bella — and reclaiming it as a badge of honour.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel is right that the speech is incantatory, though I would resist the word satirising. What Wilfer is doing is something older and more fundamental than satire — he is pronouncing a blessing. The ritual repetition is not ironic — it is liturgical. And indeed the passage ends with the explicit invocation: 'My blessing on this engagement betwixt you.' This is a father performing the one act of paternal authority that poverty has not stripped from him. The scene that follows only deepens this. The three of them sit down together to this simple supper, with the iron safe of Chicksey, Veneering, and Stobbles staring from the corner. Dickens compares it — quite beautifully — to a fairy tale. 'It was, as Bella gaily said, like the supper provided for the three nursery hobgoblins at their house in the forest, without their thunderous low growlings of the alarming discovery, Somebody's been drinking my milk!' 'It was a delicious repast; by far the most delicious that Bella, or John Rokesmith, or even R. Wilfer had ever made.' The fairy tale without the threat — without the territorial snarl — is happiness itself, for Dickens.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Caroline's right that the passage is doing more than illustrating misery. Notice that the child's father is described as 'a weak wretched trembling creature, falling to pieces, never sober' — and then immediately: 'But a good workman too, at the work he does.' That qualification is devastating in its precision. The system extracts his labour even as it destroys his person. He is useful precisely to the degree that he is ruined. Now set that beside John Harmon's predicament: a man whose father's entire fortune was a mechanism for evil, never for good. Harmon himself puts it plainly — in his long interior reckoning he describes the trouble as stemming from 'the distrust engendered by his wretched childhood and the action for evil—never yet for good within his knowledge then—of his father and his father's wealth on all within their influence.' Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to resist the impulse to reduce all of this to economics, however. What interests me in that interior monologue of Harmon's is the language of moral choice under impossible conditions. I think Daniel is identifying something real — but I'd push back on the framing. Harmon is not a predator. He is a man trying to escape the predatory logic of the will that haunts him. He observes Bella precisely because he refuses to marry her as a transaction — as the will demands. The disguise is, in that sense, an act of respect. He even checks his own account of the facts at the end of the passage — "I have all these facts right? Yes. They are all accurately right." The self-interrogation of a scrupulous conscience. Contrast that with Riderhood — who also claims accuracy, also protests his fidelity to fact. The difference is everything.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That connection Edmund has just made — between Riderhood's "those were the words as ever he used" and Harmon's "they are all accurately right" — that is the kind of thing that makes you love this novel. Two men claiming the accuracy of their testimony. One of them lying to destroy a man. One of them telling the truth to free himself. Dickens is asking us to read the difference — to develop that moral ear. That is what the whole novel trains us to do.
