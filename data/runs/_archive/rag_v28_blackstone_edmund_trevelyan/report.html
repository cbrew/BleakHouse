Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What Dickens is doing, I think, is something rather more precise than is generally acknowledged. The fog is not, as some eager critics would have it, a metaphor for capitalism, or for the obfuscations of power. It is, first and foremost, a literary device — and a supremely confident one. Dickens is establishing a moral atmosphere — one in which clarity itself is the rarest and most precious quality. And into that atmosphere he will place individuals — Esther, Jarndyce, Richard — whose moral visibility, or invisibility, is the real subject of the novel. Dr. Johnson observed that the greatness of a work lies not in its subject but in what the author makes of it. Dickens makes of his fog a world — and that is the measure of his genius.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right that it is a literary device — I wouldn't dispute that for a moment. But the fog is also — and I think Dickens knew this perfectly well — a documentary fact. London in the eighteen-fifties produced a particular kind of coal-smoke fog — a 'pea-souper,' as they were called — that genuinely blinded the city for days at a time. And Chancery — the real Court of Chancery — was itself a kind of institutional fog. Cases ran for decades — sometimes longer than the lifetimes of the original litigants — consuming estates entirely in legal fees. The Jarndyce and Jarndyce case is not a satirical exaggeration — it is barely a composite. When Dickens writes that 'this scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means,' he is reporting, not inventing. 'the scarecrow of a suit... has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means.' That is not hyperbole — that is a precise description of how the system worked.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
I love that both of you are correct — and I'd add that from where I stand, as a performer, the fog is also simply magnificent to read aloud. The rhythm of that opening page — 'Fog everywhere. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver puts his finger on something I would not wish to dismiss — and I concede it freely. Dickens is not naïve about class — he never was. But I would argue that what concerns him is not class as a system — it is the moral corruption that respectability can license. Consider Tulkinghorn — the novel's great cold villain — described by the narrator with a beautiful, withering irony. 'The peerage may have warmer worshippers and faithfuller believers than Mr. Tulkinghorn, after all, if everything were known.' Oliver puts his finger on something essential, and I'd press it a little further. The nickname 'Rick' is not merely affectionate — it is diagnostic. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is quite right about the lawsuit as moral trap, and I want to supply the institutional scaffolding beneath it. Because Dickens wasn't inventing a metaphor — Jarndyce and Jarndyce is a composite portrait of real cases that ran for generations. And there's a moment very early in the novel where Dickens, through old Krook, gives us the most economical diagnosis of what Chancery actually does to human beings. Tom Jarndyce — the man who shot himself — put it this way: "It's being ground to bits in a slow mill; it's being roasted at a slow fire; it's being stung to death by single bees; it's being drowned by drops; it's going mad by grains." That accumulation — mill, fire, bees, drops, grains — is not rhetoric for its own sake. It precisely captures the phenomenology of attrition — how institutional delay kills not with a blow but with a thousand postponements. And that is the mechanism working on Richard Carstone throughout the novel.

'Going mad by grains' — I've read that passage hundreds of times and it still catches me unprepared. What Dickens understands as a prose artist is that rhythm enacts meaning — those five parallel clauses build exactly the sensation they describe. By the fifth repetition you feel the grind yourself. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The Skimpole comparison is very illuminating, and I think it reveals exactly why I resist the purely institutional reading of Richard's fall. Skimpole and Richard are both caught in the Chancery orbit — but Skimpole is not destroyed, and Richard is. The difference is not the institution — the difference is character. Richard has the capacity for genuine attachment — to Ada, to Esther, to Jarndyce — and that very capacity makes him vulnerable in a way Skimpole, the perfect egotist, simply is not. Dr. Johnson observed that the chains of habit are too light to be felt until they are too strong to be broken. Richard's habit is hope — corrupted, delusional hope — and by the time he perceives its weight, it has already done its work.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I take Edmund's point, and I don't want to flatten the question of character — Richard is not simply a victim. But I want to insist that the system is designed to consume people like Richard — and that Dickens knew it, and said so with surgical precision. There's a passage involving Mr. Vholes and Mr. Kenge that is, in my view, among the most devastatingly accurate pieces of institutional satire in the English language. Kenge is explaining why reform of Chancery is simply impossible: "Alter this law, sir, and what will be the effect of your rash proceeding on a class of practitioners very worthily represented, allow me to say to you, by the opposite attorney in the case, Mr. Vholes? Sir, that class of practitioners would be swept from the face of the earth." 'Swept from the face of the earth' — said with such magnificent moral alarm, as though the extinction of Vholes were a catastrophe to rival the Flood. And then — and this is the masterstroke — the parliamentary committee inquiry collapses entirely when one witness confirms that Vholes is 'a most respectable man.' Respectability is the system's immune response — it rejects every reform before it can take hold. And Richard, by engaging Vholes, does not just employ a solicitor — he plugs himself into that machine.

Gridley is not a fictional exaggeration. That is the thing people always need to understand first. Dickens based him on cases he had personally observed — men whose entire adult lives were consumed by Chancery suits they had inherited and could not escape. The Court of Chancery, in the early Victorian period, was catastrophically backed up — suits ran for decades, costs swallowed estates entire. And Gridley articulates something that contemporary reformers were also saying — that the system drove reasonable men to apparent unreason. And the detail of the tradespeople is historically exact: Blaze and Sparkle, Sheen and Gloss — Dickens invented the names, but not the practice. The luxury trades of the 1840s and 1850s operated precisely on this system — of cultivated dependence, of manufactured prestige. What Dickens sees — and this is where his genius lies — is that the fashion system and the legal system are structurally identical. Both exist ostensibly to serve their clients. Both exist actually to perpetuate themselves.
