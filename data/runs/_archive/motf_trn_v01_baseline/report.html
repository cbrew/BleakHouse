James Blackstone (social_historian)
Tom's dismissal is funny, but it does reflect a real social geography. In mid-nineteenth-century England, Romani communities occupied a genuinely ambiguous position — visible, marginalised, and the object of a great deal of middle-class projection. What strikes me first is how early Eliot plants the seed of Maggie's tragedy — and how she does it through something that looks, on the surface, like a child's petty jealousy. There's this extraordinary passage in chapter ten — Maggie watching Tom make much of Lucy Deane. "Maggie would have thought a little while ago that she could never be cross with pretty little Lucy, any more than she could be cruel to a little white mouse; but then, Tom had always been quite indifferent to Lucy before, and it had been left to Maggie to pet and make much of her." That conditional — 'a little while ago' — is doing enormous structural work. It tells us that Maggie's emotional world is not fixed — it bends entirely under the pressure of Tom's attention. She doesn't hate Lucy; she hates the situation that has made Lucy Tom's favourite. That's not just psychologically real — it's the blueprint for everything that follows.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's worth being precise about what that starvation actually means in historical terms. Maggie's need for Tom's approval isn't merely a personality quirk — it reflects a structure in which a girl's social and emotional world is entirely mediated through male relatives. She has no independent standing, no separate sphere of achievement that is recognised. Tom gets to go to school; Tom gets to come home and be feted; Tom's preferences reorganise the household. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
James, that connects directly to what happens much later — and I think Eliot has been building to it all along. When Maggie finally confronts Tom — directly, fully, in chapter thirty-seven — she names the structure that has constrained her entire life. The speech she delivers is — I don't think I'm overstating this — one of the great moral indictments in Victorian fiction. "You have always enjoyed punishing me; you have always been hard and cruel to me; even when I was a little girl, and always loved you better than any one else in the world, you would let me go crying to bed without forgiving me." That 'always' — repeated twice — is the sound of a life's accumulation of evidence. And then she goes further — into theology, almost. She's not just attacking Tom — she's articulating a whole counter-ethics of feeling, against the ethics of rule-following he embodies.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Pharisee accusation lands differently if you hold the historical context alongside it. Tom is not simply a hard-hearted brother — he is the embodiment of Dodsonian respectability, of a provincial commercial morality that prizes surface conduct above inner life. In that world, virtue really is measured by result, by reputation, by whether you have paid your debts and kept your name clean. Eliot is mounting a serious philosophical challenge to that entire system — and she's putting it in the mouth of a young woman who, by that system's criteria, has just disgraced herself. That is a deliberately provocative structural choice.

And then — immediately after all of this private anguish — Eliot turns the lens outward, to the town.
