Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's the tragedy that Dickens understands most deeply, I think — the tragedy of the wasted self. Richard is not a bad man — that's the crucial thing Dickens insists on from the very beginning. He's introduced to us as genuinely likeable — open, warm, full of talent that spills in every direction at once. And Dickens makes us love him before he begins to destroy him. That sequencing is the structural trap — you're already invested by the time the rot sets in. What Dickens is doing architecturally is using Richard's trajectory as a slow negative of Esther's. Both are wards, both enter Bleak House with uncertain futures. Esther constructs an interior life impervious to the suit; Richard surrenders his to it. And I think Dickens is asking: what is a self without anchorage?
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is absolutely right to centre this on character rather than circumstance. This is, at its heart, a study in the failure of will — and Dickens is quite merciless about it. Jarndyce sees the danger immediately and says so with the plainness of a man who loves Richard precisely because he sees through him. The suit is not Richard's enemy — his own irresolution is. Chancery is simply the form his weakness takes — had there been no suit, some other fixation would have served. Dr Johnson wrote that the chains of habit are too weak to be felt until they are too strong to be broken. Richard is the fictional embodiment of that observation. And what makes Dickens great here is that he gives Richard every opportunity for self-knowledge — and shows us, with terrible precision, how each opportunity is declined.
