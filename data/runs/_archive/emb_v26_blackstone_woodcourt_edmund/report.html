Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Every time, without fail. What strikes me afresh is how Dickens refuses to ease us in. There is no 'it was a dark and stormy night' — no narrator settling into his chair. The prose itself enacts the condition it describes. Consider how he introduces the Megalosaurus — that extraordinary sentence. "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A creature extinct for sixty million years, strolling through Victorian London — and Dickens makes it feel entirely plausible. That is the measure of what fog and mud have done to civilisation in this opening page. The modern world has dissolved back into the primeval.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
With respect to Edmund, I think the child in the cold and the symbol are not mutually exclusive. Dickens knew exactly what he was doing when he placed that boy next to the 'wrathful skipper' in his warm, close cabin. But let me come to what I think is the genuinely electrifying passage — where Dickens turns from the atmosphere and walks us into the Court of Chancery itself. Because here, the fog is no longer weather. Dickens builds to a passage that I think is one of the great denunciatory paragraphs in the English language. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." Every shire. Every madhouse. Every churchyard. Dickens is insisting on the national scale of this — that Chancery's damage is not confined to London's legal district. And this is not exaggeration for rhetorical effect. The records bear it out — cases in Chancery ran for generations, consuming estates that had once been substantial. The model for Jarndyce and Jarndyce, the case of Jennens v Jennens, had already lasted over a century when Dickens was writing. The novel did not invent this horror.

What strikes me about that passage — and it's something that gets lost if you just extract the great sentence — is the rhetoric Dickens uses to get there. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Esther is, throughout this novel, the most reliable of moral witnesses — not because she lacks feeling, but because her feeling is so precisely calibrated. And what she sees in Richard, quite early, is the precise nature of his danger. She admires him — genuinely, warmly — and then she performs a kind of diagnostic on his character that is almost clinical in its accuracy. The passage I find most remarkable in Chapter Seventeen is Dickens' account of what education had done to Richard — or rather, what it had failed to do. "They were good qualities, without which no high place can be meritoriously won, but like fire and water, though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." That is a sentence of Johnsonian balance — gift and ruin folded into the same breath. Dickens is not condemning Richard's gifts — his quickness, his gaiety, his charm. He is condemning the failure of formation — the educational system that never taught Richard to be master of himself. What follows, I think, is the tragedy of a man whose virtues were never disciplined into strengths.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to sit with that phrase — "the system which had addressed him in exactly the same manner as it had addressed hundreds of other boys" — because it does considerable work. Edmund reads it as a failure of individual formation, and he's right, but it is also a systemic critique — of educational institutions that flatten individual capacity in the name of uniformity. Dickens understood — acutely — that institutions don't merely reflect society's failures; they produce them. And that brings me to Jarndyce's speech in Chapter Thirty-Five, which is, to my mind, the most precise account in all Victorian fiction of how a legal institution destroys a human being. "It procrastinates, disappoints, tries, tortures him; wears out his sanguine hopes and patience, thread by thread; but he still looks to it, and hankers after it, and finds his whole world treacherous and hollow." Thread by thread. That image of gradual unravelling — not a sudden blow, but an incremental erosion — is historically very precise. Chancery cases did not ruin people overnight — they ruined them across decades, through delay, through false hope, through the perpetual deferral of resolution. Jarndyce and Jarndyce was modelled on real cases — some of which outlasted three generations of the families involved.

The 'moved on' motif is not incidental — it is Dickens' direct reference to the Vagrancy Acts. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right about the moral texture, and I want to add the institutional specificity — because it matters enormously that Dickens names actual offices. The Six Clerks' Office, the receivers in Chancery, Chizzle and Mizzle — these are not confections. They are thinly satirised versions of real functionaries who were drawing real salaries from a court that was processing almost nothing. The Court of Chancery by the 1840s had a backlog measured in decades. Some suits had been running for generations. And what Dickens grasps — what reformers of his day were also arguing — is that the people employed by such a system have a professional interest in its continuation. The passage Dickens gives us is: in trickery, evasion, procrastination, spoliation — that extraordinary list — there are influences that can never come to good. "In trickery, evasion, procrastination, spoliation, botheration, under false pretences of all sorts, there are influences that can never come to good." The rhythm there is prosecutorial — a list of charges, building, with botheration as the almost comic final blow before the verdict lands. Dickens prosecuted better than most barristers who appeared before that court.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The closing portrait of Chesney Wold is a masterclass in what we might now call institutional reputation management. Sir Leicester paid people to keep silent. That is not a metaphor. That is a specific, recognisable practice with a long history in English aristocratic life. And what Dickens does with that fact is quite subtle — he doesn't confirm it, he gives us the rumour. "The story goes that Sir Leicester paid some who could have spoken out to hold their peace." "But it is a lame story, feebly whispering and creeping about, and any brighter spark of life it shows soon dies away." The story that cannot be suppressed is itself described in the language of suppression — feebly whispering, creeping, dying. Truth, in Dickens' world, is not heroic. It is battered and tired and losing. That is not comfortable reading. But it is honest writing.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The portrait of Chesney Wold at the close is, I think, one of Dickens' most formally achieved passages. The gothic atmosphere — the owl in the park, the dark arching trees, the mausoleum — is not decorative. It is the objective correlative of a class that has chosen reputation over truth, and is now living inside the tomb it chose. What strikes me about the detail of Lady Dedlock's old friends — the peachy-cheeked charmers with the skeleton throats — is the savagery of the comedy. They wonder, apparently, whether the Dedlock ancestors in the mausoleum resent her company. And Dickens' answer — "the dead-and-gone Dedlocks take it very calmly" — is so dry it almost conceals the devastation it contains. The living are nastier than the dead. That is not a comfortable thing to say about a society.

What moves me most about the ending is the hush itself — the word Dickens chooses for Chesney Wold. "There is a hush upon Chesney Wold in these altered days." Not silence — hush.
