Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And what's so thrilling about that Megalosaurus image is that it's not decoration — it's doing structural work. Dickens is reaching back to before human civilization — the mud as primordial, pre-legal, pre-institutional chaos. And then the fog arrives, and it does something the mud cannot. The fog moves. It has agency. It creeps, it rolls, it droops, it pinches. Oliver, you were talking about hearing it — well, the fog passage is the closest Dickens ever came to writing prose as pure incantation. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That parenthetical — "and dirty" — is such a Dickensian sting, almost muttered under his breath, as if the grandeur momentarily embarrassed him. But the repetition of "fog" — it accumulates the way the mud accumulates, clause by clause, compound interest, as he says himself.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me — and I say this as someone who has spent years reading actual Chancery documents — is how precisely Dickens positions the fog before he introduces the court. He is not being metaphorical for the sake of poetry. The connection is forensic. And then he makes it explicit, with a sentence that I find almost unbearably controlled in its fury: "Never can there come fog too thick, never can there come mud and mire too deep, to assort with the groping and floundering condition which this High Court of Chancery, most pestilent of hoary sinners, holds this day in the sight of heaven and earth." "Most pestilent of hoary sinners." That is not a novelist reaching for colour — that is an indictment. And Dickens earns the right to deliver it because he has spent three pages establishing the atmosphere as moral fact, not mere backdrop.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The anaphora — that repeated "which has" — is doing something very specific rhetorically. Each clause is a new count in the indictment, and the sentence cannot end until Chancery's reach has been mapped across the entire country. But what I find most brilliant is where it ends — not with a grand flourish, but with a whisper of professional conscience. "Suffer any wrong that can be done you rather than come here!" That's an honourable man inside the system, crying out — and Dickens buries him at the end of a paragraph so you almost miss him. That's the structural genius — the indictment contains its own dissenter, its own note of tragic futility.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor has put her finger on something I think is historically important, too. There really were lawyers who knew Chancery was broken and said so — privately, in letters, in memoirs. Dickens was writing in 1852, and Chancery reform had been discussed, promised, and deferred for the better part of a century. So when he says "the warning" comes from honourable men within the profession — that's not invention. That's reportage. And that makes the satire more chilling, not less. The fog is not a metaphor for ignorance — it is a metaphor for knowing, and doing nothing.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Dickens gets magnificently right is the moral contagion — the way the institution doesn't just waste time and money but actually corrupts everyone it touches. The real Court of Chancery in the 1850s was genuinely dysfunctional in ways that beggar belief. Cases had been running for decades — there are documented suits stretching forty, fifty years — consuming estates that were once considerable fortunes. But Dickens' deeper insight — and this is where he surpasses the reformers who were writing pamphlets at the time — is that the damage is not merely financial. He sees it as a machine for producing moral degradation. That passage in the first chapter is really the thesis statement of the entire novel. "In trickery, evasion, procrastination, spoliation, botheration, under false pretences of all sorts, there are influences that can never come to good." Notice he doesn't say these influences produce bad outcomes — he says they can never come to good. The corruption is absolute, irremediable, systemic. And then — this is the clincher — he extends the contamination outward even to passive observers. Even those who merely watch are, quote, "insensibly tempted into a loose way of letting bad things alone to take their own bad course." That is a precise description of institutional complicity, and it was as true of Victorian England as it is of any bureaucracy today.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage — and James has put his finger on exactly the right thing — is the grammar of it. Look at that list: trickery, evasion, procrastination, spoliation, botheration. Dickens is performing the very thing he's describing — a kind of verbal accumulation that never resolves, never settles. And then the structural trick he pulls elsewhere in the chapter — the passage about the rocking-horse. He sets up this almost nursery-rhyme rhythm and then detonates it. "Innumerable children have been born into the cause; innumerable young people have married into it; innumerable old people have died out of it." That triple repetition of 'innumerable' — it's almost liturgical. Birth, marriage, death — the entire human cycle consumed by this one abstraction. And then the rocking-horse image — the child who was promised a toy when the suit ended, and grew up and died before the suit did. That is Dickens at his absolute best — the enormous made heartbreaking through the miniature.

Eleanor's right about the liturgical quality — when you read that passage aloud, you feel the congregation forming. The story goes that Sir Leicester paid some who could have spoken out to hold their peace; but it is a lame story, feebly whispering and creeping about, and any brighter spark of life it shows soon dies away. It is known for certain that the handsome Lady Dedlock lies in the mausoleum in the park, where the trees arch darkly overhead, and the owl is heard at night making the woods ring; but whence she was brought home to be laid among the echoes of that solitary place, or how she died, is all mystery. The pipes that have no stop — that detail haunts me. A pipe with no stop plays one note forever.
