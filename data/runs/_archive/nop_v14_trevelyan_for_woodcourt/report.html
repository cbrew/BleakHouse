James Blackstone (social_historian)
Oliver has put his finger on something important, and it's worth pausing on what the fog is actually hiding — historically speaking. The Court of Chancery, by 1852 when Dickens was writing, was genuinely in crisis. There were cases that had been in progress for thirty, forty, fifty years — not as hyperbole, but as documented fact. The Jarndyce and Jarndyce suit at the centre of the novel is scarcely an exaggeration of what Dickens' contemporaries would have recognised. Dickens himself drew partly on a real Chancery case — the Jennings case — which dragged on for decades and consumed its estate entirely in costs. So when he writes — and this comes slightly later in Chapter One — "Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. This scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means." James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'll add one thing from a social rather than a formal perspective, if I may. The fog isn't metaphorical for everyone in this novel equally. For the wealthy — the Dedlocks at Chesney Wold, the lawyers in their comfortable chambers — the fog is an atmosphere, a nuisance, a symbol. For Jo the crossing-sweeper, for Nemo dying in his wretched room above Krook's shop — the fog is the actual condition of their lives. No warmth, no visibility, no prospect. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Jo is one of the great structural achievements in Dickens — and I say that knowing how large a claim that is. He first appears in the Chancery chapters as a kind of witness at the inquest into Nemo's death — this illiterate boy who barely exists in the eyes of the law. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Tom-all-Alone's — it's worth dwelling on that name for a moment, because it's a real typology of urban space, not an invention. Districts like it existed in St. Giles, in Bermondsey, in Southwark — entire rookeries of collapsing tenements where displaced workers and the destitute were crammed in conditions that guaranteed epidemic disease. James Blackstone (social_historian)
'Wrong Reverends' — it does land, doesn't it, because the Church's failure around the pauper poor was systematic and documented. The parish vestries which administered poor relief were dominated by ratepayers who had every financial incentive to deny claims and minimize expenditure. The clergy who sat on those vestries often shared those interests. Chadband in the novel is not an exaggeration — he's a compression of a type that Dickens observed constantly: the minister who mistakes oleaginous self-performance for Christian service. Chadband actually gets hold of Jo at one point and subjects him to one of those magnificent rhetorical performances — the 'Terewth' speech. And Jo's honest answer — 'I don't know nothink' — is more truthful, more morally clear, than anything Chadband produces in the entire novel. Dickens understood something that sociologists would not name for another half century: that radical ignorance in Jo is entirely the product of radical social neglect. Not character. Not fate. Policy.

Then there is my Lord Boodle, of considerable reputation with his party, who has known what office is and who tells Sir Leicester Dedlock with much gravity, after dinner, that he really does not see to what the present age is tending. A debate is not what a debate used to be; the House is not what the House used to be; even a Cabinet is not what it formerly was. He perceives with astonishment that supposing the present government to be overthrown, the limited choice of the Crown, in the formation of a new ministry, would lie between Lord Coodle and Sir Thomas Doodle—supposing it to be impossible for the Duke of Foodle to act with Goodle, which may be assumed to be the case in consequence of the breach arising out of that affair with Hoodle. Then, giving the Home Department and the leadership of the House of Commons to Joodle, the Exchequer to Koodle, the Colonies to Loodle, and the Foreign Office to Moodle, what are you to do with Noodle? You can’t offer him the Presidency of the Council; that is reserved for Poodle. You can’t put him in the Woods and Forests; that is hardly good enough for Quoodle. What follows? That the country is shipwrecked, lost, and gone to pieces (as is made manifest to the patriotism of Sir Leicester Dedlock) because you can’t provide for Noodle!
Lord Boodle explains to Sir Leicester that government is failing because there is no office suitable for Noodle after all the important positions are distributed among his equally mediocre associates.
