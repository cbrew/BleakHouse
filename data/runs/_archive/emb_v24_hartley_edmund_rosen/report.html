What Dickens does in those opening paragraphs is audacious, and I think it's worth sitting with just how strange it is. He doesn't give us a character. He doesn't give us a story. He gives us a condition. London in November. Mud. Fog. And then — this extraordinary image. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage — every time I read it — is the grammar. There are no finite verbs in those opening sentences. "London. Implacable November weather." Fragments. Noun-piles. It's as if syntax itself has broken down under the weight of the fog. The novel refuses to start properly — the machinery of narrative won't turn over — and that mimics exactly what Chancery does to the people inside it. Then there's that other glorious moment — the soot described as going into mourning for the death of the sun. "Smoke lowering down from chimney-pots, making a soft black drizzle, with flakes of soot in it as big as full-grown snowflakes — gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun." That phrase — the death of the sun — is doing something quietly apocalyptic, and Dickens slips it in with almost casual elegance.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Both of those observations are well made, and I shan't quarrel with the admiration — the writing is magnificent. But I'd enter a small caveat about the interpretive direction we're taking. Fog in literature is not new with Dickens. Johnson's London, Dante's Inferno — obscurity has always been the emblem of moral confusion, not of economic systems. What Dickens is establishing here is a moral atmosphere — a world in which it is genuinely difficult to see clearly, to act rightly, to find one's way. That challenge falls on individuals — on Esther, on Jarndyce, on Richard — not on abstract institutions. The fog is a test of character. And Dickens, I think, was primarily interested in who passes that test and who fails it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I will grant Daniel the geography. The fog does settle most heavily on the law. But I would note that Dickens' complaint against Chancery is not — despite what is sometimes claimed — a complaint against property or wealth as such. It is a complaint against a particular institution that has become self-perpetuating and divorced from its original purpose — which is justice. Burke would have recognised it immediately — the corruption of an institution that has lost its moorings in natural justice and grown monstrous on precedent alone. Dickens gives us the full horror of it in that great catalogue — the lunatics, the dead, the ruined suitors. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." The nation is riddled with Chancery's wreckage. That is a moral catastrophe — but its cure, for Dickens, is reform, not revolution.

Thank you — and yes, I hold to that view, though I want to qualify it carefully, because Dickens himself is quite careful. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel, I accept the structural point — the suit is a machine, and Richard is fed into it. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor's reading of that passage is exactly right, and I want to press on one word: "substances." The suit is impalpable — Dickens uses that word explicitly — and what it produces is a need for something concrete to blame. That is a precise description of how ideological systems work on individuals. You cannot punch the market, you cannot indict capital — so you find a face to attach it to.
