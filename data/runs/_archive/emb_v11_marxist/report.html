That image is doing precise ideological work, and I think we should name it. The Megalosaurus isn't just a joke — though it's a brilliant joke. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That's well put, and I want to add the historical weight to it, because readers sometimes take the fog as metaphor and forget it was also simply real. London in November 1852 was genuinely one of the most polluted environments any human being had ever inhabited. The Thames was an open sewer. The air was coal smoke and effluvium. So when Dickens writes this — and it's worth listening to the full reach of the fog passage — "Fog in the eyes and throats of ancient Greenwich pensioners, wheezing by the firesides of their wards; fog cruelly pinching the toes and fingers of his shivering little 'prentice boy on deck." The fog is not evenly distributed. The pensioner wheezes. The apprentice shivers on deck. The skipper at least has a pipe and a close cabin. Dickens has arranged these figures in strict social order — descending — and the fog gets crueller as you go down the ladder. That is not sentiment. That is a precise sociological observation dressed in extraordinary prose.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to just briefly register what Chancery actually was, because the modern reader loses some of the horror if they treat it purely as symbol. The Court of Chancery was the equity jurisdiction of the Lord Chancellor — in theory, a court of conscience, designed to correct the rigidities of common law. In practice, by the mid-nineteenth century, it was a machine for delay. Cases ran for decades. Costs consumed estates entirely. There was a real case — the Jennings case — that ran for eighty-two years and cost the estate the entirety of what it was contesting. Dickens knew all of this. Jarndyce and Jarndyce — which we'll come to — is barely an exaggeration.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Dickens establishes in those opening pages is nothing less than the operating logic of the whole society he's describing. The fog isn't decorative — it's structural. It's what power looks like when it doesn't want to be seen clearly: pervasive, self-renewing, and entirely indifferent to those suffocating inside it. And then Dickens gives us this extraordinary catalogue of damage. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." That anaphora — that drumbeat of 'every' — is not rhetoric for its own sake. Dickens is mapping the geographic reach of institutional harm — lunatics, corpses, beggars, one in every parish. This is a system that produces specific, countable human wreckage across the entire nation.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it's worth saying how accurate that portrait actually is — because Dickens is not exaggerating for comic effect, or not only that. The real Court of Chancery in the 1840s and early 1850s had a backlog of cases that ran to decades. Suitors genuinely did inherit litigation from dead parents and pass it on to their children. The solicitors in the passage — Chizzle, Mizzle, Drizzle — those names are absurd, of course, but the dynamic Dickens describes is precise. He writes that those practitioners have learned evasion and procrastination as a kind of moral grammar. "In trickery, evasion, procrastination, spoliation, botheration, under false pretences of all sorts, there are influences that can never come to good." 'Spoliation' and 'botheration' in the same list — there's the dry wit, the precision and the absurdity held together. But the serious point underneath it is that the court doesn't just delay justice — it actively degrades the character of everyone inside it.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I read that opening — and I've read it five times now — is the tense Dickens uses. 'Ought to be — as here he is.' 'Ought to be — as are they not?' He's doing something formally strange — describing what is happening while simultaneously suggesting it is exactly what we should expect to find. As if the corruption is so total, so inevitable, that reality and satire have merged. And then Jarndyce himself — dear, warm, battered old Jarndyce — gives us the most maddening summary of what the lawsuit actually is. "We are always appearing, and disappearing, and swearing, and interrogating, and filing, and cross-filing, and arguing, and sealing, and motioning, and referring, and reporting, and revolving about the Lord Chancellor and all his satellites, and equitably waltzing ourselves off to dusty death, about costs." James Blackstone (social_historian)
Tom-all-Alone's is not an invention, or not entirely. There were streets exactly like it in Southwark, in St. Giles, in Jacob's Island — which Dickens had already visited in Oliver Twist. The mechanism was quite specific: property fell into disrepair, landlords abandoned formal tenancy, and squatters moved in — who then sub-let to the desperate at extortionate rates. The result was overcrowding of a kind we now struggle to imagine — six, eight, ten people per room, no sanitation, no clean water. And into these conditions, cholera came with exceptional efficiency. Now, what Dickens does — and this is the passage that still makes me angry — is locate the political argument precisely inside the description of the disease vector. He writes about the poor of Tom-all-Alone's as if they were themselves the contagion — 'a crowd of foul existence that crawls in and out of gaps in walls' — and you flinch at that.
