Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The first thing to observe about this novel is how completely Dickens has organised it around a single, extraordinarily potent image. A man presumed dead, whose fortune depends on that death, who must choose whether to return to life and inherit — or remain a ghost. That is not a social thesis. That is a moral situation of the highest order — worthy of Shakespeare, I would venture. And Dickens does not squander it. The river is not merely atmospheric backdrop — it is the very element in which identity dissolves and must be recovered.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund's point about identity is well-taken, and I think the novel earns it precisely because Dickens anchors it in something historically exact. The Thames in the 1860s was a working river — it carried bodies, real ones, with terrible regularity. The river police, the body-finders, the inquest system — these were functioning institutions, and Dickens knew them. What strikes me in the John Harmon passages is how precisely the horror is located — it isn't supernatural. It is administrative. Harmon reads about his own death in a government placard posted at Whitehall. The state has certified him dead. Dickens gives us the moment of drowning consciousness first, and it is extraordinary — listen to this passage. "This is John Harmon drowning! John Harmon, struggle for your life. John Harmon, call on Heaven and save yourself!" The self addresses the self in the second person — as if identity can only be recovered by speaking one's own name back to oneself. That is not the rhetoric of melodrama. That is something much stranger and more precise.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
James has put his finger on exactly what makes that passage remarkable to read aloud — and believe me, I have read it aloud. The repetition of "John Harmon" three times — it's almost liturgical. You can't rush it. Dickens has written stage directions into the grammar itself. The exclamation mark, then the comma, then the imperative — each one a beat. And then he follows it with the murder of Radfoot — which is told with this extraordinary cold economy, almost a legal summary. Harmon says, and here I must quote it directly, that — "Radfoot had been murdered by some unknown hands for the money for which he would have murdered me, and that probably we had both been shot into the river from the same dark place into the same dark tide." "The same dark place into the same dark tide" — that repetition is Dickens doing something almost Biblical. It's the cadence of fate. Two men, one fortune, one river. The novel's entire moral universe is already present in that sentence.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver is quite right about the cadence — and I would add that this is precisely the kind of effect one cannot achieve by paraphrase. The critics who want to read Our Mutual Friend as social polemic tend to paraphrase it into something much flatter. What Dickens has written is a story about what money does to the soul — which is a different thing entirely from a story about what money does to society. John Harmon, at the bottom of the Thames, fighting to speak his own name — that is a scene about the self, not the system.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd resist that dichotomy slightly, Edmund — because the system is precisely what makes the self uncertain here. Harmon's identity has been confiscated by an inheritance law. He is dead on paper because the law and the money have conspired to make him so. And Dickens is sharp enough to see that even the novel's satirical grotesques carry the same burden — the Wegg passages make this wonderfully clear. There's a passage where the stuffed specimens in Wegg's lair seem to pass sardonic judgment on human greed. "The yard or two of smile on the part of the alligator might have been invested with the meaning, 'All about this was quite familiar knowledge down in the depths of the slime, ages ago.'" The alligator has seen it all before. Greed, envy, ingratitude — they are older than civilisation, and the slime knows it. Dickens is doing both things at once — the moral and the social — and I think that is what makes this novel inexhaustible.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What Dickens gives us in Mr Boffin's account of young John Harmon is something quite beyond sentiment. It is the novel's first insistence that there was a moral debt contracted long before any of the plot's mechanics began to turn. Boffin speaks in the vernacular, but Dickens is composing something closer to elegy. Listen to how he sets the scene: "There was his little scanty travelling clothes upon him. There was his little scanty box outside in the shivering wind, which I was going to carry for him down to the steamboat." That doubled word — scanty, scanty — does extraordinary work. It is the repetition of a man who cannot quite bring himself to move past the image. And then Mrs Boffin kneels, warms her hands, rubs the child's cheeks — and the tenderness tips over into something she cannot contain. The moral texture of everything that follows — including Bella's own test — is embedded in that single scene.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to hold that image a moment longer, because there's something Dickens is being precise about which a modern reader might miss. The old man — Harmon senior — won't allow sixpence for coach-money to get this seven-year-old to the steamboat. Sixpence. This is a man who will die one of the wealthiest in London, and he begrudges his son the price of a short cab ride. The whole novel's moral argument about what money does to its possessors — and to the children of its possessors — is contained in that single withholding. Which is exactly why John Harmon, when he has the chance to step back from his inheritance, does so.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The plot mechanics here are worth pausing over, because they are stranger and more troubling than they first appear. And the river holds all of it — the comedy and the dread, the lovers and the murderer, the drowned man who refused to stay dead. That, I think, is the structural genius of the novel.
