Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Bleak House reveals, when you read it without flinching, is a society in which every official structure — legal, philanthropic, aristocratic — exists to reproduce itself at the expense of the people it claims to serve. Chancery is the clearest example, but it's not the only one. Consider Mrs. Pardiggle — the novel's great philanthropist. She visits the brickmakers — the absolutely destitute — and what does she bring? Not food, not material relief. A good book — forced upon them with what Esther calls, and here Dickens is at his most precise: "she really did it as if she were an inexorable moral policeman carrying them all off to a station-house." An inexorable moral policeman. That word — inexorable — is doing the real work. Charity here is not relief — it is coercion dressed in virtue. And Dickens understands — whether he would have put it this way or not — that this is how ideology operates.

And what makes Richard's destruction so legally precise — so historically accurate, if I can put it that way — is the mechanism Dickens uses.
