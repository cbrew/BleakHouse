Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
The first thing Gaskell does when she brings Margaret into Milton is force her — and us — into a very specific architecture of power. The Thornton house sits inside the mill yard — not apart from it, not above it, but literally enclosed within it. On one side, the offices — the transaction of business, as Gaskell puts it — on the opposite, the immense many-windowed mill. The house is handsome, scrupulously maintained, blackened by smoke — and Margaret's instinct is immediately to wonder why anyone would choose this over a smaller house in the country. That instinct tells us everything about Margaret's class position — and about the novel's central argument. But then we get into the drawing-room, and Gaskell does something extraordinary with description. She writes: "Everything reflected light, nothing absorbed it. The whole room had a painfully spotted, spangled, speckled look about it." That is a devastating sentence. The room performs wealth — obsessively, defensively — but it produces nothing warm, nothing at ease. Gaskell calls it — and this is the phrase I keep returning to — "icy, snowy discomfort."

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And that phrase lands so hard precisely because Gaskell has been building it brick by brick. The bagged-up chandelier, the linen drugget over the carpet, the alabaster groups under their glass shades — it is a room that has been sealed against life. And that final clause she adds is almost cruel in its precision: "care and labour to procure ease, to help on habits of tranquil home employment" — that is what is absent. Everything is there to ornament and then to preserve the ornament from dirt. It is a drawing-room that has never drawn anyone in.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me is how historically precise that room actually is. The new manufacturing class in the eighteen-fifties had enormous capital and very little accumulated domestic culture — no inherited portraits, no library built over generations. So they bought the signals of gentility wholesale — and then guarded them ferociously, because they had no other claim to respectability. Gaskell sees this — and she doesn't let it off lightly — but she also doesn't simply mock it. She makes you feel the labour that produced that cold, perfect room.

Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
And that labour question is exactly where this novel becomes most dangerous, most serious. Because Gaskell is not only talking about the labour of keeping a drawing-room spotless. She is talking about what the power of Milton actually costs — and who pays it. There is a passage early in the novel where Gaskell contrasts Mr. Hale's dazzlement at Milton's industrial grandeur with Margaret's much quieter, darker observation. The question Gaskell puts directly is this: "has everything been done to make the sufferings of these exceptions as small as possible? or, in the triumph of the crowded procession, have the helpless been trampled on" That is not a rhetorical flourish. That is the moral engine of the entire novel, stated plainly, in chapter eight.

It was about two miles from their house in Crampton Crescent to Marlborough Street. It was too hot to walk very quickly. An August sun beat straight down into the street at three o’clock in the afternoon. Margaret went along, without noticing anything very different from usual in the first mile and a half of her journey; she was absorbed in her own thoughts, and had learnt by this time to thread her way through the irregular stream of human beings that flowed through Milton streets. But, by and by, she was struck with an unusual heaving among the mass of people in the crowded road on which she was entering. They did not appear to be moving on, so much as talking, and listening, and buzzing with excitement without much stirring from the spot where they might happen to be. Still, as they made way for her, and, wrapt up in the purpose of her errand, and the necessities that suggested it, she was less quick of observation than she might have been, if her mind had been at ease. She had got into Marlborough Street before the full conviction forced itself upon her, that there was a restless, oppressive sense of irritation abroad among the people; a thunderous atmosphere, morally as well as physically, around her. From every narrow lane opening out on Marlborough Street came up a low distant roar, as of myriads of fierce indignant voices. The inhabitants of each poor squalid dwelling were gathered round the doors and windows, if indeed they were not actually standing in the middle of the narrow ways—all with looks intent towards one point. Marlborough Street itself was the focus of all those human eyes, that betrayed intensest interest of various kinds; some fierce with anger, some lowering with relentless threats! some dilated with fear, or imploring entreaty; and as Margaret reached the small side-entrance by the folding doors, in the great dead wall of Marlborough mill-yard, and waited the porter’s answer to the bell, she looked round and heard the first long far-off roll of the tempest;—saw the first slow-surging wave of the dark crowd come, with its threatening crest, tumble over, and retreat, at the far end of the street, which a moment ago seemed so full of repressed noise, but which now was ominously still; all these circumstances forced themselves on Margaret’s notice, but did not sink down into her pre-occupied heart. She did not know what they meant—what was their deep significance; while she did know, did feel the keen sharp pressure of the knife that was soon to stab her through and through by leaving her motherless. She was trying to realise that, in order that, when it came, she might be ready to comfort her father.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me most about Margaret in these early Milton chapters is how thoroughly Gaskell refuses to protect her. There's a passage in chapter eight that I find almost unbearably precise about what it means to fall out of the world you were made for. Gaskell writes of Margaret's old London life: "The smooth sea of that old life closed up, without a mark left to tell where they had all been." That image — a smooth sea closing over them — is devastating. Society has not even registered their departure. The habitual dinners, the calls, the dancing evenings — all going on, going on forever, without them. And what does Margaret do with that recognition? She finds a strange, almost stoic solace in it. "They were at the lowest now; they could not be worse." It reads almost like she's talking herself off a ledge. But there's a peculiar dignity to it — and it's the beginning of something harder and tougher in Margaret's character.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto that word, 'lowest,' because I think Gaskell is doing something very specific there. Margaret's grief is real — but it's still the grief of someone who has descended into the professional middle class, not into the working class. The charwomen who can't be hired, the factory girls who are deemed unsuitable for a gentleman's house — they are the true floor. But what Gaskell is brilliant at is making that proximity legible — Margaret's fall brings her close enough to witness the structure below her. And then by chapter fifteen, that witness has sharpened into something like analysis. She puts it with extraordinary clarity: "I see two classes dependent on each other in every possible way, yet each evidently regarding the interests of the other as opposed to their own." That is not a sentimental observation. That is a structural diagnosis, delivered by a woman who has no formal education in political economy. The North has educated her in a way the South never could.
