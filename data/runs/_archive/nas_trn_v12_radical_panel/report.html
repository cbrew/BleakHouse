Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Milton is not a backdrop — it is an argument. Gaskell builds an environment in which the pressure of industrial capitalism is felt on the body — on skin, on breath, on the pace at which a person walks. And she's extraordinarily specific about how that pressure fractures individuals in different ways depending on where they sit in the hierarchy. There is a passage that stays with me — Thornton leaving the borough court after dealing with the riot cases, walking through the streets of Milton in a kind of dissociation. He went along the crowded streets mechanically, winding in and out among the people, but never seeing them. That word — mechanically — is doing extraordinary work. Thornton has absorbed the rhythm of the machines he owns, and it has colonised his own movement through the world. The city he built is now walking him.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take your point about the atmosphere, Dr. Rosen, but I'd resist the mechanical explanation — if you'll forgive the pun. Thornton is walking through Milton unseeing not because capitalism has colonised his nervous system, but because he is devastated by private feeling. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Caroline's right that Gaskell refuses the clean separation — and that's actually what makes her more radical than she's given credit for. Consider what the novel does with Boucher — a worker entirely destroyed by the system, and then presented to us in death with a specificity that is almost unbearable to read. His face was swollen and discoloured; besides, his skin was stained by the water in the brook, which had been used for dyeing purposes. The brook used for dyeing purposes — Gaskell cannot let even the water in this world be innocent. Industrial production has contaminated the very element in which this man drowned. That is not atmosphere — that is indictment.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The passage is harrowing — I won't dispute that. But notice what Gaskell reaches for immediately, in response to that horror. Margaret goes forward and softly covered the dead man's countenance with her handkerchief. That gesture — a handkerchief over a ruined face — is not political theory. It is an act of individual moral grace in the face of a world that produces suffering. Gaskell's answer to the question of what Milton does to people is, finally, a human one — not a structural one.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that tension — between the political and the personal — is nowhere more painfully rendered than in what the novel asks us to understand about time. There's a passage about how Thornton will remember his eighteen months in Milton — and how Margaret will remember the same period. To him — so unspeakably precious, down to its very bitterness, which was worth all the rest of life's sweetness. The same city, the same months, the same suffering — experienced as treasure by one person and as wound by another. That asymmetry is not just romantic — it is also class. For Margaret, Milton was a place of loss, hunger, and death. For Thornton, it was a stage lit by the possibility of her.

That tea-table scene is one of those passages I return to every time, because it's doing so many things at once. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Caroline is quite right that the comedy and tension are inseparable here — but I think the moral weight of the scene is carried by what Gaskell tells us about Margaret's headache.
