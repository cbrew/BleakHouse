Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What he does is so audacious that readers in 1852 must have found it genuinely disorienting. The novel opens with no characters, no scene-setting in the conventional sense, no narrator introducing himself. It opens with a condition — fog, mud, and the relentless grinding present tense. The very first word is 'London' — and then Dickens refuses to describe London in the normal way. He describes a city that has become indistinguishable from geological time. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me is where the fog ends up — and that's not accidental. Dickens tracks the fog from the river through the city, and it settles — densest of all — in the Court of Chancery. That is not a meteorological observation. He's telling us from line one that the institution which supposedly administers justice is itself the source of the darkness. The passage that clinches it for me is the one where he positions the Lord Chancellor inside the fog. "On such an afternoon, if ever, the Lord High Chancellor ought to be sitting here — as here he is — with a foggy glory round his head, softly fenced in with crimson cloth and curtains, addressed by a large advocate with great whiskers, a little voice, and an interminable brief." Verified (100%) A foggy glory — it's Dickens at his most precisely savage. The Lord Chancellor has the halo of a saint, but the halo is made of fog. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
'Drones on' — I want to stop at those two words, because they're doing something very sly. 'Drones' is the sound of Chancery — the endless droning of advocates in a fog-filled court. But it's also drone as in something that does no work — a parasite on the hive. Dickens gets both meanings into two syllables, and then he just carries on as if he's done nothing remarkable. That's the thing about performing this opening — you have to resist the temptation to hit every effect. The prose is so densely packed that if you underline everything, you underline nothing. You have to let some of it pass the listener like weather — and trust that it lands anyway.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver's point about the drone actually opens onto something I think is underappreciated about this opening. Dickens is not just describing Chancery — he's describing the people it has destroyed. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to push on the word 'argument' — because Dickens isn't simply offering metaphor here, he's making a forensic case. The fog is not random atmospheric mood-setting. It's the deliberate obfuscation of a system that benefits from not being understood. Chancery is not broken — and this is the point the novel insists on, again and again. It is working exactly as it was designed to work: to protect property, to extract fees, and to make the cost of resistance higher than the cost of submission. Look at the Jarndyce suitors — Miss Flite, Richard Carstone, even old Tom Jarndyce who blew his brains out in a coffee-house. They are not victims of accident or bad luck — they are the product of a machine that grinds people down in order to sustain itself. And Dickens frames this with almost unbearable precision when he describes the case of Jarndyce and Jarndyce itself. "Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. This scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means." Verified (100%) 'Scarecrow' — not a labyrinth, not a puzzle, but a scarecrow: hollow, constructed, designed to frighten the living away from something of value.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Daniel's reading of 'scarecrow' is exactly right — it's a word that reveals Dickens's hand as a crafter of argument through image. But I want to add the structural dimension — because what's so remarkable about the Chancery satire is that it never relents. Most Victorian novels of social criticism make their point, and then the story moves elsewhere. Dickens keeps Chancery active for nearly nine hundred pages — as a presence, a threat, an ongoing drain. Think of how he handles Richard Carstone's decline — it is a slow-motion portrait of a man being consumed by a legal system that promises everything and delivers nothing. Richard can't pursue a profession because the case is always there, always almost resolved, always suggesting that once it's settled he can really begin to live. That 'always almost' is the psychological mechanism of Chancery — it's hope deployed as a weapon.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver's point about Richard is crucial, and it connects directly to the class dimension that Dickens is careful — perhaps strategically careful — not to overstate. Richard is middle-class, educated, sympathetic. His destruction by Chancery is the one the novel grieves openly, because he is legible to the novel's audience as one of their own. But what about the people the system destroys who are never given a chapter to mourn in? Jo the crossing-sweeper — illiterate, homeless, moved on by every policeman and philanthropist alike — he is what Chancery produces at the bottom of the social order. And Dickens gives him that magnificent, terrible apostrophe — the most radical passage in the book, I'd argue. "Dead, your Majesty. Dead, my lords and gentlemen. Dead, right reverends and wrong reverends of every order. Dead, men and women, born with heavenly compassion in your hearts. And dying thus around us every day." Verified (100%) That is not sentiment — that is an indictment. Dickens is addressing the ruling class directly, naming their offices, their titles, their pretensions to compassion — and telling them that children are dying because of what they have chosen not to do.
