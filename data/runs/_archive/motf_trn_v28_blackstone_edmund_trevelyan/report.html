What strikes me first, returning to these early chapters, is the precision of Eliot's moral observation of a child. We are not being asked to find Maggie charming, exactly — though she is. The gypsy episode is funny, of course — but it's also a document of how a particular kind of child thinks about the margins of respectable society. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What James points to is important — but I think the narrator's own position in this passage is worth dwelling on. Eliot introduces a remarkable observation about the nature of Maggie's education — or rather, her self-education. The passage reads, in part: "in travelling over her small mind you would have found the most unexpected ignorance as well as unexpected knowledge." She has deduced the meaning of the prefix poly from the dictionary — this child who has read the dictionary for want of other books. And yet she has no idea that gypsies are not well supplied with groceries. Eliot calls it — and it is a marvellous formulation — the oddest mixture of clear-eyed acumen and blind dreams. That is not a description of a child's limitation — it is a description of a certain kind of genius, poorly served by circumstance.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Tom's speech to Philip is the novel's most explicit statement of the social logic that the ruin has enforced. Honour, in Tom's world, is entirely external — it consists in what others may be made to laugh at or respect. Philip's body becomes evidence — evidence of unworthiness, of presumption. And this is where the novel's broader moral argument enters — because Eliot is building, across the whole book, toward a critique of exactly this kind of judgment by rule and appearance. She gives us the counter-statement with characteristic directness — that the mysterious complexity of our life is not to be embraced by maxims. "the mysterious complexity of our life is not to be embraced by maxims, and that to lace ourselves up in formulas of that sort is to repress all the divine promptings and inspirations that spring from growing insight and sympathy." Tom Tulliver is the novel's great man of maxims — and the ruin of the family is what his maxims have cost.

For Mr Tulliver was in a position neither new nor striking, but, like other everyday things, sure to have a cumulative effect that will be felt in the long run: he was held to be a much more substantial man than he really was. And as we are all apt to believe what the world believes about us, it was his habit to think of failure and ruin with the same sort of remote pity with which a spare, long-necked man hears that his plethoric short-necked neighbour is stricken with apoplexy. He had been always used to hear pleasant jokes about his advantages as a man who worked his own mill, and owned a pretty bit of land; and these jokes naturally kept up his sense that he was a man of considerable substance. Maggie was only dimly conscious of the banks, as they passed them, and dwelt with no recognition on the villages; she knew there were several to be passed before they reached Luckreth, where they always stopped and left the boat. At all times she was so liable to fits of absence, that she was likely enough to let her waymarks pass unnoticed.

James is absolutely right — and yet I want to resist any reading that entirely externalises the moral weight. Eliot is not writing a novel of social determinism. Maggie knows the waymarks are there. The tragedy is that she is both fully conscious and fully suspended in that consciousness — and Eliot holds both conditions simultaneously without resolving the tension. That, to my mind, is what places this novel among the very highest achievements of the English imagination.
