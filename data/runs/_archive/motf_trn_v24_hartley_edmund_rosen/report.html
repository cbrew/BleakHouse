Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The first thing to understand about Maggie is that she loves too fiercely for the world she's born into. And Eliot tracks that love with extraordinary precision — not sentimentally, but structurally. There's a moment early in the novel where we see Maggie face her father's helplessness — and the narrator's comment on it is devastating in its simplicity. Eliot writes of what Maggie experiences as — "that simple, primitive love which knits us to the beings who have been nearest to us, in their times of helplessness or of anguish." That word "primitive" is doing enormous work. It's not degrading — it means foundational, pre-rational, the love that exists before judgment has a chance to operate. And notice the narrator says "we" — she pulls the reader directly into Maggie's condition. This is Eliot's great technique — the generalising narrator who makes one girl's private anguish the anguish of all of us.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold both of those readings and then press a little harder on the social conditions that make Maggie's love so dangerous. There's another passage where Eliot is absolutely explicit about what Maggie's emotional world is set against. She writes — "There is no hopelessness so sad as that of early youth, when the soul is made up of wants, and has no long memories, no superadded life in the life of others." Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Daniel's point about the present tense is sharp — and it connects to something I find technically thrilling in the way Eliot handles Maggie's relationship with Tom. Because Tom is, in a sense, the mechanism through which the world exerts its pressure on Maggie's conscience. Listen to how she frames his moral certainty — "Tom was not given to inquire subtly into his own motives any more than into other matters of an intangible kind; he was quite sure that his own motives as well as actions were good, else he would have had nothing to do with them." Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That scene is one of Eliot's most audacious structural gambits in the entire novel. She reaches for the language of classical tragedy — and then deflates it, precisely and deliberately. The narrator sets it up with almost theatrical gravity — listen to this: "There were passions at war in Maggie at that moment to have made a tragedy, if tragedies were made by passion only; but the essential τι μέγεθος which was present in the passion was wanting to the action." The Greek phrase — τι μέγεθος, meaning a certain greatness of scale — is borrowed from Aristotle's Poetics. Eliot is invoking the full weight of tragic theory — and then the action that follows is to push little Lucy into cow-trodden mud. The bathos is total — and it is the point. Maggie has the inner life of a tragic heroine trapped in the body and circumstances of a child in a provincial mill town.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is absolutely right to locate the pathos there — and I'd add that this disproportion never quite resolves itself. It is Maggie's permanent condition: passion that exceeds the scale of available action. What strikes me is that this is not a failure of society — it is a feature of Maggie's particular, singular character. Eliot gives us the same structural irony with her father, and it illuminates something quite different. Mr Tulliver vows he will not borrow from any client of Wakem's — and yet the world arranges itself against him. The narrator frames it thus: "Mr Tulliver had a destiny as well as Œdipus, and in this case he might plead, like Œdipus, that his deed was inflicted on him rather than committed by him." That is the tragic register applied to a man of very modest station — and it is neither satirical nor condescending. Eliot grants him full moral dignity precisely because his defeat is genuine, not comic.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto that image of the world arranging itself — because for Eliot it is not abstract fate. It is specifically Wakem — specifically the legal and financial machinery of provincial capitalism — that closes around Mr Tulliver. The Oedipal frame is brilliant precisely because it names the cruelty: his deed was inflicted on him. And the same structure of constraint shapes the Dodson world that Maggie must grow up inside. Eliot's portrait of Dodson religion is devastating — and it is a portrait of a class ideology masquerading as virtue. The passage describes a religion of respectability — proper pall-bearers, well-cured hams, unimpeachable wills. But Eliot puts it perfectly: "Their religion was of a simple, semi-pagan kind, but there was no heresy in it,—if heresy properly means choice,—for they didn't know there was any other religion, except that of chapel-goers, which appeared to run in families, like asthma." No heresy because no choice — that is the condition of the world Maggie is born into. Her hunger for mind and spirit is not a personal quirk — it is the logical protest of a large consciousness against a world that has abolished the very concept of alternatives.

That word — heresy, properly meaning choice — is one of Eliot's most quietly lethal observations in the novel. And it sets up what happens when Maggie actually discovers a choice — a different kind of book, a different kind of life. The Thomas à Kempis scene is one of the most carefully staged moments of spiritual awakening in Victorian fiction. The book itself is described with extraordinary physical intimacy — corners turned down, pen-and-ink marks browned by time, made by some hand now forever quiet. What moves me most in that sequence is the narrator's lucidity about what Maggie fails to see. She receives the doctrine of renunciation as the discovery of happiness — not as the acceptance of sorrow. That passage is one of the most explicitly political sentences in Victorian fiction, and it tends to be underestimated because it arrives in what looks like a digression. But it is not a digression. That epitaph is borrowed from Second Samuel — 'Saul and Jonathan, in their lives they were lovely and pleasant, and in their death they were not divided' — and Eliot deploys it with full knowledge of its weight. She's claiming for Maggie and Tom a heroic, even sacred scale of fraternal love — while simultaneously showing us a society that made that love impossible to sustain in life. The craft of it is devastating — the gap between the grandeur of the allusion and the smallness of the provincial world that destroyed them.
