Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The Sol's Arms is one of the great minor triumphs of the novel — a pub that becomes a sort of accidental theatre of justice, or rather, injustice made palatable. An inquest has just been held there — a man has died in squalor, a crossing-sweeper has been brushed aside as a witness, and now, same evening, same room: the Harmonic Meeting. Dickens writes it with this extraordinary compression — the pub melts into darkness, flares back up in gaslight, and suddenly the whole machinery of death has been converted into after-dinner entertainment. And then Little Swills steps up — and here's the line that kills me every time — he says, "Gentlemen, if you'll permit me, I'll attempt a short description of a scene of real life that came off here to-day." And he's applauded — warmly applauded — before he's even started. Then he does his impression of the coroner — note that: not the least in the world like him — and the refrain is this absurdist tippy-tol-li-doll nonsense. Dickens is doing something very precise here: the law has a comic understudy, and the audience loves the understudy more than the real thing.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver's right that it's precise — but I'd push one step further. What Dickens is showing us is how institutions neutralise their own violence through spectacle. The inquest produced nothing — Jo was dismissed as a witness because he couldn't articulate his oath in the approved form. A man is dead, a child is silenced, and the system has functioned exactly as designed. And then — within hours — that same system becomes the subject of a comic song. The laughter doesn't critique the inquest — it absorbs it, digests it, makes it harmless. This is the genius of Dickens the radical — he shows us how thoroughly the working class has been trained to applaud the mechanisms of its own exclusion.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Vholes is, for my money, the most quietly terrifying character in the novel — more than Tulkinghorn, more than Krook. Because Tulkinghorn is visibly sinister — you can see him operating. Vholes is more dangerous because he is utterly placid. Richard Carstone says nothing has been done — and Vholes simply, gently corrects him. "Don't say nothing done, sir. That is scarcely fair, sir, scarcely fair!" That repetition — scarcely fair, scarcely fair — is not emphasis, it's procedure. Vholes isn't defending the law — he's defending the bill. Something has been done — countless somethings — and every one of them has cost Richard money and produced precisely nothing of value. Vholes is the law's digestive system — slow, thorough, and entirely indifferent to what it consumes.
