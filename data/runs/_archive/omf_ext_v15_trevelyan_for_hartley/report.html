Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
The thing you have to understand about Our Mutual Friend is that Dickens is writing a novel about rubbish — and he means that quite seriously. The dust heaps are real — enormous mounds of ash, cinders, and refuse on the edge of London that could make a man spectacularly wealthy. Old Harmon built his fortune on them, and now his former servant Boffin has inherited the lot. The moment that news becomes public, Dickens shows us exactly what happens — and it is one of the funniest passages he ever wrote. He gives us the Secretary sorting through the flood of letters, and what lands first is this: "Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet." Six words of physical description and you already know exactly what kind of man sent it — and what he wants. That is Dickens working at full comic speed — the adjectives doing the moral work.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the Duke of Linseed is not entirely a caricature — that is what makes it sting. The great Victorian charities absolutely did operate this way — recruiting aristocratic patrons not for their ideas but for their names on a letterhead, and charging them a fee for the privilege. The comedy Dickens is writing here has a precise legal and institutional target. He tells us the letter is lithographed — printed by the hundred — and that Boffin's name has been filled in by a different hand. The Duke has never thought about Nicodemus Boffin for a single moment. Dickens calls it "friendly" on the Duke's part — and adds, with magnificent deadpan, "and thoughtful in the postscript." The postscript being, of course, the three-guinea fee.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I read this section is that Dickens doesn't just satirise the nobility — he moves immediately to the individual beggars, and that shift is devastating. Because these are people in genuine distress — or claiming to be — and the prose holds both the comedy and the pathos at once. There is a line about the documents they enclose that I find extraordinary. "They call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf." That simile is so peculiar, so specific, and so perfectly right that you have to stop and read it again. Minced veal to a calf — all the substance has been processed out of it. And yet it is also quietly grotesque — because a calf didn't choose to become veal.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And all of this — the dukes, the beggars, the charities — circles around a single legal fact that Dickens sets up with extraordinary care. The Harmon fortune exists because of a will — a strange, punitive will built by a man with no friends and a rebellious family. When Wegg reads out the terms of an earlier will he discovers, the language Dickens gives him is both satirical and precise. "Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown." "Which is quite enough for him" — that aside is extraordinary. Old Harmon is making a moral judgment from beyond the grave — deciding what someone else deserves, and recording his contempt in a legal instrument. Wills in Victorian England were exactly that — instruments of control, reward, and punishment — and Dickens understood that better than almost any other novelist.

The thing about Dickens and money is that he never lectures you directly — he builds you a dining room and lets you feel the horror for yourself. There's a character introduced in the opening pages — Twemlow — and the way Dickens introduces him is, frankly, one of the great comic inventions in English prose. He calls him, to your face, without apology — listen to this — "an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James's, when not in use." A human being. Introduced as furniture. And the sustained metaphor that follows — Twemlow as the dining table that gets extended with leaves the more people are invited — is executed with such deadpan relentlessness that you almost forget it's monstrous. Almost. The punch is in the last detail: the more guests are added, the further Twemlow finds himself from the centre of the table.

There was an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James’s, when not in use, to whom the Veneerings were a source of blind confusion. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I love about that passage is that it does two things at once — it's genuinely funny, and it's quietly devastating. Twemlow has a name — he's a cousin to Lord Snigsworth, which is itself a tiny comedy — but Dickens has decided that in this world, lineage without wealth makes you an object. And the Veneerings, who represent new money with no soul, they use him as social infrastructure. It sets up everything the novel will interrogate about what money does to people — not abstractly, but very precisely: it decides where you sit at the table. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Veneering world is not satirical exaggeration — or not only that. The mid-Victorian dinner party was a genuinely elaborate mechanism for sorting and displaying social rank. Who you could invite, who lent you legitimacy, how the table was arranged — all of it was legible to contemporaries in ways we barely notice now. So Twemlow's function — cousin to a lord, borrowed for social credibility — that was a real transaction. What Dickens has done is literalise it with savage precision — turned the social metaphor into a material one. And once you've seen the furniture metaphor, you can't unsee it when you meet the Boffins — because they represent the exact counter-argument.

‘The last time me and Mrs Boffin saw the poor boy,’ said Mr Boffin, warming (as fat usually does) with a tendency to melt, ‘he was a child of seven year old. For when he came back to make intercession for his sister, me and Mrs Boffin were away overlooking a country contract which was to be sifted before carted, and he was come and gone in a single hour. I say he was a child of seven year old. He was going away, all alone and forlorn, to that foreign school, and he come into our place, situate up the yard of the present Bower, to have a warm at our fire. There was his little scanty travelling clothes upon him. There was his little scanty box outside in the shivering wind, which I was going to carry for him down to the steamboat, as the old man wouldn’t hear of allowing a sixpence coach-money. Mrs Boffin, then quite a young woman and pictur of a full-blown rose, stands him by her, kneels down at the fire, warms her two open hands, and falls to rubbing his cheeks; but seeing the tears come into the child’s eyes, the tears come fast into her own, and she holds him round the neck, like as if she was protecting him, and cries to me, “I’d give the wide wide world, I would, to run away with him!” I don’t say but what it cut me, and but what it at the same time heightened my feelings of admiration for Mrs Boffin. The poor child clings to her for awhile, as she clings to him, and then, when the old man calls, he says “I must go! God bless you!” and for a moment rests his heart against her bosom, and looks up at both of us, as if it was in pain—in agony. Such a look! I went aboard with him (I gave him first what little treat I thought he’d like), and I left him when he had fallen asleep in his berth, and I came back to Mrs Boffin. But tell her what I would of how I had left him, it all went for nothing, for, according to her thoughts, he never changed that look that he had looked up at us two. But it did one piece of good. Mrs Boffin and me had no child of our own, and had sometimes wished that how we had one.
