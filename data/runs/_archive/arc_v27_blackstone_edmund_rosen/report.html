James Blackstone (social_historian)
The first thing to understand about Chancery is that it was not a fiction Dickens invented for satirical effect. The court had been consuming estates, destroying families, and suspending lives for generations before Dickens sat down to write. And the practitioners who fed on it — the Vholeses of the world — were not grotesque exaggerations. They were protected by exactly the kind of circular, self-serving argument that Dickens renders with such devastating precision. Listen to how Kenge defends the indefensible — the setup is worth savouring. "Sir, that class of practitioners would be swept from the face of the earth." Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James is entirely right about the historical substrate, and one shouldn't minimise it. But what strikes me about that passage is the quality of the comedy — it is not merely satirical, it is structurally comic in the manner of Swift. The question numbers in that parliamentary inquiry — five hundred and seventeen thousand eight hundred and sixty-nine — are themselves a kind of joke about bureaucratic infinity. The system does not merely harm people — it bores them into submission with its own enormity. And what Dickens understands — what makes him a great novelist rather than a pamphleteer — is that this machinery has human victims with individual faces. Lady Dedlock is not a symbol of the aristocracy — she is a woman carrying a secret that will destroy her.

I'd push back on the pamphleteer distinction, though — not to diminish Dickens but to complicate it. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and they sit in striking contrast to one another, which I think is deliberate on Dickens's part. The first is the ghost legend of Chesney Wold — Mrs. Rouncewell's account of the old Lady Dedlock who sabotaged her husband's horses during the Civil War. What strikes me historically is how precise Dickens is about the political context — a Royalist household, a wife whose brother had been killed by her husband's kinsman fighting for the same king. The housekeeper tells us her feeling was so violent that she hated the race into which she had married. And then at night — in secret — she goes down to the stables and lames the horses. When her husband catches her and the struggle follows, the text gives us this: "she was lamed in the hip and from that hour began to pine away." That phrase — pine away — carries enormous weight. It is a body broken by a marriage that was itself a political contract — and her resistance to that contract is what destroys her.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Wiglomeration is the word of a man who has survived the system — which is itself revealing. Jarndyce can name it, can coin a satirical term for it, because he has the private income to stand at a safe distance. Richard Carstone, the ward, doesn't have that luxury — and the novel shows us what happens when you don't. I want to put alongside Jarndyce's verbal wit a passage from the Smallweed household — which is Dickens's portrait of what Chancery's economy produces at the human level. Old Grandfather Smallweed is trying to tell his grandson Bart about his father's early death — but he keeps erupting into abuse at his wife. The passage performs its own argument through its syntax — watch how the insults interrupt the inheritance narrative: "just as he was beginning to build up the house that he had been making the foundations for, through many a year—you jade of a magpie, jackdaw, and poll-parrot, what do you mean!—he took ill and died of a low fever." The Smallweeds are a family that has organised its entire existence around accumulation — money is the grammar of every sentence they speak. And yet the old man can't even complete the sentence about his son's death without erupting into violence against his wife. The system that dehumanises the poor and the ward in Chancery also dehumanises the moneylender — it corrodes everything it touches.

That is finely put — and I don't quarrel with it as a reading of the Smallweeds. But I think Dickens is doing something else as well: the Smallweeds have chosen to become what they are. We are told, elsewhere, that they have methodically starved every gentle impulse out of their household — there are no fairy tales, no childhood in that house. That is a moral failure of will, not merely a product of economic circumstance. My passage concerns Mr. Bucket — and it gives us a very different kind of professional deformation. Which brings me to the passage that I think is, quietly, the most affecting in the entire novel. He has one thing left to offer Lady Dedlock — his forgiveness, his protection, his loyalty — and he insists on saying it in the proper way, before those who serve his family.
