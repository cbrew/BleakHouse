Thank you — and yes, this novel is a provocation to any writer the moment you open it. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor's right to linger on that — and historically, the detail is devastating in a very specific way. A Chancery suit in the mid-nineteenth century could literally outlast the lifetimes of everyone involved in originating it. Children were born into these suits as heirs — heirs not to property, but to litigation. The Court of Chancery at the time Dickens was writing had a backlog of cases measured not in months but in decades. So when Richard says he was born into an unfinished contention — that isn't metaphor for Dickens's first readers. That was a recognisable, common, legal reality. The genius — and Eleanor is pointing at this — is that Dickens turns a legal fact into a psychological portrait of a ruined young man.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And then there are passages in this novel where the gothic atmosphere tips over into something genuinely horrifying — and Dickens earns it completely. The death of Krook — Krook, the grotesque rag-and-bottle merchant, the dark mirror of the Lord Chancellor — is one of the most audacious set pieces in Victorian fiction. He dies by spontaneous combustion. And what's left is described by the omniscient narrator in slow, clinical, unbearable detail. "Here is a small burnt patch of flooring; here is the tinder from a little bundle of burnt paper, but not so light as usual, seeming to be steeped in something." "And here is — is it the cinder of a small charred and broken log of wood sprinkled with white ashes, or is it coal?" "Oh, horror, he IS here!" That capitalised IS — in a novel printed in serial parts, read aloud in parlours — lands like a hammer. The narrator has been performing rational uncertainty — is it wood, is it coal — and then the certainty breaks through with genuine horror. It's one sentence, five words, and it's the most frightening thing in the chapter.

It is extraordinary — and what strikes me most is what Dickens is doing with the very concept of a home in this novel. Yes — and the craft of that passage is worth pausing over, because it's doing something very unusual structurally. The whole long sentence is held in grammatical suspense until the very end — "were our first impressions of Bleak House" — the verb withheld, everything piling up before the main clause resolves. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold that thought about interrupted stories, because the contrast Dickens sets up is not accidental. Bleak House the building is warm and hospitable precisely because Jarndyce has withdrawn from the suit — he refuses to let Chancery colonise his inner life. But the people who are fully inside the system — Richard Carstone above all — have no such shelter. And what Chancery destroys, it destroys absolutely. Gridley's words, echoed through Richard's departure, make this devastatingly clear. "Of all my old associations, of all my old pursuits and hopes, of all the living and the dead world, this one poor soul alone comes natural to me, and I am fit for. And the historical reality underpins every word of that. The Court of Chancery by Dickens's time had accumulated a backlog of cases that stretched back generations — cases in which the original litigants had died, their children had died, and their grandchildren were still waiting.
