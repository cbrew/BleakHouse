Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's a passage that could read as pure charm — Esther peeping over Charley's shoulder, the comedy of a pen that behaves like a 'saddle-donkey.' But Dickens is doing something much more precise here — he's constructing a tiny emblem of the whole novel's concern with writing, documents, and what hands can and cannot do. The simile is where the passage earns its keep. Listen to how he puts it: "every pen appeared to become perversely animated, and to go wrong and crooked, and to stop, and splash, and sidle into corners like a saddle-donkey." The accumulation of verbs — wrong, crooked, stop, splash, sidle — that's not decoration; that's comic rhythm built out of grammar itself. And then that gorgeous paradox: 'what old letters Charley's young hand had made, they so wrinkled and shrivelled and tottering, it so plump and round.' That contrast — the ancient, decrepit text produced by the young, capable hand — is Dickens in miniature. It's comic, yes, but it's also the novel's central anxiety: that the legal documents shaping these young lives are monstrous old things, written in a hand no one living chose.

Caroline, that's exactly right — and it connects to what Dickens does with the whole ruling class in this novel, which is show how they are managed even as they believe themselves to be managing.
