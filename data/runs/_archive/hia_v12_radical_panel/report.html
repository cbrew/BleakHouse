Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me immediately about Dickens's legal world — even in its dormancy — is that it remains a structure of extraction. The long vacation in Chancery Lane isn't a holiday — it's the system on standby, still consuming. And Dickens announces this with a metaphor so perfectly chosen it almost feels too good. "The good ships Law and Equity, those teak-built, copper-bottomed, iron-fastened, brazen-faced, and not by any means fast-sailing clippers are laid up in ordinary." That list — teak-built, copper-bottomed, iron-fastened, brazen-faced — reads like a company prospectus. Solid, respectable, advertised to inspire confidence — and then you hit "brazen-faced," and the whole edifice tilts. The system is shameless, and Dickens knows it, and he smuggles the verdict in between the compliments.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And the system doesn't only consume its clients — it consumes its children. The Smallweeds are the most concentrated image of this in the novel. Dickens traces this family across generations — each one going out early, marrying late, discarding everything that isn't profit. The house of Smallweed, Dickens tells us, has — and I want to read this carefully — "discarded all amusements, discountenanced all story-books, fairy-tales, fictions, and fables, and banished all levities whatsoever." That list — story-books, fairy-tales, fictions, fables — is not incidental. Dickens is telling us that what the Smallweeds have extinguished is the imagination itself — and with it, any capacity for sympathy. The system doesn't just impoverish the poor — it produces a class of people who cannot conceive of the poor as human.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I would resist the urge to make the Smallweeds too representative. They are grotesques — magnificently observed grotesques — but grotesques nonetheless. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Caroline is right to dwell on the word vocation, because it carries genuine theological weight that Dickens deploys with great precision. A vocation is a calling — something that comes from outside the self and lays a claim upon you. Richard's tragedy, as I read it, is not social — it is moral and, in the deepest sense, spiritual. He confesses it himself, quite openly, to Esther — and the confession is both touching and damning. "I seem to want something or other to stand by. Even you have no idea how fond I am of Ada — but I don't settle down to constancy in other things. It's such uphill work, and it takes such a time!" 'It takes such a time.' The vexation in that phrase is almost comic — and then one remembers what it will cost him. Richard is not wicked. He is inconstant — and in Dickens's moral universe, inconstancy in the face of genuine obligation is its own category of failure.

Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
Jarndyce's shadow is real — but let's be honest about what he's watching, and what he cannot prevent. By Chapter Twenty-Three, Richard's 'industry' — Esther's word — has become entirely misdirected toward the suit. He has convinced himself the judgment is imminent. He has begun to haunt the Court. He has made friends with Miss Flite. And Esther sees exactly what that friendship means — even if Richard cannot. Free hopes. Caged birds. The parallel is not accidental — it is the whole argument of the novel in a single sentence. Miss Flite is what Richard is becoming. And Chancery — not Richard's weakness alone — is the mechanism that produces both of them.

Our sitting-room was green and had framed and glazed upon the walls numbers of surprising and surprised birds, staring out of pictures at a real trout in a case, as brown and shining as if it had been served with gravy; at the death of Captain Cook; and at the whole process of preparing tea in China, as depicted by Chinese artists. In my room there were oval engravings of the months—ladies haymaking in short waists and large hats tied under the chin, for June; smooth-legged noblemen pointing with cocked-hats to village steeples, for October. Half-length portraits in crayons abounded all through the house, but were so dispersed that I found the brother of a youthful officer of mine in the china-closet and the grey old age of my pretty young bride, with a flower in her bodice, in the breakfast-room. As substitutes, I had four angels, of Queen Anne’s reign, taking a complacent gentleman to heaven, in festoons, with some difficulty; and a composition in needlework representing fruit, a kettle, and an alphabet. All the movables, from the wardrobes to the chairs and tables, hangings, glasses, even to the pincushions and scent-bottles on the dressing-tables, displayed the same quaint variety. They agreed in nothing but their perfect neatness, their display of the whitest linen, and their storing-up, wheresoever the existence of a drawer, small or large, rendered it possible, of quantities of rose-leaves and sweet lavender. Such, with its illuminated windows, softened here and there by shadows of curtains, shining out upon the starlight night; with its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw; and just wind enough without to sound a low accompaniment to everything we heard, were our first impressions of Bleak House.
