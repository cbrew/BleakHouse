Yes — and it is a scene that Gaskell almost smuggles past you, it's so light on its feet. Thornton arrives at the Hales' sitting room, and Margaret is pouring tea — and Gaskell turns this ordinary domestic ritual into something quite electric. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to add a layer of social reality to what we're admiring, because the scene has genuine stakes beyond the comedy. Thornton is a manufacturer — he is, in the precise language of the period, in trade. The Hales are a clergyman's family — they are gentry, however reduced, and the distinction was not trivial. So when Margaret serves Thornton tea with the proud air of an unwilling slave, she is enacting a class performance — hospitality extended across a boundary she quietly resents. And Gaskell understands that these domestic rituals were the grammar of social life — every cup of tea was a statement.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a very precise class grammar operating in that scene, and it's worth pausing on. Thornton is a manufacturer at the height of his powers — a man who has earned significant wealth through his own labour and intellect. By the standards of the industrial north, he is a figure of considerable consequence. And yet Margaret's sheer ease unmoors him. The 'poor fellow' is genius, because it also tells us something about where Margaret is in Chapter Nine. She hasn't seen him clearly yet. She knows he is impressive. She has registered the empress-in-a-bonnet effect from his side. But she hasn't understood his actual moral seriousness. And Gaskell's structural gambit is to show us that moral seriousness through Mrs. Thornton — of all people — before we even see it in Thornton himself.
