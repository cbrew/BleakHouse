Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Thank you — and I have to say, the architectural question is exactly the right one to start with. Dickens builds this novel on two pillars: a pitiless, omniscient narrator who dissects society, and Esther Summerson, whose first-person voice is intimate, fallible, domestic. Those two voices are in permanent tension, and that tension is the engine of the whole book. But what truly interests me is the moment that dual structure delivers its most devastating blow — and it comes through Esther herself. Near the very end of the novel, Esther goes to a gate in the dark and kneels down. "I passed on to the gate and stooped down. I lifted the heavy head, put the long dank hair aside, and turned the face. And it was my mother, cold and dead." Six words after a sentence of physical precision — and the novel's central secret collapses into them. That is Dickens at the height of his structural power: earning the devastation through the accumulated weight of everything before it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
It is a remarkable passage, I agree — but I should resist the temptation to begin at the end. What strikes me about this novel, at every level, is that Dickens is fundamentally a moralist — and the law is his great antagonist because it has ceased to be moral. One of the omniscient narrator's most scathing observations concerns the nature of English law itself. "The one great principle of the English law is to make business for itself." Now, one might dismiss that as polemic — and it is polemic, Dickens never denies it. But beneath the satirical thrust lies a genuinely conservative complaint: that an institution has abandoned its proper function and serves only its own perpetuation. Burke would have recognised it immediately.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline, that observation about the dialect is so right — and it connects to something I keep coming back to about the omniscient narrator's own rhetoric. When the narrator dissects the law, the prose is controlled, almost lawyerly in its repetition. "There is no other principle distinctly, certainly, and consistently maintained through all its narrow turnings" — Dickens is imitating the very thing he condemns. And then there is Tulkinghorn — who embodies that legal machinery at its most personally sinister. He speaks of delivering a shock to Sir Leicester and, in his own words, managing what he calls the consequences. "If I inflicted this shock upon him to-morrow morning, how could the immediate change in him be accounted for?" It's the phrase "inflicted" that betrays him — he knows exactly what he is doing to this family. The law, in Tulkinghorn, does not merely make business — it makes cruelty feel administrative.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I would want to pause on Mr. Jarndyce before we move too quickly toward Richard's failure. Because what Jarndyce says to these young people in Chapter Thirteen is — in the strictest sense — morally admirable. He refuses to lecture them about imprudence. He simply offers them his confidence and his friendship, and asks only for honesty in return. He says — "If you DO change — if you DO come to find that you are more commonplace cousins to each other as man and woman than you were as boy and girl — don't be ashamed still to confide in me, for there will be nothing monstrous or uncommon in it." That parenthetical — your manhood will excuse me, Rick — is extraordinarily delicate for a Victorian novelist. Jarndyce treats Richard as a person capable of growth and change — which is, in the end, a greater compliment than certainty.

And then Jarndyce gives Richard the most direct piece of moral instruction in the chapter — which Dickens embeds inside what feels like a bracing, affectionate speech.
