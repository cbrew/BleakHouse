Bleak House is not a novel about Chancery's chaos—it's a novel that uses musical form to argue that Chancery operates through false rigidity. The long vacation passage freezes the legal system in what seems like harmonic suspension, but Dickens is being precise: in music, suspension requires resolution. The Court reconvenes. Sarah Chen (computational_linguist)
Two registers is probably more precise than two languages — but the distinction is almost as stark. What the classifier finds is that domestic Bleak House sentences tend to use nominal subjects — named people, named objects — acting on the world. Chancery sentences systematically suppress agents — the passive voice proliferates, nominalizations crowd out verbs, and the grammatical subject is often an abstraction. And here is what that means for reading Dickens: he has built the novel's moral argument into its grammar, not just its plot. The legal world doesn't just do harmful things — it constructs sentences in which nobody is doing anything to anybody. Look at the c19 passage: 'The good ships Law and Equity are laid up in ordinary.' 'The courts are all shut up; the public offices lie in a hot sleep.' Everything is passive, somnolent — and crucially, nobody closed those offices, nobody put those ships in harbor. The grammar enacts the very evasion of accountability the novel is attacking.

Richard Carstone is the only character in *Bleak House* whose internal contradictions are made temporally legible—Dickens allows us to measure them, track them, and watch them accumulate. Richard's moral collapse is not caused by the Chancery lawsuit; it is caused by the fact that he lives in rhythmic time while the novel's institutions live in procedural repetition.
