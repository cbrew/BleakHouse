Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is quite right that the architecture is deliberate — though I would want to resist the temptation to read the fog too narrowly. There is an old critical habit of treating the fog as a simple equation — fog equals Chancery, Chancery equals the system, the system equals oppression. And that, I think, is rather to flatten what Dickens is doing. The fog, in this opening chapter, is a profoundly moral atmosphere — not merely a social one. Dickens writes of the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln's Inn Hall, and he gives us this marvellous phrase — the 'groping and floundering' condition of those before the court. "Fog everywhere" is the human condition of moral confusion — not merely a legislative complaint. What Dickens understands — and this is where his genius lies — is that institutions corrupt not because they are evil in themselves, but because they become substitutes for conscience. Chancery is not simply a bad court — it is a court in which hope has curdled into habit.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right that the technique is masterly, but I think we should pause on what that pallor actually signifies — morally. Lady Dedlock is not merely startled — she is, at that precise instant, confronted with the full weight of a life she has concealed. A child she believes dead — a lover she has never named — and a secret that, if known, would destroy the entire edifice of her position. Dickens was deeply interested — as was Burke, whom he had almost certainly read — in how a great social position can become a kind of moral prison. What interests me most, however, is not the scandal but the response — the instant recovery, the way she regains her composure within seconds. That is not weakness — that is a formidable will.
