Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Thank you — yes, and I think that passage is remarkable precisely because of what Eliot does with time. Maggie is a child, and this is a child's moment — and yet the narrator insists it belongs to a category of adult experience that most people reach only much later in life. The phrase Eliot uses is extraordinary. "that simple, primitive love which knits us to the beings who have been nearest to us, in their times of helplessness or of anguish" The word "knits" — not bonds, not ties — knits. It implies something domestic and painstaking and continuous — love as a practice, not a feeling that simply arrives. And notice that Eliot places the narrator in the first person plural — "knits us," "nearest to us." She pulls every reader into Maggie's experience, makes it universal — this has happened to you, or it will. That is a very deliberate and very confident narrative move, and it sets the emotional stakes for everything that follows.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right to dwell on that word "primitive," I think, because it carries the novel's deepest argument. Eliot is not using it pejoratively — she means something closer to foundational, elemental. This is the love that precedes all calculation, all resentment, all the accumulated grievances of family life. And it is precisely that love which makes Maggie's situation so painful throughout the novel. She cannot simply reason herself free of her father and her brother, however much they constrain her, because this bond is prior to reason. We see this again with the father himself, near the end — and there is a line of Mr Tulliver's that I find almost unbearable in its simplicity. "we shall never be young again—this world's been too many for me" There is no anger left in him there, no accusation — only exhaustion, and a kind of battered tenderness toward his wife. That line does more for his character than pages of description could.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto what Edmund said about that exhaustion — because the question the novel keeps pressing is: what has exhausted him, and why? "This world's been too many for me" — the world, not a person, not bad luck. Tulliver is a man destroyed by a legal and financial system he never had the means to navigate — and the Tullivers' ruin is entirely legible as a class story. But here is what makes the novel genuinely complex: that primitive love Eleanor identified, that foundational bond Edmund speaks of, becomes the very instrument through which the family's humiliation is reproduced. Look at what Tom does with it. "I can't trust you, Maggie. There is no consistency in you... while I have been contriving and working that my father may have some peace of mind before he dies,—working for the respectability of our family,—you have done all you can to destroy both" Tom weaponises the family's love and suffering — uses their father's dying wish, the sacrifices made — as a cudgel against his sister's autonomy. The bond that should shelter Maggie becomes the mechanism of her enclosure. Eliot is clear-eyed about this — she loves the Tullivers and she indicts them simultaneously.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Daniel, that phrase — "shelter" becoming "enclosure" — is exactly the double movement that Eliot builds into every major scene involving the Tulliver family. And what strikes me technically is how the Bible-oath scene is staged. Tom says, "Do what I require" — not ask, not request: require. He is demanding a spoken formula — a liturgical renunciation — as if love itself can be regulated by oath. The form of the scene mimics a religious rite, and that mimicry is deeply ironic — because what Tom is actually enforcing is respectability, not conscience. Eliot understands that the machinery of moral language can be entirely captured by social anxiety.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take Daniel's point about enclosure, and Eleanor's about the irony — though I would resist framing Tom purely as a villain of the piece. Tom has genuinely sacrificed — he has surrendered his own prospects, his own youth, to honour his father's name. His tragedy is that he cannot see how his own discipline has calcified into something rigid and punishing. He has made duty his entire self, and duty without imagination becomes — as Burke might have said — mere compulsion dressed in virtue's clothing. The pathos of the novel, for me, is that both Tom and Maggie are, in their different ways, destroyed by the same primitive bond — the same love that Eliot shows us so movingly in that early passage.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about that passage is how Eliot complicates Tom before she celebrates Maggie. Tom is not simply the dull, obstructing brother — not yet, not here. Eliot gives us something surprisingly tender: a boy whose pride has been entirely stripped away, and who is left with nothing but human sensibility. And then she does something architecturally brilliant — she turns that humiliation into prayer. Tom can't improvise in class, he can't improvise socially — and he almost can't improvise spiritually either. He is so bound by form and precedent that adding a single extempore line to his evening prayers feels like an act of bold transgression. Listen to what he actually says when he finally dares it — the narrator sets this up with such careful restraint: "and please to make me always remember my Latin." It's heartbreaking precisely because it's so small — and so earnest.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Quite so — and I think one must resist the temptation to condescend to Tom here, which is a temptation Eliot herself occasionally indulges. What Eliot is actually rendering is something Johnson would have recognised: the moral weight of honest effort. Tom is not lazy, not rebellious — he is simply in the wrong place, fitted with the wrong tools. The prayer is touching because it reveals a character still capable of supplication — still reaching beyond himself for help. The final petition — asking that Mr. Stelling should simply make him stop doing Euclid — is of course comic. But comedy in Eliot is rarely decorative; it illuminates character with a precision that pure pathos cannot.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto something Eleanor said — about form and precedent binding Tom so tightly he can barely speak a free sentence even to God. That is not individual temperament alone — that is a class formation. Tom has been shaped by a world where inherited custom is the only safe ground — improvisation is for those with the social capital to survive getting it wrong. Mr. Stelling's school is not educating Tom — it is demonstrating to him, daily, that he belongs to an inferior order. And Eliot sees this with brutal clarity — which makes it all the more extraordinary that she then cuts immediately to Maggie, who flourishes in exactly the same environment.

She took up the little, old, clumsy book with some curiosity; it had the corners turned down in many places, and some hand, now forever quiet, had made at certain passages strong pen-and-ink marks, long since browned by time. Maggie turned from leaf to leaf, and read where the quiet hand pointed: “Know that the love of thyself doth hurt thee more than anything in the world.... If thou seekest this or that, and wouldst be here or there to enjoy thy own will and pleasure, thou shalt never be quiet nor free from care; for in everything somewhat will be wanting, and in every place there will be some that will cross thee.... Both above and below, which way soever thou dost turn thee, everywhere thou shalt find the Cross; and everywhere of necessity thou must have patience, if thou wilt have inward peace, and enjoy an everlasting crown.... If thou desirest to mount unto this height, thou must set out courageously, and lay the axe to the root, that thou mayest pluck up and destroy that hidden inordinate inclination to thyself, and unto all private and earthly good. On this sin, that a man inordinately loveth himself, almost all dependeth, whatsoever is thoroughly to be overcome; which evil being once overcome and subdued, there will presently ensue great peace and tranquillity.... It is but little thou sufferest in comparison of them that have suffered so much, were so strongly tempted, so grievously afflicted, so many ways tried and exercised. Thou oughtest therefore to call to mind the more heavy sufferings of others, that thou mayest the easier bear thy little adversities. And if they seem not little unto thee, beware lest thy impatience be the cause thereof.... Blessed are those ears that receive the whispers of the divine voice, and listen not to the whisperings of the world. Blessed are those ears which hearken not unto the voice which soundeth outwardly, but unto the Truth, which teacheth inwardly.”

And what Eliot does with that revelation is quite extraordinarily honest. She shows us Maggie in a state of genuine spiritual illumination — and simultaneously shows us that the illumination is incomplete. The narrator tells us plainly what Maggie has not yet perceived — that renunciation remains sorrow, though a sorrow borne willingly. But Maggie, for now, is in ecstasy — and Eliot renders that ecstasy with full tenderness. That is not irony at Maggie's expense — it is the deepest moral sympathy.

Maggie drew a long breath and pushed her heavy hair back, as if to see a sudden vision more clearly. Here, then, was a secret of life that would enable her to renounce all other secrets; here was a sublime height to be reached without the help of outward things; here was insight, and strength, and conquest, to be won by means entirely within her own soul, where a supreme Teacher was waiting to be heard. It flashed through her like the suddenly apprehended solution of a problem, that all the miseries of her young life had come from fixing her heart on her own pleasure, as if that were the central necessity of the universe; and for the first time she saw the possibility of shifting the position from which she looked at the gratification of her own desires,—of taking her stand out of herself, and looking at her own life as an insignificant part of a divinely guided whole. She read on and on in the old book, devouring eagerly the dialogues with the invisible Teacher, the pattern of sorrow, the source of all strength; returning to it after she had been called away, and reading till the sun went down behind the willows. With all the hurry of an imagination that could never rest in the present, she sat in the deepening twilight forming plans of self-humiliation and entire devotedness; and in the ardor of first discovery, renunciation seemed to her the entrance into that satisfaction which she had so long been craving in vain. She had not perceived—how could she until she had lived longer?—the inmost truth of the old monk’s out-pourings, that renunciation remains sorrow, though a sorrow borne willingly. Maggie was still panting for happiness, and was in ecstasy because she had found the key to it. She knew nothing of doctrines and systems, of mysticism or quietism; but this voice out of the far-off middle ages was the direct communication of a human soul’s belief and experience, and came to Maggie as an unquestioned message.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I find myself moved by that reading of Edmund's — and also troubled by it. Because what Maggie discovers, let's be clear, is a spiritual path that promises peace through self-erasure. Eliot describes it with such precision — the flash of insight, like the suddenly apprehended solution of a problem. "It flashed through her like the suddenly apprehended solution of a problem, that all the miseries of her young life had come from fixing her heart on her own pleasure, as if that were the central necessity of the universe." Now — Eliot is not endorsing this as a sufficient answer. The novel will go on to test renunciation — and find it wanting, in precisely the way the passionate little girl found tragedy wanting. The structure of the novel echoes itself — and that echo is devastating.

That structural echo is exactly right — and it's what makes Eliot a novelist rather than a moralist. The passionate child who cannot perform tragedy — and the passionate young woman who cannot perform sainthood. Maggie's tragedy is that she is always too much for the forms available to her. And Eliot honours that excess rather than correcting it.
