The hearts of all three had been more deeply dejected than ever during the last few days. James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me first about this scene is how precisely Eliot understands the legal and financial mechanics of ruin. Guest and Company do not buy the mill — that detail matters enormously. The mill goes to Wakem at auction — knocked down, as Eliot puts it — and Wakem then offers Tulliver employment as manager on his own former property. That arrangement was entirely realistic for the period — it was one of the standard ways a creditor could extract continued value from a going concern while the former owner provided the operational knowledge. The family debate about whether to accept is not sentimental hand-wringing — it is a genuine calculation about survival. And then there is this — Eliot's description of what Tulliver walks into, without knowing it. "the flood of sunshine importunate, as if it had an unfeeling pleasure in showing the empty places, and the marks where well-known objects once had been." Sunshine as a kind of cruelty — the light that reveals, indifferently, what has been stripped away.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James is right that the material circumstances are rendered with admirable precision. But I think what Eliot is really exploring here is something older and less reducible to economic category — the question of pride, and whether pride can survive its own humiliation. Mrs Glegg's verdict is the key. Eliot gives it to us with perfectly calibrated irony — she tells us that Tulliver, in Mrs Glegg's view, must be made to feel that he could never humble himself enough. The moral universe of the aunts is one in which suffering is deserved, and deserved suffering is a lesson. Eliot does not endorse this view — but she renders it with such fidelity that we recognise its real power over real people.

What I keep coming back to is the image of Mr Tulliver not knowing. He comes downstairs thinking sunshine means cheerfulness. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a passage a little later that puts this loss in a context that I think is philosophically as well as historically important. When Eliot turns to reflect on why Tulliver cannot simply accept the offer and move on, she goes to his roots — quite literally. She writes of his love for the old premises as part of his life, part of himself. "he felt that the shape and colour of every roof and weather-stain and broken hillock was good, because his growing senses had been fed on them." The phrase 'fed on them' is doing extraordinary work — the landscape has become nutritive, constitutional. This is not nostalgia in the sentimental sense — it is identity located in place, and dispossession is therefore not merely financial ruin but a kind of self-erasure.

But the strongest influence of all was the love of the old premises where he had run about when he was a boy, just as Tom had done after him. James has touched on something that Burke understood very well — that attachment to place is not irrational but is in fact among the most rational and human of all attachments. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And then Eliot does something formally very daring, which she prepares us for with that Rhine and Rhone passage. She draws an extended contrast — the romantic ruins of the Rhine, which thrill us with poetry, against the dead, hollow villages on the Rhone. And she uses it to say something quite startling about the Tullivers' world. "human life—very much of it—is a narrow, ugly, grovelling existence, which even calamity does not elevate, but rather tends to exhibit in all its bare vulgarity of conception." That word 'grovelling' — she means it. She is not condescending to the Tullivers — she is insisting we honour them without prettifying them. That is a much harder, much braver artistic choice than making their suffering picturesque.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That passage is one of the most honest things Eliot ever wrote about class — the refusal to romanticise ordinary poverty. The Rhone villages are depressing precisely because they belonged to no grand historical narrative. They were swept away, and the sweep left nothing to commemorate — no poetry, no grandeur, just the bare fact of having existed. And Eliot is telling us: that is what the Tullivers face. Not tragic grandeur, but the far more common fate of being forgotten.

Welcome to The Mill on the Floss Unpacked, a close reading of George Eliot's great novel of passion, family, and the impossible weight of being. In writing the history of unfashionable families, one is apt to fall into a tone of emphasis which is very far from being the tone of good society, where principles and beliefs are not only of an extremely moderate kind, but are always presupposed, no subjects being eligible but such as can be touched with a light and graceful irony. But then good society has its claret and its velvet carpets, its dinner-engagements six weeks deep, its opera and its faëry ball-rooms; rides off its ennui on thoroughbred horses; lounges at the club; has to keep clear of crinoline vortices; gets its science done by Faraday, and its religion by the superior clergy who are to be met in the best houses,—how should it have time or need for belief and emphasis? But good society, floated on gossamer wings of light irony, is of very expensive production; requiring nothing less than a wide and arduous national life condensed in unfragrant deafening factories, cramping itself in mines, sweating at furnaces, grinding, hammering, weaving under more or less oppression of carbonic acid, or else, spread over sheepwalks, and scattered in lonely houses and huts on the clayey or chalky corn-lands, where the rainy days look dreary. This wide national life is based entirely on emphasis,—the emphasis of want, which urges it into all the activities necessary for the maintenance of good society and light irony; it spends its heavy years often in a chill, uncarpeted fashion, amidst family discord unsoftened by long corridors. Under such circumstances, there are many among its myriads of souls who have absolutely needed an emphatic belief, life in this unpleasurable shape demanding some solution even to unspeculative minds,—just as you inquire into the stuffing of your couch when anything galls you there, whereas eider-down and perfect French springs excite no question. Some have an emphatic belief in alcohol, and seek their ekstasis or outside standing-ground in gin; but the rest require something that good society calls “enthusiasm,” something that will present motives in an entire absence of high prizes; something that will give patience and feed human love when the limbs ache with weariness, and human looks are hard upon us; something, clearly, that lies outside personal desires, that includes resignation for ourselves and active love for what is not ourselves. Now and then that sort of enthusiasm finds a far-echoing voice that comes from an experience springing out of the deepest need; and it was by being brought within the long lingering vibrations of such a voice that Maggie, with her girl’s face and unnoted sorrows, found an effort and a hope that helped her through years of loneliness, making out a faith for herself without the aid of established authorities and appointed guides; for they were not at hand, and her need was pressing. From what you know of her, you will not be surprised that she threw some exaggeration and wilfulness, some pride and impetuosity, even into her self-renunciation; her own life was still a drama for her, in which she demanded of herself that her part should be played with intensity. And so it came to pass that she often lost the spirit of humility by being excessive in the outward act; she often strove after too high a flight, and came down with her poor little half-fledged wings dabbled in the mud. In writing the history of unfashionable families, one is apt to fall into a tone of emphasis which is very far from being the tone of good society, where principles and beliefs are not only of an extremely moderate kind, but are always presupposed, no subjects being eligible but such as can be touched with a light and graceful irony. But then good society has its claret and its velvet carpets, its dinner-engagements six weeks deep, its opera and its faëry ball-rooms; rides off its ennui on thoroughbred horses; lounges at the club; has to keep clear of crinoline vortices; gets its science done by Faraday, and its religion by the superior clergy who are to be met in the best houses,—how should it have time or need for belief and emphasis? But good society, floated on gossamer wings of light irony, is of very expensive production; requiring nothing less than a wide and arduous national life condensed in unfragrant deafening factories, cramping itself in mines, sweating at furnaces, grinding, hammering, weaving under more or less oppression of carbonic acid, or else, spread over sheepwalks, and scattered in lonely houses and huts on the clayey or chalky corn-lands, where the rainy days look dreary. This wide national life is based entirely on emphasis,—the emphasis of want, which urges it into all the activities necessary for the maintenance of good society and light irony; it spends its heavy years often in a chill, uncarpeted fashion, amidst family discord unsoftened by long corridors. Under such circumstances, there are many among its myriads of souls who have absolutely needed an emphatic belief, life in this unpleasurable shape demanding some solution even to unspeculative minds,—just as you inquire into the stuffing of your couch when anything galls you there, whereas eider-down and perfect French springs excite no question. Some have an emphatic belief in alcohol, and seek their ekstasis or outside standing-ground in gin; but the rest require something that good society calls “enthusiasm,” something that will present motives in an entire absence of high prizes; something that will give patience and feed human love when the limbs ache with weariness, and human looks are hard upon us; something, clearly, that lies outside personal desires, that includes resignation for ourselves and active love for what is not ourselves.
