Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What Oliver has identified is the tonal signature of the entire novel — and it is worth naming it precisely. Bleak House moves between the comic and the tragic with a fluency that no other Victorian novelist — not Thackeray, not George Eliot — quite matches. The fog is both funny and terrible. The Lord High Chancellor — that magnificent, almost pantomime title — sits in splendid uselessness while lives dissolve around him. Dickens understood that the grotesque is often the most accurate mode for describing institutional failure. Burke would have called it — rather approvingly, one suspects — an appeal to the imagination over the understanding.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Though I'd note that the imagination and the understanding were, in this case, pointing at precisely the same target. Chancery reform was a live political debate in 1852 when Bleak House began publication — Dickens was not shadowboxing. The fog is also, quite literally, the fog of industrial London — coal smoke, river damp, the particular opacity of a city that had grown faster than any city in human history. When Dickens describes the fog settling on the shoulders of the Lord Chancellor like a wig — and I think of it as the fog wearing him, rather than him wearing the fog — he is describing a man who has become indistinguishable from the system he administers. That is not a literary conceit — that is a sociological observation of the first order.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
That contrast is the structural genius of the novel — and I don't use the word genius lightly. The anonymous narrator sees everything and is moved by nothing — or rather, is moved only by outrage expressed as irony. Esther sees less but feels everything — and her claim not to be clever is, of course, one of the more sophisticated narrative gestures in Victorian fiction. Because she is deeply intelligent — observant, morally precise, unfailingly good without being sentimental. Dickens gives her the fog too — her fog is the fog of her own unknown parentage, her uncertain place in the world.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is absolutely right that the warning comes early — but I want to add the institutional dimension, because without it Richard can seem merely weak-willed. Jarndyce and Jarndyce is not a fictional exaggeration — or not much of one. The Court of Chancery in Dickens' time was genuinely capable of absorbing an estate for decades, leaving heirs in legal limbo that could last a lifetime. There were real cases — Jennens v Jennens being the most notorious — that ran for well over a century before resolution. Richard is not simply a young man who makes poor choices — he is trapped in a system designed, almost structurally, to generate false hope. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a very precise historical reason why that ironic voice sounds the way it does, and it's worth pausing on. Dickens was writing Bleak House between 1852 and 1853 — in the immediate aftermath of the Great Exhibition, when institutional confidence in Britain was at its peak. The Chancery reform debates had been grinding through Parliament for years without resolution — the Court of Chancery Amendment Act of 1852 was actually passing as he wrote. So the omniscient narrator's fury is the fury of a man watching reform happen too slowly, too partially, with too many compromises — the fury of the just impatient.
