Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close reading of Charles Dickens's last completed novel, and perhaps his most ambitious. Our Mutual Friend is a novel obsessed with money — where it comes from, what it does to people, and what people will do to get it. At its centre is a fortune built on dust heaps — the Victorian rubbish mounds on the edge of London that made one man extraordinarily rich. And when that man dies, the scramble for his wealth sets the whole novel in motion. Today we begin at the beginning — with the shadow the dust heaps cast over everyone who comes near them. I'm joined by three readers who know this novel with genuine intimacy, and I want to introduce them properly. Dr Eleanor Hartley is a novelist and creative writing teacher whose great subject is the craft of fiction — how a sentence is built, how an image works, how a chapter earns its effects. She is exactly the reader Dickens deserves — someone who notices everything. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don who reads novels as moral stories — tests of character, not social arguments. He is a bracing corrective to any reading that reduces a great novel to a pamphlet. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, every time I return to this material, is the sheer structural audacity of what Dickens is doing here. He doesn't show us Boffin being overwhelmed by his new wealth through scenes or dialogue — he shows us the secretary's postbag. We see the fortune's gravitational pull entirely through what it attracts — and it's a magnificent indirect method. The passage begins with that sardonic apostrophe — the narrator addressing us directly. "Oh the varieties of dust for ocular use, offered in exchange for the gold dust of the Golden Dustman!" That phrase — gold dust of the Golden Dustman — is doing something almost vertiginous. It makes the whole economy of the novel visible in a single compressed image: actual dust transformed into metaphorical gold, and now attracting every variety of counterfeit charity. And then the list! Fifty-seven churches, forty-two parsonage houses, twenty-seven organs — the accumulation is breathless and brilliant.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The list is indeed formidable — but I think one must resist the temptation to read it primarily as social satire. What Dickens is anatomising here is something older and more permanent than any particular Victorian institution. It is the corruption that wealth visits upon human character — on both the givers who are importuned and the beggars who importune them. Consider the passage about individual beggars — and that devastating parenthesis about spelling. "Several daughters of general officers, long accustomed to every luxury of life (except spelling)." The cruelty of that parenthesis is exquisite — and entirely moral in its target. It is not the class these women belong to that Dickens condemns — it is the hypocrisy of their appeal, the pretension dressed as distress.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I keep coming back to, reading these pages, is the simile Dickens uses for the beggars' documents. It's so odd and so precise that it stops you cold. "They call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf." Minced veal to a calf — it's grotesque, it's funny, and it's somehow also rather sad. There's a violence implied in the reduction — a life ground down into fragments that are then presented as proof of that life. And the rhythm of the whole passage — category after category of beggar, each with their own absurd logic — becomes almost musical after a while. The beggars on horseback, the inspired beggars, the beggars of punctual business habits — you start to hear a kind of dark comedy in the repetition itself.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that musical quality Caroline describes is absolutely deliberate — it's what I'd call catalogic satire. Dickens learned it partly from Rabelais, partly from the street literature he devoured as a child — the relentless list that becomes its own form of argument. But then he pivots to something genuinely chilling — the ducal letter from the Duke of Linseed. "Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet." Five words of noun phrase before any verb — the sentence itself enacts the pompous self-importance of the thing it describes. And then the postscript — the Steward's fee is three guineas — delivered with such murderous understatement.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
There is a passage I find particularly revealing in all of this — and it comes not from the begging letters but from Wegg's account of the will. Wegg reads aloud what old Harmon has written — and the sheer misanthropy of it is striking. "Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown." Quite enough for him — the contempt in that phrase is remarkable. This is a man who could not be loved, and who repays that failure by ensuring his money does as little good as possible. The dust heap was his monument to himself — and the will is the monument's inscription.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about the Harmon confession is how Dickens structures it as a kind of trial — John Harmon literally cross-examining himself. He doesn't just narrate; he interrogates. The passage ends with that extraordinary rhetorical move — 'Now, stop, and so far think it out, John Harmon. Is that so? That is exactly so.' It's a man holding himself to account in real time, on the page. But what I love is how Dickens loads the reasons for his return with that devastating triple repetition. 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife.' Each repetition narrows inward — from money, to memory, to the intimate dread of a poisoned marriage. It's a sentence that mimics the accumulation of dread itself.
