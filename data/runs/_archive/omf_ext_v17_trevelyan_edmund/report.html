Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The river in this novel is not scenery. It is a character — possibly the most powerful one Dickens ever invented — and it does things to a story that no human character can do. It swallows identities whole. Consider the extraordinary passage where John Harmon discovers, reading a placard at Whitehall, that he is officially dead. He rushes to see his own corpse — which is not his corpse at all — and what he realises, standing there, is this: "I perceived that Radfoot had been murdered by some unknown hands for the money for which he would have murdered me, and that probably we had both been shot into the river from the same dark place into the same dark tide." The same dark place, the same dark tide — that doubling is the whole novel in miniature. Two men, one death, one river — and money at the centre of all of it.

What strikes me about that passage is the grammatical symmetry — Dickens writes himself into a kind of terrible balance. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think we must be careful not to let the atmosphere swallow the human being inside it. What Dickens has achieved in that passage is something older than gothic atmosphere — it is the confrontation of a man with the fact of his own erasure. John Harmon stands at Whitehall reading his own death notice — and the question that animates the entire novel follows from that moment: what does a man do when he has been given the freedom of not existing? That is a question of character, not of capitalism.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And yet — against all that darkness and self-interest — there's a passage in the novel that feels almost like a creed. Dickens steps back from the story to make a direct declaration about the nature of good and evil — and it is one of the most confident sentences he ever wrote. He's been describing the Boffins — ignorant, unpolished, but guided by genuine duty and the desire to do right. And then, in the narrator's voice, without apology or ornament, comes this: "For, Evil often stops short at itself and dies with the doer of it; but Good, never." That comma after 'Good' — the pause before 'never' — is not accidental. It's Dickens conducting the sentence, making sure you feel the weight of that final word before it arrives.

That is Dickens at his most Johnsonian — the aphorism as moral assertion, delivered without hedge or qualification. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately about Harmon's confession is how Dickens structures it as an act of self-interrogation — almost judicial in its form. He doesn't simply explain himself. He puts himself on trial. Listen to the accumulation of that word 'shrinking' — Dickens hammers it twice in quick succession. 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife.' That repetition of 'shrinking' is almost physical — you feel Harmon recoiling from the very inheritance that defines him. And the chain of 'mistrustful' clauses that follows — mistrustful of the marriage, mistrustful of his father, mistrustful even of himself — it's a portrait of a man made of anxiety. What Dickens is doing, architecturally, is making Harmon's psychological state inseparable from his relationship to money. You cannot read his character without reading the economics of his upbringing.
