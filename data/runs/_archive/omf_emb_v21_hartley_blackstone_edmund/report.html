Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is quite right about the architecture of that sentence — but I want to resist the temptation to make the fog merely symbolic. Dickens was a man who walked London obsessively, who knew its smells and its textures with the intimacy of a native creature. When he writes of gaslights with a haggard and unblest air, as knowing themselves to be night-creatures, he is giving us something felt, not merely theorised. The atmosphere is the precondition for everything that follows — the fog of moral confusion through which individual characters must find their own way. And Dr. Johnson would have said, I think, that the power lies precisely in that — the particular made universal through the pressure of imagination.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I take Edmund's point — but the fog isn't innocent geography, either. Victorian London's fogs were partly natural and very substantially industrial — coal smoke, effluent, the literal byproduct of the economy Dickens is elsewhere so ferocious about. And this novel is ferocious about the economy of waste — nowhere more pointedly than in the dustheap passages. Dickens turns to address the governing classes directly — and I think it's one of the most controlled acts of rage in the English novel. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Serene is the right word, and I'd add something further: that statement is also a rebuke — delivered with perfect courtesy, but a rebuke nonetheless. There is a distinction, which Dickens understood very well, between suspense and mystification. Suspense is moral — it asks us to care about what will happen to a person. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The syntax is remarkable, but I want to press on the moral content of that multiplication, because I think we risk losing it in admiration of the technique. Dickens takes considerable care to show us that the deception did not begin from vanity or malice. The relevant passage in chapter thirty-one is one of the most searching psychological passages in the novel. That is not a social argument — that is a moral one, and it is the deeper architecture of the whole Harmon plot.

Eleanor is exactly right that the passage is funny — but I'd want to press on why it's so specifically painful, because the institutional context matters enormously here.
