Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The very first word is "London." Not "It was a dark and stormy night" — just the city's name, sitting there on its own like a stone dropped into water. And then Dickens starts fragmenting the prose deliberately — "Implacable November weather" — no subject, no verb, just the weather as a brute fact. When I read it aloud, I always feel as though the normal rules have been suspended before the novel has even properly started. And then he delivers this extraordinary sentence — listen to it: "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A dinosaur waddling up Holborn Hill. It's preposterous and it's perfect — because he's saying London in 1852 is prehistoric, it's primordial, the city has regressed to something before civilisation.

What strikes me every time is that word "waddling." He could have said "stalking" or "lumbering" — something suitably terrifying. James Blackstone (social_historian)
What I find fascinating from a historian's vantage is how precisely geographical this passage is. Dickens isn't describing some generalised gloom — he's placing us at Temple Bar, the old gateway between the City and Westminster, with Chancery just beyond it. And he pinpoints the density of the fog there with what I'd almost call forensic contempt: "The raw afternoon is rawest, and the dense fog is densest, and the muddy streets are muddiest near that leaden-headed old obstruction, appropriate ornament for the threshold of a leaden-headed old corporation, Temple Bar." "Leaden-headed old obstruction" — said twice, once for the gatehouse, once for the institution it guards. The rhetoric of the thing is almost legalistic — repetition used not for beauty but for indictment.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And from that precise location, he opens out to the full scale of what Chancery means. There's a passage in the opening chapter that I think is the single greatest bill of indictment in English prose fiction. You have to hear it at full stretch: "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that warning was not hyperbole, historically speaking. The Jarndyce case at the centre of this novel is modelled on real suits — Jennens v. Jennens, for instance, which ran for over a century. Dickens gives us the line about the fog never being too thick, never the mud too deep to match Chancery's condition — and then he names it: "Most pestilent of hoary sinners." Not merely inefficient — sinful. Dickens makes a moral charge, not an administrative complaint — and in 1852, that distinction carried enormous weight.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Thank you — and it's worth establishing, right from the start, that Jo is not a metaphor or a convenient symbol. Children like Jo were a documented, visible, abundant feature of mid-Victorian London — crossing-sweepers who cleared horse dung from street junctions in exchange for pennies. Henry Mayhew had interviewed dozens of them for London Labour and the London Poor, published just a year before Bleak House began its serial run. Dickens' readers would have seen these boys every day and, in most cases, looked straight through them. What Dickens does in Chapter Sixteen is something quite extraordinary — he asks us to actually inhabit that invisibility. Listen to how the passage opens: "It must be a strange state to be like Jo!" That exclamation mark is everything — it's not pity at a distance, it's a genuine shock of imaginative effort.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me, reading it closely, is the grammatical audacity of that passage. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That observation is historically crucial, and I'm glad Caroline caught it. The prevailing Victorian discourse about the poor — particularly in the wake of the 1834 Poor Law — was systematically committed to denying their inner life. The pauper was meant to be a moral category, not a person. And the legal system confirmed it — Jo is literally disqualified from being a witness in court because he cannot explain what an oath means. He cannot testify to what he has seen — and what he has seen is directly connected to the novel's central mystery. So the law has rendered him not merely poor but legally mute — epistemically erased.

"Move on" is a phrase with specific legal force — I want to be precise about that. What strikes me every time I return to that opening chapter is the precision of Dickens' anger — it's not rhetorical gesture, it's a genuinely accurate sociological account. He traces the corruption not just through the named solicitors and judges — but down through the copying clerks, the solicitors' boys who kept suitors waiting in hallways.
