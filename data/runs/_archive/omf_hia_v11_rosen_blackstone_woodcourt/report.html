Daniel is right about the commercial language, and it's worth pausing on what 'proving' meant in a legal and mercantile context. You prove a will. You prove metal. You prove a case. And yet — and I say this as someone who has argued with herself about Harmon across many re-readings — Dickens does something rather remarkable in chapter thirty-one. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Boffin's rant is historically precise as well as comic. By the eighteen-sixties, charitable institutions had proliferated to an extraordinary degree, and the subscription list was quite genuinely a form of social currency. You bought a title with your donation, and the title bought you social respectability — which could then be converted back into credit, patronage, connections. Dickens saw that the charitable machinery was, in large part, a circulation system for elite self-regard — not a mechanism for actually relieving suffering. And the Rokesmith in that passage — drowning in charity circulars, 'pretty well up to his neck' — that's not accidental placement. The man trying to manage new wealth, with a false identity, surrounded by a paper avalanche of false virtue — it's a perfect image of what the inheritance actually means.

What Daniel is pointing to has a very specific legal dimension as well. Old Harmon's cruelty is not merely personal — it is protected. The law of the period gave a father near-absolute authority over his children and household. Boffin can witness the violence. He can be outraged by it. He cannot stop it.
