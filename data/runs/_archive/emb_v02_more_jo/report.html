James Blackstone (social_historian)
What hits me first is the sheer geological confidence of the thing. Dickens doesn't begin with a character or a plot — he begins with an era, with deep time pressing down on a single November afternoon in London. And the instrument he uses to do that is preposterous and completely magnificent. He writes — and I cannot read this without a kind of helpless admiration — "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A dinosaur. Waddling up Holborn Hill. In 1852. The Megalosaurus had only been formally named in 1824 — it was the most fashionable prehistoric terror of the age — and Dickens plants it in the middle of London's legal district with complete deadpan seriousness. The implication is precise: this city, this legal system, these institutions, are survivals from a primordial age that should have died out long ago.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And notice he doesn't say you would meet a Megalosaurus — he says it would not be wonderful to meet one. That double negative is doing tremendous structural work. It's not absurdist — it's the narrator adopting a tone of absolute, world-weary plausibility. Given the state of this city, given the state of this November, a forty-foot prehistoric lizard is merely the logical next thing. What Dickens is establishing here is a narrative voice that we trust completely even as it tells us the most outrageous things. And that voice is going to carry us through nine hundred pages — so he has to earn it on the first half-page, and he does.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And then the fog migrates — quite deliberately — from the streets into the courtroom. Dickens makes the geography do the argument for him. He writes, with that magnificent escalating triple — "The raw afternoon is rawest, and the dense fog is densest, and the muddy streets are muddiest near that leaden-headed old obstruction, appropriate ornament for the threshold of a leaden-headed old corporation, Temple Bar." The superlatives accumulate around Temple Bar the way the fog itself does — the further in you go towards the law, the worse everything becomes. And Temple Bar was the literal gateway between the City of London and Westminster — between commerce and law. Dickens' contemporaries would have caught that geography immediately — the fog is thickest exactly where power sits.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That repetition — rawest, densest, muddiest — is one of the great rhetorical set-pieces in the opening. Dickens uses anaphora — the deliberate repetition of a grammatical structure — as a kind of legal procedural mime. The prose itself starts to repeat and circle, the way Chancery cases do. And it builds to that extraordinary indictment — the passage that I think is the moral centre of the entire first chapter. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." Every shire. Every madhouse. Every churchyard. The repetition of "which has" hammers it home like a charge sheet — Dickens is the prosecutor, and Chancery is on trial before we've even met a single client of it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that warning was, historically, not far from the truth. Chancery suits could run for decades — there are documented cases from the period that lasted longer than the lifetimes of the original suitors. The costs were ruinous by design — every procedural step generated fees for solicitors, barristers, masters, registrars. Dickens had watched this happen to people he knew — his own father had brushed against the edges of the legal system — so the anger in that passage is absolutely personal. What is remarkable is that he channels it into formal control rather than rant.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That passage is one of the architectural keystones of the entire novel — and I think it's worth being precise about why. Dickens doesn't simply assert that society is interconnected — he performs the interconnection by listing these wildly disparate things in a single sentence. Listen to the list itself — he sets it up like this: "What connexion can there be between the place in Lincolnshire, the house in town, the Mercury in powder, and the whereabout of Jo the outlaw with the broom, who had that distant ray of light upon him when he swept the churchyard-step?" The Mercury in powder — that's the liveried footman, emblematic of aristocratic display — placed beside Jo the outlaw with his broom. Dickens puts them in the same grammatical list and forces you to feel their proximity even before the plot makes it literal. That phrase — 'distant ray of light upon him' — is doing so much work: it's faint, it's accidental, it belongs to the churchyard, not to Jo.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right to focus on that word 'outlaw' — and I want to sit with it for a moment, because it's historically precise in a way that is easy to miss. Jo has no fixed abode, no verifiable identity, no standing before any court as a credible witness — he is, in a quite literal Victorian legal sense, outside the protection of the law. And Dickens knew this, because he had researched it. The passage in Chapter Sixteen that follows — the great meditation on Jo's illiteracy — is really a legal and social autopsy.
