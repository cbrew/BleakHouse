Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately about this novel — and I've read it aloud more than once — is how physical the dread is. Eliot doesn't describe fear in the abstract; she puts it in the body, in the landscape, in the space between two people standing a yard apart. There's a moment I want to go to immediately — Maggie and Tom on that path, and Philip suddenly there in front of them. Eliot writes: "Her imagination, always rushing extravagantly beyond an immediate impression, saw her tall, strong brother grasping the feeble Philip bodily, crushing him and trampling on him." That word 'extravagantly' — that is Eliot's whole understanding of Maggie in one adverb. Her imagination doesn't merely anticipate — it rushes ahead of her like a runaway horse, and she has to live inside the wreckage it creates.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
There is also something worth noting about Philip Wakem in this scene — specifically the words 'feeble Philip bodily.' In Eliot's world, physical deformity carried a social weight we can barely reconstruct now — it marked a person as outside the normal economy of respect and protection. Tom's potential violence against Philip isn't just fraternal jealousy — it is the assertion of a particular kind of masculine social order, one in which Philip is constitutionally vulnerable. Eliot sees that clearly — and she makes Maggie's imagination see it too, which is what makes the vision so horrifying.

Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
James is absolutely right — and it connects to something Eliot does throughout the novel with consciousness itself. She is obsessed with the life going on inside people that they cannot say aloud. There's a passage later with Stephen Guest — and I confess Stephen is a character I find both exasperating and absolutely recognizable — where he is sitting near Maggie, stroking her little dog. And Eliot gives us this: "he only wished he dared look at Maggie, and that she would look at him,—let him have one long look into those deep, strange eyes of hers, and then he would be satisfied and quite reasonable after that." 'Quite reasonable after that' — there it is. The self-delusion of desire, rendered in free indirect style with perfect comic and tragic precision, simultaneously.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The asymmetry Caroline describes is not merely psychological — it is structural. Stephen Guest has options that Maggie does not. His 'monomania,' as Eliot calls it, is a luxury — a young man of property can afford to be recklessly obsessed and the world will absorb the consequences. For Maggie, the same feeling carries entirely different stakes — social ruin, family rupture, the loss of everything she has held onto.

Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
And Eliot makes sure we understand those stakes in the most devastating way possible. There is a passage — Maggie waking on the boat after the elopement — that I think is one of the great moments of moral awakening in all of English fiction. She has been dreaming — Tom, Lucy, Philip all appear in the dream — and then she wakes to 'the plash of water against the vessel, and the sound of a footstep on the deck, and the awful starlit sky.' And then Eliot writes — and I ask you to hear this: "she had brought sorrow into the lives of others,—into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love." 'Knit up.' That phrase does so much — it's domestic, it's physical, it suggests something woven so tightly that to pull on one thread is to distort the whole fabric.

She had fallen asleep before nine, and had been sleeping for six hours before the faintest hint of a midsummer daybreak was discernible. She awoke from that vivid dreaming which makes the margin of our deeper rest. She was in a boat on the wide water with Stephen, and in the gathering darkness something like a star appeared, that grew and grew till they saw it was the Virgin seated in St Ogg’s boat, and it came nearer and nearer, till they saw the Virgin was Lucy and the boatman was Philip,—no, not Philip, but her brother, who rowed past without looking at her; and she rose to stretch out her arms and call to him, and their own boat turned over with the movement, and they began to sink, till with one spasm of dread she seemed to awake, and find she was a child again in the parlour at evening twilight, and Tom was not really angry. From the soothed sense of that false waking she passed to the real waking,—to the plash of water against the vessel, and the sound of a footstep on the deck, and the awful starlit sky. There was a moment of utter bewilderment before her mind could get disentangled from the confused web of dreams; but soon the whole terrible truth urged itself upon her. Stephen was not by her now; she was alone with her own memory and her own dread. The irrevocable wrong that must blot her life had been committed; she had brought sorrow into the lives of others,—into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love. The feeling of a few short weeks had hurried her into the sins her nature had most recoiled from,—breach of faith and cruel selfishness; she had rent the ties that had given meaning to duty, and had made herself an outlawed soul, with no guide but the wayward choice of her own passion. And where would that lead her? Where had it led her now? She had said she would rather die than fall into that temptation. She felt it now,—now that the consequences of such a fall had come before the outward act was completed. There was at least this fruit from all her years of striving after the highest and best,—that her soul though betrayed, beguiled, ensnared, could never deliberately consent to a choice of the lower. And a choice of what? O God! not a choice of joy, but of conscious cruelty and hardness; for could she ever cease to see before her Lucy and Philip, with their murdered trust and hopes? Her life with Stephen could have no sacredness; she must forever sink and wander vaguely, driven by uncertain impulse; for she had let go the clue of life,—that clue which once in the far-off years her young need had clutched so strongly. She had renounced all delights then, before she knew them, before they had come within her reach. Philip had been right when he told her that she knew nothing of renunciation; she had thought it was quiet ecstasy; she saw it face to face now,—that sad, patient, loving strength which holds the clue of life,—and saw that the thorns were forever pressing on its brow. The yesterday, which could never be revoked,—if she could have changed it now for any length of inward silent endurance, she would have bowed beneath that cross with a sense of rest.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Greek is doing serious critical work, though — it's not purely comic. Eliot is invoking the Aristotelian requirement that tragedy demands not only passion but stature — magnitude of person and consequence. Maggie has all the passion. What she lacks, as a girl of eight in rural Lincolnshire, is any possible scope for action. The world will not grant her the magnitude. And that is not a comic observation. That is the engine of the entire novel.

Maggie’s intentions, as usual, were on a larger scale than Tom imagined. The resolution that gathered in her mind, after Tom and Lucy had walked away, was not so simple as that of going home. What Eliot is quietly anatomising there is something with genuine legal and social weight — the position of a man who defines his honour as dominance over women. Mr Tulliver's decision to litigate is not fundamentally about water rights or Pivart's irrigation scheme. It is about masculine pride operating through the machinery of the law. And that passage speaks directly to what Maggie is actually going through — which is not a failure of moral rules but a collision between different moral claims, all of them real. The novel gives us a passage of extraordinary honesty about Maggie's inner struggle — her cruelty toward herself and the fleeting temptation to let others bear the cost for once. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and it is, I think, one of the most compressed philosophical statements in Victorian fiction. Eliot is writing about natural disaster — floods, uprooted trees, scarred hillsides. She grants that nature does repair herself — but incompletely, always incompletely. Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
Gladly — and I'm going back to that extraordinary gypsy scene, because it encapsulates Maggie completely. She has run away to the gypsies, intending to be their queen and educate them. And then — within five minutes — her imagination turns the whole adventure inside out. Listen to how Eliot puts it: "she had begun to think that they meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking." "Gradual cooking" — the precision of that is almost unbearable. It's terrifying and ridiculous at the same moment, which is Maggie's whole inner life in miniature. Eliot loves her too much to make her simply tragic — and too honestly to make her simply comic.

Her ideas about the gypsies had undergone a rapid modification in the last five minutes. From having considered them very respectful companions, amenable to instruction, she had begun to think that they meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking; the suspicion crossed her that the fierce-eyed old man was in fact the Devil, who might drop that transparent disguise at any moment, and turn either into the grinning blacksmith, or else a fiery-eyed monster with dragon’s wings. It was no use trying to eat the stew, and yet the thing she most dreaded was to offend the gypsies, by betraying her extremely unfavourable opinion of them; and she wondered, with a keenness of interest that no theologian could have exceeded, whether, if the Devil were really present, he would know her thoughts.
