James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Caroline is pointing at — that juxtaposition of luxury and squalor — is not simply comic technique. It is a precise social observation about how a certain class of person in Victorian England could maintain the apparatus of gentility while being entirely dependent on the labour and credit of others. Skimpole is Dickens's most damning portrait of aesthetic irresponsibility dressed as innocence. But what I keep returning to in this novel's atmosphere is the death imagery — and how Dickens deploys setting as a kind of moral verdict. The passage describing the discovery of Tulkinghorn is one of the most extraordinary things in the novel. "For Mr. Tulkinghorn's time is over for evermore, and the Roman pointed at the murderous hand uplifted against his life, and pointed helplessly at him, from night to morning, lying face downward on the floor, shot through the heart." The Roman — the painted figure on the ceiling — has been pointing since the opening of that chapter. Dickens has constructed an entire architectural accusation.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Wiglomeration is one of the great invented words in English fiction — and it earns its place because it describes something absolutely real. The Court of Chancery in Dickens's time was not merely slow — it was structurally designed to generate cost. Every party, every stage, every document required a fee payable to someone — counsel, clerks, Masters, the court itself. Jarndyce's definition of the thing is not satire at the expense of accuracy — it is satire in the service of accuracy. He catalogs every participant — Kenge and Carboy, the Master, counsel, the Chancellor — and his setup is mordant and precise. "Kenge and Carboy will have something to say about it; Master Somebody — a sort of ridiculous sexton, digging graves for the merits of causes in a back room at the end of Quality Court, Chancery Lane — will have something to say about it." A sexton digging graves for the merits of causes — that is one of the most precise images in the novel. The causes don't die of natural causes — they are buried, alive, by the process itself.

And if you want to understand how the system sustained itself against any challenge, there is a passage in chapter thirty-nine that I find almost unbearably perfect. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That detail about the roads being dry elsewhere — it's historically precise, incidentally. Dickens was writing during and after the great debates about urban sanitation — the Sanitary Movement, Chadwick's reports, the cholera outbreaks. These streets existed precisely because no one with power to act had any incentive to act. The same logic that keeps Wiglomeration alive keeps Tom-all-Alone's undrained. And the novel insists on the connection — the disease bred in Tom-all-Alone's eventually crosses every social boundary. Dickens understood — and said explicitly — that you cannot wall off the consequences of neglect.
