Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Gaskell shows us, with absolute precision, is Margaret performing the work that industrial capitalism requires of everyone who enters its orbit. She looks at Thornton and she revises her judgment upward — not because he has become kinder, but because she reads power in his face. And Gaskell gives us Margaret's verdict in language that is quite startling in its candour. "With such an expression of resolution and power, no face, however plain in feature, could be either vulgar or common." And then, almost in the same breath: "I should not like to have to bargain with him; he looks very inflexible." That word — bargain — is doing enormous work. Margaret is not simply admiring Thornton; she is already sizing him up as an adversary in a commercial transaction. She calls him sagacious and strong — and then, crucially, "as becomes a great tradesman." That qualifier — tradesman — is at once a concession to his class and a reminder of its limits.

And yet there's a counter-movement in the novel — a movement toward grief and dissolution — that complicates any reading of Milton as simply a site of energetic power. The novel is full of loss. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And then, as if to answer all that grief, Gaskell gives us one of the most quietly devastating meditations in the novel — Margaret at Helstone, watching the landscape change. It's a passage about mutability — about how nothing holds its shape, not places, not people, not institutions. Gaskell moves from the landscape to the human lifespan in a single, fearless sentence. "The natural mutations brought by days and months and years, which carry us on imperceptibly from childhood to youth, and thence through manhood to age —" "— whence we drop like fruit, fully ripe, into the quiet mother earth." "Drop like fruit" — that is not the language of consolation. It is the language of naturalism, of a writer who will not sentimentalise death into something easier than it is.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold on to that phrase — 'proud air of an unwilling slave' — because I think it's doing something politically serious, even if comic in register. Margaret's social performance at the tea-table is coded as a form of compelled service — she has a headache, she'd rather not be there. And the man observing her with such proprietorial fascination is a manufacturer — someone who organises compelled labour for a living. Gaskell may intend comedy, but the class architecture underneath is quietly unsettling. And this connects directly to what happens in the argument later in that same chapter — when Margaret actually confronts Thornton. She doesn't accept his framing of the North as progress and the South as backward. She names what she actually sees in the streets of Milton. "I see men here going about in the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care — who are not only sufferers but haters." 'Not only sufferers but haters' — that is a remarkable phrase for a Victorian novel about industrial relations. It acknowledges a legitimate grievance — and a political consciousness among workers — that the mill-owners in the novel would prefer to ignore.

Margaret yielded, and lost her train. In the suddenness of the event, Mrs. Shaw became bewildered and hysterical, and so the precious time slipped by. But there was another train in a couple of hours; and after various discussions on propriety and impropriety, it was decided that Captain Lennox should accompany Margaret, as the one thing to which she was constant was her resolution to go, alone or otherwise, by the next train, whatever might be said of the propriety or impropriety of the step. Her father’s friend, her own friend, was lying at the point of death; and the thought of this came upon her with such vividness, that she was surprised herself at the firmness with which she asserted something of her right to independence of action; and five minutes before the time of starting, she found herself sitting in a railway carriage opposite to Captain Lennox. It was always a comfort to her to think that she had gone, though it was only to hear that he had died in the night. She saw the rooms that he had occupied, and associated them ever after most fondly in her memory with the idea of her father, and his one cherished and faithful friend.
