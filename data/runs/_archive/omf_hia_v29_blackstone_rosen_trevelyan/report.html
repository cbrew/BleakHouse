Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close, sustained look at what many consider Charles Dickens's greatest and darkest novel. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me — returning to it for this series — is how precise the novel is about the mechanisms of wealth. Not just that money corrupts — that's the comfortable reading — but exactly how it corrupts, and whom it protects. The dust heaps are not metaphor. They are London's waste economy made literal — the fortune of the Harmon family rests on what the city throws away.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the moment that fortune becomes visible — the moment Boffin inherits — the entire parasitic apparatus of Victorian society attaches itself to him. Dickens captures this with a simile that I find absolutely devastating in its precision. The mansion, he writes, was like an eminently aristocratic family cheese — it was much too large for his wants, and bred an infinite amount of parasites. An aristocratic cheese. Dickens puts the English ruling class inside a dairy metaphor and leaves it there to ripen. And the parasites — they are not incidental. They are the system functioning exactly as designed.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Twemlow is one of the most precise satirical inventions in the whole novel, and Dickens gives him to us immediately — almost before the story has properly begun. He is not described as a person who resembles furniture — he is furniture. Listen to how Dickens introduces him: 'There was an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James's, when not in use.' Not a man who has been reduced to an object — a man who was never granted the status of subject in the first place. He is stored when not required, retrieved when useful, and extended — literally pulled out — to accommodate more important people. And Dickens makes the structural irony explicit: the more guests arrive, the further Twemlow is pushed from the centre, nearer to the sideboard. That is not comic coincidence — that is the mechanism of social class described with absolute clarity.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Twemlow is also, I'd note, an entirely accurate social portrait. Victorian society ran on exactly this logic — the impoverished relation of a peer was a genuinely useful commodity at a dinner table, in a drawing room, in a marriage negotiation. What Dickens does is make the metaphor literal and then refuse to apologise for it. The Veneerings are new money — parvenus — and they need legitimacy that only aristocratic adjacency can supply. Twemlow, who has breeding and not a penny, is their instrument for purchasing exactly that. The exchange is perfectly mutual and perfectly degrading for both parties — though only one of them seems to feel it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
This passage is extraordinary — and I think underread, because readers tend to rush through it as broad satire. But every category of beggar Dickens lists here corresponds to something real. The daughters of officers 'long accustomed to every luxury of life' — Dickens adds the parenthetical '(except spelling)' — were a genuine Victorian type. The collapse of military families after the Napoleonic wars produced exactly this class of educated, impoverished women with no economic recourse whatsoever. Dickens makes them absurd — but their absurdity is a product of real structural destitution.
