Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Thank you — yes, and I'd say that comedy Oliver mentions is doing serious analytical work from the very first pages. Gaskell introduces us to the southern world — the world of Mrs. Shaw, of Edith, of comfortable, unexamined privilege — and she introduces it through satire. There's a line in the opening chapter that I find almost surgically precise about marriage as an economic transaction dressed up as sentiment. Gaskell writes that Mrs. Shaw said that her only child should marry for love, "and sighed emphatically, as if love had not been her motive for marrying the General." One sentence — and the entire moral architecture of that world is exposed. The sigh is the tell. The whole performance of romantic feeling is a cover for a calculating arrangement.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I read that passage is the phrase 'sighed emphatically.' It's such a perfectly judged adverb. The emphasis is the self-betrayal. She's performing the sigh so vigorously that Gaskell doesn't even need to spell out the irony — it does the work itself. And then there's Margaret, in the corner of all this, listening. She's the one who glows at the idea of adventure in Corfu — the things that make Edith pretend to shudder. From the very first scene, Gaskell is quietly telling us: Margaret is different. She will respond differently to life.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Bessy Higgins is, for me, one of the most important figures in the novel — and one of the most under-discussed. She is dying from fluff in her lungs — the cotton dust of the mill — and Gaskell refuses to sentimentalise it. Instead, she gives Bessy a voice of almost unbearable clarity. Bessy describes what her night fevers feel like — the mill noises, the fluff, the longing for clean air — and it becomes a kind of indictment. "if all I've been born for is just to work my heart and my life away, and to sicken i' this dree place, wi' them mill-noises in my ears for ever, until I could scream out for them to stop, and let me have a little piece o' quiet—" "and wi' the fluff filling my lungs, until I thirst to death for one long deep breath o' the clear air yo' speak on—" That phrase — 'thirst to death for one long deep breath' — is not the voice of Victorian pathos. It is the voice of industrial murder.

The dialect is crucial there, isn't it. Gaskell writes Bessy's speech in her own voice — she doesn't translate it into standard English. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It is one of the novel's most devastating pieces of retrospective narration — Mrs. Hale telling Margaret what it felt like to wait for news of Frederick after the mutiny. The detail of the newspapers, of scanning the list of rescued officers and finding no Frederick Hale — that is the texture of real, particular dread. And then Mr. Hale returns from Southampton, and Mrs. Hale describes him in a way that is almost unbearable to hear. "He came at last, his arms hanging loose down, his head sunk, and walking heavily along, as if every step was a labour and a trouble." That physical description — the hanging arms, the heavy walk — is grief rendered in the body before a single word is spoken.

And Mrs. Hale's account doesn't stop there — it goes further, and gets more intimate. She ran to meet him, and he didn't speak or seem surprised — he just put her arm in his and stroked her hand. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
'Strange muffled, groaning voice.' Three adjectives — and each one doing different work. Strange — because men of his class and generation were not supposed to cry. Muffled — because he's suppressing it even as it overwhelms him. And groaning — because it goes deeper than tears. It is the sound of something breaking. Gaskell hears language with extraordinary precision. She always chooses the right word — and the right word is never the expected one.
