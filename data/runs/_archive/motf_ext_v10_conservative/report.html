Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
The passage I keep returning to is from those early chapters — Tom and Maggie as small children, trotting along the river together. It's so simple on the surface, and then you read it again and realise it's doing something quietly devastating. Eliot gives us the children's inner world with perfect fidelity — their certainty that nothing will ever change. The line reads, in full — "They trotted along and sat down together, with no thought that life would ever change much for them; they would only get bigger and not go to school, and it would always be like the holidays; they would always live together and be fond of each other." That word 'always' — it appears twice in one sentence — and Eliot is practically ringing a bell. We know, because we are reading a novel, that 'always' is precisely what will be taken from them. And there's something else — the list of things that will always be the same: the mill, the chestnut tree, the river. These aren't decorative details. They are the world as the children know it to be permanent.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is the narrative sleight of hand Eliot is performing. She's inside the children's consciousness — free indirect discourse, inhabiting their certainty completely. And yet the reader is simultaneously outside it, holding the irony. You are asked to feel the children's happiness as real and total — and at the same instant to feel its fragility. The technical achievement is that Eliot never breaks the spell to tell you it will end. The future presses against the prose from outside. And that image of the Floss itself — described as something they 'wandered' along 'with a sense of travel' — it's already a form of departure. The river leads outward. Even here, in the happiest passage in the novel, the river is going somewhere they cannot follow.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think we should be precise about what kind of irony this is — because it is not satirical, and it is not cold. Eliot is not superior to these children. She mourns with them, from the first page. What she is doing — and this is the moral heart of the novel for me — is insisting that childhood happiness was real. That it was not illusion. That its loss is genuine tragedy, not mere sentiment. The passage about the Floss is preparing us for what the place will mean to Maggie — and to us — when circumstances have stripped everything away. Memory of belonging is, in this novel, both the deepest comfort and the sharpest wound.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The verbal echo is unmistakable once you hear it — and that is exactly the kind of structural stitching that distinguishes Eliot from her contemporaries. That passage also has something that I think is underrated — the comedy of Tom's theological dilemma over Euclid. He's trying to work out how to frame a petition for geometry — 'whether he should ask to see what it meant.' And then he settles on asking that Mr Stelling say he 'sha'n't do Euclid any more.' The logic is impeccable and completely childlike — he can't master the subject, so eliminate it entirely. Eliot is funny in a way people often forget, because the comedy is so thoroughly embedded in the pathos.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The comedy, yes — though I would say it earns its place precisely because it is not at Tom's expense. Eliot is the most scrupulously fair of novelists in that respect. She never condescends. And that fairness becomes all the more important when we come to the later passage — the confrontation in the Red Deeps. There Tom has become, to many readers, the villain of the piece — his severity toward Maggie and Philip apparently cruel. But Eliot does something remarkable. She shows us simultaneously the effect of Tom's behaviour and its hidden cause. "Tom was not given to inquire subtly into his own motives any more than into other matters of an intangible kind; he was quite sure that his own motives as well as actions were good, else he would have had nothing to do with them." This is not a sentence of condemnation. It is a sentence of tragic understanding. Tom cannot see himself. And because he cannot, his goodness and his cruelty become the same act.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The novel's great sociological intelligence is never more apparent than in those early chapters concerning Mr Tulliver and the parish of Basset. George Eliot gives us a portrait of self-deception so quietly precise that it almost escapes notice. Consider how she frames Tulliver's false confidence in his own substance. "He had been always used to hear pleasant jokes about his advantages as a man who worked his own mill, and owned a pretty bit of land; and these jokes naturally kept up his sense that he was a man of considerable substance." The word 'jokes' is doing extraordinary work there. It is not vanity that undoes Tulliver — it is the flattering noise of social consensus, which he has simply absorbed as truth. This is the condition that Maggie is born into — a household whose very foundations are built on pleasant illusion.

For Mr Tulliver was in a position neither new nor striking, but, like other everyday things, sure to have a cumulative effect that will be felt in the long run: he was held to be a much more substantial man than he really was. And as we are all apt to believe what the world believes about us, it was his habit to think of failure and ruin with the same sort of remote pity with which a spare, long-necked man hears that his plethoric short-necked neighbour is stricken with apoplexy. He had been always used to hear pleasant jokes about his advantages as a man who worked his own mill, and owned a pretty bit of land; and these jokes naturally kept up his sense that he was a man of considerable substance. They gave a pleasant flavour to his glass on a market-day, and if it had not been for the recurrence of half-yearly payments, Mr Tulliver would really have forgotten that there was a mortgage of two thousand pounds on his very desirable freehold. That was not altogether his own fault, since one of the thousand pounds was his sister’s fortune, which he had to pay on her marriage; and a man who has neighbours that will go to law with him is not likely to pay off his mortgages, especially if he enjoys the good opinion of acquaintances who want to borrow a hundred pounds on security too lofty to be represented by parchment. Our friend Mr Tulliver had a good-natured fibre in him, and did not like to give harsh refusals even to his sister, who had not only come in to the world in that superfluous way characteristic of sisters, creating a necessity for mortgages, but had quite thrown herself away in marriage, and had crowned her mistakes by having an eighth baby. On this point Mr Tulliver was conscious of being a little weak; but he apologised to himself by saying that poor Gritty had been a good-looking wench before she married Moss; he would sometimes say this even with a slight tremulousness in his voice. But this morning he was in a mood more becoming a man of business, and in the course of his ride along the Basset lanes, with their deep ruts,—lying so far away from a market-town that the labour of drawing produce and manure was enough to take away the best part of the profits on such poor land as that parish was made of,—he got up a due amount of irritation against Moss as a man without capital, who, if murrain and blight were abroad, was sure to have his share of them, and who, the more you tried to help him out of the mud, would sink the further in. It would do him good rather than harm, now, if he were obliged to raise this three hundred pounds; it would make him look about him better, and not act so foolishly about his wool this year as he did the last; in fact, Mr Tulliver had been too easy with his brother-in-law, and because he had let the interest run on for two years, Moss was likely enough to think that he should never be troubled about the principal. The Dodsons are one of the great comic creations in English fiction — and I use the word 'great' with care.
