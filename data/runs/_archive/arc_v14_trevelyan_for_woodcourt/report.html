Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That movement from the cosmic to the particular is the whole architecture of the opening in miniature. Dickens starts from the sky, essentially — the fog as a feature of the landscape — and then tightens his lens until he's inside a cabin, inside a pipe, inside a child's frozen fingers. That is not just atmospheric scene-setting — that is a political argument delivered through prose rhythm. The fog doesn't discriminate by class or age — but it hurts the small and the poor most cruelly. The word Dickens chooses for that child is devastating — he's "cruelly" pinched. And that cruelty is not weather — it is a system.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me, reading that opening now, is how precisely Dickens has mapped the actual geography of Victorian London's fog problem. The Essex marshes, the Kentish heights, the collier-brigs — this is not invented atmosphere, this is the Thames estuary in winter. The coal barges are there because London ran on coal, and the coal smoke combined with the river damp to produce the particular horror of a Victorian pea-souper. So when Dickens uses fog as a metaphor for Chancery — for legal obscurity, for the suffocating weight of unresolved suits — he is also describing something people were literally dying from. Those Greenwich pensioners wheezing by the fireside are not picturesque detail. They are evidence.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And you see that same quality — the fog as both literal and moral weather — working its way through the novel long after the first chapter. Much later in the book, there's a passage I find irresistible about a court — a literal court of houses, not a law court — consumed by gossip about what old Krook may have hidden. The fog of not-knowing has spread from the Thames to the neighbourhood. Dickens writes of it with such relish. "Never since it has been a court has it had such a Fortunatus' purse of gossip as in the proceedings at the rag and bottle shop." A Fortunatus' purse — inexhaustible, magical, never running dry. The whole neighbourhood is in a delirious fog of speculation — imagining guineas pouring from teapots, banknotes stuffed in mattresses. And of course they find nothing — which is the oldest Chancery joke in the book.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That passage is doing something more than comedy, though. The court's collective delusion — the invented fortunes, the gold in the teapots — is structurally identical to what Chancery does to its suitors. It makes people believe there is treasure at the centre of a fog — and keeps them searching until they've spent everything they have on the search. Which brings us to Richard Carstone — the novel's most heartbreaking case study in precisely that delusion. There is a passage in Chapter 23 where Richard tries to explain to Esther why he cannot settle — why Chancery has made him constitutionally incapable of it. "If you lived in an unfinished house, you couldn't settle down in it; if you were condemned to leave everything you undertook unfinished, you would find it hard to apply yourself to anything; and yet that's my unhappy case." That is one of the most quietly devastating self-diagnoses in Victorian fiction. He was born into the suit — born into the fog — before he could tell, as he puts it, a suit at law from a suit of clothes.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Richard's predicament was not unusual in the real world of Chancery. There were suits in Chancery at this period that had run for generations — some of them very like Jarndyce and Jarndyce, with beneficiaries dying before the case concluded. The psychological effect on those caught inside such a suit was precisely what Dickens describes — a kind of learned helplessness, an inability to make plans or commitments. When Richard says he was born into this unfinished contention and it unsettled him before he knew what it was — that is historically accurate, not merely metaphorical. Chancery was a system that consumed its own beneficiaries.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes, and it's worth pausing on that move — the way Dickens grounds his polemic in something utterly material. Jarndyce doesn't lecture Esther about legal theory; he walks her — metaphorically — to an actual street. And what he says about ownership is historically precise: the property belongs, nominally, to the suit — but in practice it belongs to costs. In mid-Victorian Chancery, legal costs could consume an estate entirely before any beneficiary ever saw a penny. That wasn't hyperbole — it was, for a significant number of cases, the literal outcome. But then Dickens does something extraordinary with the physical description of those houses. "It is a street of perishing blind houses, with their eyes stoned out, without a pane of glass, without so much as a window-frame, with the bare blank shutters tumbling from their hinges and falling asunder, the iron rails peeling away in flakes of rust, the chimneys sinking in, the stone steps to every door (and every door might be death's door) turning stagnant green." Every detail of that decay is a legal consequence — not neglect by an absent landlord, but the paralysis of a system that can neither resolve nor release.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a legal dimension to that interrogation that Dickens is absolutely precise about. To give sworn testimony in a Victorian court, you had to demonstrate an understanding of what an oath was — a religious understanding. Jo can't satisfy that test, so he cannot testify — the system throws him out before he's spoken a meaningful word. And yet Dickens makes clear he has a moral sensibility — a genuine one. Jo knows that a broom is a broom, and knows it's wicked to tell a lie — he just can't tell you who taught him. The law has no category for that kind of knowledge, so it pretends it doesn't exist.
