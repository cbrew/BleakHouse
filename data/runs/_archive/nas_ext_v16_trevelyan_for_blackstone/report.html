Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, every time I return to this novel, is how deliberately Gaskell refuses to make Milton picturesque. So many Victorian novelists, when they write about industry, are essentially writing about it from a carriage window — at a safe aesthetic distance. Gaskell puts you inside it — inside the smoke, inside the anxiety, inside the bodies of people who are actually breathing cotton-fluff and worrying about wages. And she does it through Margaret's consciousness, which is a very shrewd structural choice. Because Margaret arrives as an outsider — a southerner, a clergyman's daughter — she registers everything freshly, even hostilely, which gives Gaskell permission to be honest about how ugly and overwhelming Milton is. But what I find most daring is that Gaskell then turns the screws on Margaret's very certainties — her southern gentility, her quiet Anglican faith. By the time we reach the passage where Margaret stands at the window unable to find God in the night sky, we feel how much has already been stripped away from her. Gaskell writes that those never-ending depths of space, in their still serenity, were more mocking to her than any material bounds could be. That word 'mocking' — from the infinite silence, no less — is doing extraordinary work.

Eleanor's absolutely right about Margaret as the instrument through which we feel the world — and I'd add that Gaskell is remarkably unsparing about what that world does to a person's interior life. When I first read that passage aloud in the recording booth, I had to stop. There's a full sentence in there that just — falls. That repetition of 'lost — lost for ever' — Gaskell is writing for the voice there. She knows the second 'lost' has to fall into silence, and that the silence is the point. And then — and this is the craft — the father walks in, unheard, and touches her shoulder. After all that cosmic vastness, a human touch.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What Oliver just described — that structural movement from the infinite to the intimate — is exactly what makes Gaskell so hard to put down. She never lets the large themes — faith, class, the condition of England — float free of actual people in rooms, feeling things in their bodies. And what I find so moving in that passage is that Margaret doesn't resolve anything. She just — stands there, gazing, and feeling the weight of unanswered prayer. Gaskell is writing about doubt in a way that I think would have felt genuinely dangerous in 1855. Not theatrical doubt — not Byron — but quiet, domestic, middle-of-the-night doubt, with your father asleep in the next room.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's one of the great first-meeting scenes in Victorian fiction, and what makes it great is that Gaskell refuses to give either character the moral high ground. Thornton misreads Margaret completely — and yet his misreading is entirely understandable, because what he sees is formidable. Gaskell gives us this portrait of Margaret almost through Thornton's stunned, resentful gaze — and it is an extraordinary piece of writing. Her round white flexile throat rising out of the full, yet lithe figure; her lips moving so slightly as she spoke, not breaking the cold serene look of her face with any variation from the one lovely haughty curve. 'Flexile' — that's the word I keep returning to. It means supple, yielding — but everything else in the description is unyielding, cold, serene. Gaskell is encoding the contradiction of Margaret — the softness that is also, somehow, steel — in a single adjective.

And Thornton's response to all of this — to this vision of loveliness that refuses to notice him — is almost comic in its wounded male pride. That's such a sharp observation — because it shows us that Margaret's snobbery, if we want to call it that, isn't really about class at all. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is how much Gaskell does through focalization. We are entirely in Thornton's point of view — Margaret is being watched, catalogued, desired — and yet she has her own counter-narrative running simultaneously. Because Gaskell tells us Margaret has a headache, and she is resolved — and this is the word — "to throw herself into the breach, if there was any long untoward pause, rather than that her father's friend, pupil, and guest should have cause to think himself in any way neglected." She is performing duty under physical suffering, and no one in the room knows it. Gaskell is already building the architecture of Margaret's agency — she acts, she chooses, even when it costs her.
