James Blackstone (social_historian)
Forster begins, rather remarkably, not with a character or a plot but with the sky. The sky settles everything, he tells us — not merely the weather, but beauty itself, fertility itself. And then comes this extraordinary image of the landscape — flat, prostrate, almost submissive, for league after league. And then, just as you've settled into that flatness, he introduces the one interruption: "Only in the south, where a group of fists and fingers are thrust up through the soil, is the endless expanse interrupted. These fists and fingers are the Marabar Hills, containing the extraordinary caves." Now — fists and fingers thrust up through the soil. That is not the language of pastoral description — that is the language of violence, of rupture, of something breaking through. Forster plants his most ominous symbol on the very first page, and dresses it as geography.

And that hierarchy becomes explicit — almost brutally so — when Forster moves from landscape to society. There's a passage I find genuinely arresting — it comes just after the educated Indians have received their invitation to Mr. Turton's Bridge Party. The narrator steps back and describes who did not receive a card — and it keeps expanding outward, circle beyond circle. "And there were circles even beyond these — people who wore nothing but a loincloth, people who wore not even that, and spent their lives in knocking two sticks together before a scarlet doll — humanity grading and drifting beyond the educated vision, until no earthly invitation can embrace it." No earthly invitation can embrace it. That phrase carries the entire weight of colonial social policy — the assumption that the truly poor are simply beyond the frame of concern. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And then there's Ronny Heaslop — who gives us the explicit, unguarded version of what the educated Indians' invisibility actually means to the colonial administration. Ronny doesn't bother to dress his contempt in euphemism. He says, with what he clearly considers perfect pragmatism: "The educated Indians will be no good to us if there's a row, it's simply not worth while conciliating them, that's why they don't matter." Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
I want to hold that phrase — 'before man, with his itch for the seemly' — for a moment longer. Because 'the seemly' is specifically a social category — it's propriety, decorum, the enforcement of appearances. The entire British colonial enterprise in India is built on the performance of seemliness — the club, the civil station, the social distance between ruler and ruled. The caves strip all of that away.
