Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Thank you — yes, and I think the fog is the single most politically precise image in the Victorian novel. It isn't decoration. It is a description of how power works — pervasive, undifferentiated, impossible to locate in any one source. Chancery sits at the heart of it, but Dickens is careful to show us that Chancery is not the disease — it is one symptom. And nowhere does he expose the mechanism more ruthlessly than in the figure of Mr. Vholes — and in the defence mounted on Vholes's behalf. There is a passage — Mr. Kenge speaking — that I consider one of the most devastating pieces of satirical prose in the language. "Repeal this statute, my good sir? Repeal it, my dear sir? Never, with my consent." And then the threat, dressed as civic concern: "Sir, that class of practitioners would be swept from the face of the earth." That is the whole logic of institutional self-preservation in one sentence. Reform is not defeated by argument — it is defeated by invoking the respectability of Mr. Vholes. And the mock parliamentary inquiry that follows is — I don't say this lightly — the funniest and most chilling piece of legislative satire before Kafka.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I won't quarrel with Kafka — though I might have said Swift. But I would caution against reading the Vholes passage as purely a systemic indictment. What Dickens is doing there is also, and primarily, a portrait of a particular kind of man — the man who has made himself morally invisible behind the word 'respectable.' Which brings me to what I find far more illuminating as an entry point into the novel — the character of Harold Skimpole. Skimpole is the great comic villain of Bleak House — and his villainy consists entirely of the performance of innocence. There is a moment at Chesney Wold where he thanks Sir Leicester, and one must read it carefully to catch exactly what Dickens is doing. "The owners of such places as Chesney Wold are public benefactors. They are good enough to maintain a number of delightful objects for the admiration and pleasure of us poor men." Now: Skimpole is flattering an aristocrat, yes — but he is also, in his blithe way, constructing an entire ideology of dependence and calling it gratitude. Dickens understands that this kind of man is not a system — he is a choice. And that is the more searching accusation.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That is beautifully observed, and I think it's precisely the right point at which to locate Dickens's greatness. Richard Carstone is, to my mind, the novel's true tragic centre — not because of what the system does to him, but because of what he does to himself. Thank you. And yes — I want to start in Chapter Seventeen, because what Dickens does there is almost forensic. Esther is reflecting on Richard's qualities — his quickness, his spirits, his charm — and she's already worried. The worry isn't that he's wicked. The setup goes like this: "The system which had addressed him in exactly the same manner as it had addressed hundreds of other boys, all varying in character and capacity." That word — system — is doing enormous work. It is not a teacher, not a parent, not a guardian. It is a mechanism indifferent to the individual. And then Dickens gives us the great sentence. It is a very fine sentence. I won't pretend otherwise. Dickens could do that — the aphorism that opens suddenly into a landscape.
