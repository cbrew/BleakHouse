Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
What she does is almost cruel, in the best possible way. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)

Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
And Gaskell is absolutely ruthless about it — the education, I mean. She doesn't let Margaret learn through comfortable epiphanies. She learns through humiliation. The riot scene at the mill gates is the centrepiece of the whole argument — and it is extraordinary theatre, frankly. Margaret steps in front of Thornton to shield him from the crowd — this gesture of extraordinary, instinctive physical courage. And the reward she gets is that all of Milton assumes she is his mistress. Gaskell sets that up with the grimness of a moralist and the timing of a satirist. The novel is practically asking: what is the price of acting on your convictions when you're a woman in 1850? And the answer it gives is — your reputation. That's what it costs.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one mustn't rush past the theological dimension of Margaret's ordeal — and it is an ordeal, in the older sense of the word. Her father's loss of faith is not merely a plot mechanism to move the family north. It places Margaret in a position of profound moral loneliness from the very beginning. She cannot discuss it with her mother, who is too frail. She cannot discuss it with Edith, who is too shallow. She carries the secret of her father's apostasy — that is the right word — entirely alone. And then she lies to protect her brother Frederick, and that lie is discovered. Two acts of loyalty — to her father's secret, to her brother's safety — and both corrode her standing and her self-respect. What is being tested here is not merely social resilience but moral character under the kind of sustained, invisible pressure that Johnson would have recognised immediately.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Edmund's point about the lie is so important, because it's the scene that most readers feel most acutely. Thornton has witnessed Margaret at the train station with Frederick — he doesn't know it's her brother.
