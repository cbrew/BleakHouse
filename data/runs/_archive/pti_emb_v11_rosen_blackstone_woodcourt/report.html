James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me every time I return to those opening pages is how completely Forster subordinates human agency to the sky. The earth on its own can manage, as Forster puts it, only feeble outbursts of flowers. Everything of consequence — beauty, fertility, even glory — descends from above. Now, for a historian of British India, that is a rather loaded image. The entire ideology of colonial administration rested on precisely this logic — that order, value, civilisation itself, came down from a superior source. But Forster immediately complicates it, because the one thing that interrupts this vast flat submission is something ancient and indifferent. Listen to how he puts it: "Only in the south, where a group of fists and fingers are thrust up through the soil, is the endless expanse interrupted. These fists and fingers are the Marabar Hills, containing the extraordinary caves." Fists and fingers thrust up through the soil — that is not pastoral language. That is the language of rupture, of something the civilising project cannot smooth over.

Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
I want to hold that contrast, because Forster uses the landscape to do something very specific — he uses it to indict. Later in the novel, when the heat has become unbearable and the colonial social machinery is grinding along, Forster cuts away to a squirrel. A squirrel pressing its belly against burning scaffolding. And then comes the line that I think is one of the most devastating in the novel: "It matters so little to the majority of living beings what the minority, that calls itself human, desires or decides." The minority that calls itself human — that phrase is doing extraordinary work. Forster is not merely saying nature is indifferent. He is questioning whether the humans who hold power in this novel — the English administrators, the Club members — have earned the title.

Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
Caroline's read is exactly what I mean when I say great novelists were more radical than they knew. Forster does not editorialize. He doesn't say: Ronny is wrong, Ronny is a bigot, Ronny represents the moral rot of colonialism. He just sends in the vultures. And Forster plants something even stranger — that beyond the sky there might be something that overarches all the skies, more impartial even than they. More impartial than the sky that already renders Ronny's pronouncements meaningless. That is a novelist writing toward a metaphysics his own characters cannot see.
