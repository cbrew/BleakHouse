Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The thing that floors me every time I come back to this novel is how funny it is about desperation. Not cruel — Dickens is never merely cruel — but surgically, devastatingly funny. There's a passage — one of my absolute favourites in the whole book — where the Secretary is cataloguing the beggars who write to Boffin after he inherits the Dust fortune. That is a joke constructed with the precision of a Swiss watch, and it lands just as hard on the tenth reading.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I love about that passage is that it keeps multiplying. The inspired beggars — sitting over a fragment of candle, when suddenly an Angel whispers the name of Nicodemus Boffin to their souls. The nobly independent beggars who always regarded gold as dross — and yet require a loan, at five per cent per annum, for precisely fourteen weeks. Each category is more elaborately self-deceiving than the last. But what moves me is the undertow beneath the comedy. These people are genuinely ruined. The comedy of the beggars on horseback — who need only a clock, or a violin, or an astronomical telescope to reach the highway to affluence — is heartbreaking if you let it be. Dickens won't let the poverty be dignified, but he also won't let you simply laugh.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That tension Caroline identifies — between comedy and devastation — is exactly where the novel's political intelligence lives. Boffin's gold is the sun around which every desperate planet in this novel orbits. And what Dickens shows us — in passage after passage — is that proximity to unearned wealth produces deformation. Not just in the beggars — but in Silas Wegg, whose corruption is far more sinister. There's a passage about Wegg hovering outside Boffin's house at night that I find genuinely chilling. Dickens writes: "Power (unless it be the power of intellect or virtue) has ever the greatest attraction for the lowest natures; and the mere defiance of the unconscious house-front, with his power to strip the roof off the inhabiting family like the roof of a house of cards, was a treat which had a charm for Silas Wegg." The power to destroy is, for Wegg, indistinguishable from pleasure. That is Dickens naming something precise and ugly about how power operates on people who have none.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That nodding along is precisely the point, and it's where Dickens is doing his sharpest work. Boffin isn't corrupting Bella against her will — he's flattering a disposition she already has. The novel is honest about that. Bella arrives at the Boffins already dreaming of wealth — she says so, openly, to her own father. What Boffin does is give that dream institutional endorsement — the rich man saying: yes, this is right, this is how the world works. That is how ideology reproduces itself — not through coercion but through the validation of appetites that the system has already cultivated.

If you want to understand what this novel is actually about, start at the very first scene — Gaffer Hexam hauling something from the Thames.
