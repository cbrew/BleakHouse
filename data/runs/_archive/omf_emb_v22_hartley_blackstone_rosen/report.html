Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I find immediately striking is how Dickens opens this novel with a murder that is publicly consumed before we've had time to care about the victim. The Harmon Murder — that phrase alone is doing something very deliberate. It's already become a named public event before we understand who Harmon was. And then Dickens gives us this astonishing sentence — really it's almost a paragraph-length wave — describing how the story circulates. "like the tides on which it had been borne to the knowledge of men, the Harmon Murder went up and down, and ebbed and flowed, now in the town, now in the country, now among palaces, now among hovels, now among lords and ladies and gentlefolks, now among labourers and hammerers and ballast-heavers, until at last, after a long interval of slack water it got out to sea and drifted away." That tidal movement is not just metaphor — it is the novel's structural principle announced in miniature. The story moves exactly like that sentence: through every rank of society, exhausting itself, and then — crucially — disappearing. Dickens is telling us from the very start that this death will be misread, circulated, misunderstood, and eventually forgotten — which is precisely what allows John Harmon to survive inside it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James is right, and I'd push that further: the tidal metaphor is doing ideological work as well as structural work. Tides are natural — they're no one's fault, they can't be resisted. By mapping the circulation of a murder story onto the tides, Dickens — consciously or not — naturalises the way information flows unequally through a class society. The lords and ladies get the full force of it; the ballast-heavers get the slack water on the way out. But what I find most remarkable is what Dickens does with this dissipation of the story — he uses it to create the conditions under which a dead man can walk freely. Once the Harmon Murder has drifted out to sea, John Harmon — or Rokesmith, or Handford — can move through London without being recognised.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What moves me about Harmon is the phenomenology of it — what it actually feels like, from the inside, to be socially dead.
