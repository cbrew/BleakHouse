Before we say anything analytical, I want to plant a flag for the sheer bravado of this chapter's opening. Dickens gives us Mrs. Piper — a witness at a coroner's inquest — and she speaks in a single, unpunctuated, parenthesis-within-parenthesis torrent. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Oliver's right that it's comic — but the comedy has a very precise target. The Victorian coroner's inquest was supposed to establish the facts surrounding a sudden or unexplained death — cause, identity, circumstances. What Dickens shows us is an inquest that is completely incapable of doing that — not because the witnesses are stupid, but because the court has no real interest in the dead man at the center of it. And the passage confirms it — Mrs. Piper's entire testimony is neighbourhood rumour about a dead man she barely knew, dressed up as evidence. The phrase Dickens gives her — that the deceased, whom she insists on calling the plaintive, was "reported to have sold himself" — is pure hearsay, twice removed, and the court accepts it without a murmur.

What strikes me is the word she uses — plaintive. She means plaintiff — the legal term for the party bringing a complaint — but she's mangled it into plaintive, meaning mournful, suffering. The oath requirement is a real and important detail. Victorian courts required witnesses to swear by God — and if you couldn't demonstrate a basic understanding of divine punishment, you were excluded. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I should add some institutional context, because the comfort Dickens describes is historically precise. The Court of Chancery, before the reforms of the 1870s, operated on a fee-based system where every procedural step generated income for its officers. Delay was not dysfunction — delay was the product. Those bundles of papers on the matting at the solicitors' feet — each one represents a bill to someone who cannot afford to pay it and cannot afford to stop. So when Dickens gives us the Lord Chancellor leaning back in his very easy chair — that ease is a political statement. The system is not failing. The system is working precisely as it was designed to work.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Smallweed family passage is the counterpoint to all that pastoral ease, and it's extraordinarily bleak in its own way. Here is a family that has fully internalised the logic of capital — no amusements, no story-books, no fairy-tales, no fictions of any kind. Dickens describes the result in a sentence that is one of the funniest and most damning in the book. "Hence the gratifying fact that it has had no child born to it and that the complete little men and women whom it has produced have been observed to bear a likeness to old monkeys with something depressing on their minds." That word gratifying is doing enormous work — it's mock-congratulatory, savagely so. The system that banishes imagination produces children who are not children at all — who are, in fact, not quite human. And Dickens is making a pointed argument: commerce, when it becomes the whole of life, is a species of deformation.

What Oliver's just described is exactly what makes the legal satire in this novel so much more than comedy. The law cannot hear Jo's testimony — cannot recognize his voice as evidence — and Dickens gives him the narrator's own voice instead.
