Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What I love about Gaskell's approach to Milton is that she does something a lesser novelist would never dare: she makes the arrival ugly on purpose. There's no romantic picturesque here, no Turner atmosphere. The chapter opens with an epigraph from Matthew Arnold — just three lines, and they land like a door slamming. "Mist clogs the sunshine, Smoky dwarf houses Hem me round everywhere." That word "hem" — it's not just atmospheric, it's claustrophobic. Gaskell is signalling from the first page that this place will press in on Margaret. And then comes the train approach — this great sweep of industrial prose. She describes the factories as — and this is a sentence I've read aloud many times — "a great oblong many-windowed factory stood up, like a hen among her chickens, puffing out black 'unparliamentary' smoke." That word — "unparliamentary" — in quotation marks, halfway between a joke and an indictment. Gaskell is referencing the legislation meant to control factory smoke — and noting, with exquisite dryness, that it isn't working.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Oliver puts his finger on something important with that word. The Smoke Nuisance Abatement Act had been on the books for years by the time Gaskell was writing — and the mill owners largely ignored it. The law existed; enforcement was another matter entirely. So when Gaskell puts "unparliamentary" in quotation marks, she's invoking a real, failed piece of legislation. It's a sly, precise joke — and it tells you that her readers would have caught the reference immediately. What also strikes me in this passage is the description of the people on the streets. She notes the cotton workers are "well-dressed as regarded the material, but with a slovenly looseness" — distinguishing them sharply from London's shabby-respectable poor. It's a sociological distinction embedded in the prose — a different kind of poverty, a different relationship to labour and wages.

Daniel Rosen (literary_critic)
Both of you are circling something I want to press directly. Gaskell gives us Margaret's perception — and Margaret arrives here as a consumer of landscape, a southerner trained to see the countryside as aesthetic object. Milton refuses her that comfort entirely. The smoke is productive smoke — it's the exhalation of capital at work — and it doesn't care how it looks. And then when we get inside — into the Thornton drawing room — Gaskell performs a kind of social dissection. The room is designed to announce wealth without permitting enjoyment of it. The furniture is bagged up, the carpet covered, the books arranged like spokes — not read, arranged. This is not comfort — this is the performance of respectability by people who have earned money but not yet absorbed the codes of the class they're climbing toward.

Daniel, that's exactly it — and Gaskell gives us the sentence that clinches it. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's a genuine historical phenomenon behind that room. New manufacturing wealth in the 1840s and 50s was doing exactly this — building show houses adjacent to the mills, furnished to signal arrival into the middling orders. The Thornton house is fifty or sixty years old, Gaskell tells us — so it was built by an earlier generation, but the furnishing is new money's idea of old money's taste. And the discomfort Margaret feels — what Gaskell marvellously calls "icy, snowy discomfort" — is precisely the discomfort of a genteel observer confronting aspiration that hasn't quite arrived.

And that education starts very fast — and with considerable comic complication. Because Crampton, where the Hales are living, sits right in the path of the factory workers streaming in and out. And Gaskell's description of Margaret's first encounters with them is one of the great social comedy sequences in the novel. She tells us — "The tones of their unrestrained voices, and their carelessness of all common rules of street politeness, frightened Margaret a little at first." The girls touch her shawl, ask about the material — there's a completely unselfconscious female solidarity in it. And then the workmen comment — not on her clothes, but on her looks. And Margaret goes scarlet with outrage — and Gaskell, with perfect timing, says: but their very outspokenness marked their innocence.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's a passage that requires some historical context to appreciate fully. The spatial and behavioural codes governing street interaction in Victorian England were extraordinarily rigid — especially between classes and between sexes. For mill workers to comment on a lady's appearance in the street was, in Margaret's frame of reference, a serious violation. But those codes were southern, and bourgeois, and simply didn't operate here in the same way. What Gaskell is doing — and she does it with remarkable precision — is showing that Margaret's social grammar is the wrong grammar for Milton.

The fragility is historically precise. The trade union movement in the 1840s and 50s was in an extraordinarily precarious legal position. The Combination Acts had been repealed in 1824, but workers could still be prosecuted for conspiracy to restrain trade — the law was a minefield. And the novel's own account of why the strike fails is unsparing. Gaskell writes it almost like a diagnosis — the narrator stepping back to anatomize the defeat. But the truth was, Mr. Thornton was hard pressed. He felt it acutely in his vulnerable point—his pride in the commercial character which he had established for himself. Architect of his own fortunes, he attributed this to no special merit or qualities of his own, but to the power, which he believed that commerce gave to every brave, honest, and persevering man, to raise himself to a level from which he might see and read the great game of worldly success, and honestly, by such far-sightedness, command more power and influence than in any other mode of life. Far away, in the East and in the West, where his person would never be known, his name was to be regarded, and his wishes to be fulfilled, and his word pass like gold. That was the idea of merchant-life with which Mr. Thornton had started. “Her merchants be like princes,” said his mother, reading the text aloud, as if it were a trumpet-call to invite her boy to the struggle. He was but like many others—men, women, and children—alive to distant, and dead to near things. He sought to possess the influence of a name in foreign countries and far-away seas,—to become the head of a firm that should be known for generations; and it had taken him long silent years to come even to a glimmering of what he might be now, to-day, here in his own town, his own factory, among his own people. He and they had led parallel lives—very close, but never touching—till the accident (or so it seemed) of his acquaintance with Higgins. Once brought face to face, man to man, with an individual of the masses around him, and (take notice) out of the character of master and workman, in the first instance, they had each begun to recognise that “we have all of us one human heart.” It was the fine point of the wedge; and until now, when the apprehension of losing his connection with two or three of the workmen whom he had so lately begun to know as men,—of having a plan or two, which were experiments lying very close to his heart, roughly nipped off without trial,—gave a new poignancy to the subtle fear that came over him from time to time; until now, he had never recognised how much and how deep was the interest he had grown of late to feel in his position of manufacturer, simply because it led him into such close contact, and gave him the opportunity of so much power, among a race of people strange, shrewd, ignorant; but, above all, full of character and strong human feeling.
