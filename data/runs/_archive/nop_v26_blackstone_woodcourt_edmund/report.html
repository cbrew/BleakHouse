James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund's reading is beautiful, and I won't quarrel with the literary achievement — but I think we should be careful not to aestheticise the fog into pure metaphor. The fog in the opening of Bleak House is doing something historically specific. Dickens was writing in 1851 and 1852, and the Court of Chancery he describes was entirely real — a system of equity jurisdiction so backlogged and so opaque that cases routinely ran for decades. The fog isn't just a governing metaphor — it's an accurate account of epistemic conditions inside that court. Nobody knew the state of a case; nobody could tell you when it would be resolved. Dickens had experienced this directly — his own case, in Chancery, over the plagiarism of A Christmas Carol, had cost him more in legal fees than he recovered in damages. So when he writes — "Jarndyce and Jarndyce drones on. This scarecrow of a suit has, in course of time, become so complicated that no man alive knows what it means." Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The Megalosaurus is a fine example of Dickensian extravagance used with perfect control. But I want to draw attention to what the opening does that is not merely satirical or atmospheric — and that is the introduction of the double narrative. Dickens is the only novelist, I think, bold enough to give us two narrators of such utterly different temperament in the same novel. The omniscient narrator of the fog chapters — cold, ironic, present tense, visionary. And then Esther — warm, self-effacing, past tense, intimate. The fog narrator and the hearth narrator — and between them, the whole of human experience in the novel.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That's well put — and the tense distinction matters enormously when you think about what each narrator can see. The omniscient narrator speaks in the present tense as though the injustice is still happening — because, in a sense, it always is. Esther speaks in the past tense because she has survived to tell the tale — she has the luxury of retrospect. What Chancery does — what those endless suits did to real people — is strip away the possibility of retrospect entirely. Richard Carstone never gets to look back.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I think Edmund is right about the moral architecture, but I'd want to push back gently on the word 'allows.' The Court of Chancery in the mid-nineteenth century was not a metaphor — it was a functioning catastrophe. Cases genuinely ran for decades. Real families were consumed. The career-hopping is worth pausing on for a moment, because it has a very specific social texture in the 1840s and 50s. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
"I will begin the world" — one of the most affecting phrases in English fiction, I've long thought. What gives it that quality is not pathos alone but the grammatical tense — the future tense, spoken from the threshold of death. Richard has spent the entire novel refusing to inhabit the present moment — and at the end, the present moment closes. I would argue — and this is where I part company somewhat with James's reading — that the court is the occasion of his ruin, not its cause. The cause is a fundamental incapacity to choose — to commit to the actual over the hypothetical. Dickens understood, as Burke understood, that character is not merely shaped by institutions — it must ultimately stand over against them.

Both of those readings are true — and I don't want to dispute them — but I keep returning to what the novel does structurally. Dickens chose to write a novel with two narrators: Esther's first-person account, and the anonymous third-person omniscient voice. That is not a small formal decision — it is the argument of the book made architectural. The omniscient narrator sees the system — sees how Chancery grinds, how Parliament postures, how the fog spreads. Esther sees the individual — one face at a time, one relationship at a time. Neither perspective alone is sufficient. Together, they enact Dickens' real claim: you cannot understand injustice without seeing both its machinery and its human cost. And the novel earns that claim because Dickens spent two years making sure both registers are equally vivid.
