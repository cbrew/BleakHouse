Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me, before anything else, is that Dickens opens this novel with an act of extraordinary artistic confidence. He does not begin with a character, or a plot, or even a scene in the conventional sense. He begins with a condition — a moral and atmospheric condition — from which everything else will follow. And the genius of that lies in the fact that the fog is not merely fog. It is the outward form of a corruption that has settled so deep into English life that no one can quite see through it anymore. Now, James will tell us what the Court of Chancery actually was — and I shall not quarrel with him on that. But I would insist that Dickens is doing something more than institutional critique.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is entirely right that it is more than institutional critique — though the institutional critique is, I should note, also devastating and accurate. But what interests me most, at the outset of this novel, is how quickly Dickens establishes that the corruption is not confined to the courts. It runs through the great houses too — through the Dedlock family, through that long and terrible inheritance of pride. There is a passage — a kind of origin myth for the Dedlock doom — in which a crippled ancestral lady, repudiating her husband on the very pavement where she has fallen, delivers something very close to a formal curse. 'I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!' Now, that is Gothic machinery, certainly — but it is also a precise anatomy of how pride becomes hereditary fate. The house is cursed not by magic, but by its own nature — and Dickens understood that perfectly.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What I find electrifying about that passage is the rhythm of it — the sheer repetition. 'I will walk here' — three times, each one heavier than the last — before that final command: 'let the Dedlocks listen for my step.' When I read that aloud, I feel the weight of those words press down on everything that follows in the novel. She never complains. She never speaks of the pain. She simply endures — and then she curses.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that buried world — the world of concealment and despair — has its most visceral expression not in the great houses but in the filthy room above Krook's shop. The body of Nemo — the man who was everything and is now nothing — lying on a low bed in spectral candlelight. Dickens describes that room with a kind of forensic disgust — every detail registered through Tulkinghorn's senses as he hesitates in the doorway. 'Through the general sickliness and faintness, and the odour of stale tobacco, there comes into the lawyer's mouth the bitter, vapid taste of opium.' The lawyer's mouth. Not his nose, not his eyes — his mouth. As if the corruption is something you swallow. That is not sentimentality about poverty. That is precision.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
And it is a precision that serves the moral argument, which is what I would insist upon. Nemo is not merely a victim of poverty — he is a man who has chosen annihilation. What strikes me first, reading Chapter Thirteen carefully, is that Dickens frames Richard's problem not as poverty of circumstance but as poverty of will. James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Oliver is describing in literary terms has a concrete institutional dimension. Richard is a ward in Chancery. He has been raised in the expectation — never fulfilled, perpetually deferred — of an inheritance that the court is administering. That structural position actively cultivates the habits Esther is trying to counsel against: deferral, dependence, the postponement of real effort. Dickens understood this mechanism with great precision — Chancery doesn't merely delay justice, it distorts character.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Jarndyce's address to Richard is one of the finest pieces of moral counsel in the novel — perhaps in Dickens entire. It has the structure of a sermon without the sanctimony. He opens by placing the young man before possibility — "the world is before you" — and then immediately grounds that vision in something unsparing. "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner." That image of the heathen waggoner — drawn from the fable of the man who prayed to Hercules without putting his shoulder to the wheel — is quintessential Dickens. He takes a classical allusion and drives it, effortlessly, into plain English.

And yet Richard cannot hear it. Jarndyce also offers him something quite remarkable — in the passage just before — a kind of unconditional pastoral care. He tells them, and I find this extraordinarily generous for the period, that if they change their minds about each other, if love cools — "Don't be ashamed still to confide in me, for there will be nothing monstrous or uncommon in it." James has made me want to weep, which I consider entirely appropriate. What I carry away from this novel — every time, without exception — is the sheer strangeness of Tulkinghorn. Because Dickens fills this book with suffering that has a face, a name, a voice — and then places at its centre a man who is simply a shell. The narrator is absolutely precise about it: "He has shown neither patience nor impatience, nor attention nor abstraction. He has shown nothing but his shell." And then that extraordinary simile — listen to this: "As easily might the tone of a delicate musical instrument be inferred from its case, as the tone of Mr. Tulkinghorn from his case." That sentence is a piece of music in itself — the word 'case' doing double duty with such gorgeous economy. Dickens builds this entire novel on that contrast: the suffering that overflows into every sentence, and the emptiness at the heart of power that cannot feel it. The fog lifts, as your title has it — but Tulkinghorn's case never opens. That is the stain that remains.

Oliver has said something I want to press on just slightly. The emptiness at the heart of power — yes, absolutely. But Dickens is careful not to make that the only word on the powerful. Sir Leicester is powerful — absurd in many ways, rigid in his class prejudices — and yet Dickens insists on his moral redemption.
