What strikes me, coming at this from the historical side, is how precisely Gaskell has calibrated the social geography. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And what makes the Thornton world coherent — and, I'd argue, sympathetic despite itself — is the domestic mirror Gaskell provides in Mrs. Thornton. The narrator's analysis of Mrs. Thornton's feelings toward her two children is one of the finest passages of psychological portraiture in Victorian fiction. She is harder on Fanny, apparently — more tender — and the novel shows us why that tenderness is actually the tell of diminished expectation. "She never called her son by any name but John; 'love,' and 'dear,' and such like terms, were reserved for Fanny." "But her heart gave thanks for him day and night; and she walked proudly among women for his sake." The emotional economy of that family is entirely communicated in those two sentences. Endearments signal doubt. Silence and pride signal love.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Which brings us to the working-class world — where Gaskell takes her most serious historical risks, and largely succeeds. Bessy Higgins — who dies of mill-dust in her lungs — is not allowed to survive the novel. But the testimony her sister Mary gives Margaret after her death is one of the novel's most quietly devastating moments. Mary tells Margaret: "She were never tired o' talking o' yo'. "She used to say yo' were the prettiest thing she ever clapped eyes on. She loved yo' dearly. "Her last words were 'Give her my affectionate respects; and keep father fro' drink.'" The social distance between these two women is registered in the dialect — and then abolished by the tenderness. And then — right at the end — the sorrow curves back into the real material world. Keep father from drink. That's the world Bessy actually lived in.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And the novel doesn't let that tenderness be merely sentimental — it presses forward into action. By the final act, Margaret has inherited money — she has resources — and what she chooses to do with them is the novel's great structural payoff. She offers to lend Thornton the money to save Marlborough Mills — and she frames it as a business proposition, rigorously, deliberately. "If you would take some money of mine, eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds, lying just at this moment unused in the bank, and bringing me in only two and a half per cent. —" "— you could pay me much better interest, and might go on working Marlborough Mills." That gap between the emotional reality and the bureaucratic language she reaches for — that's Gaskell at her absolute best.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
From a historical standpoint, what Margaret is doing is genuinely radical for the period. A woman deploying inherited capital in a direct business negotiation — with a man, as an equal — is not how Victorian finance was meant to work. The Married Women's Property Act doesn't come until 1882 — this scene is set well before that. Gaskell is imagining a different order of things — and she's imagining it through the precise figure of the businesswoman, not the romantic heroine.
