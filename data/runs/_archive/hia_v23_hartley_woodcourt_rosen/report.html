Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I want to start with something that sounds Gothic and sensational but is actually one of the most precise structural statements Dickens ever made. Late in the novel, we discover what is left of a character named Krook — a man who has been, throughout, a grotesque mirror of the Lord Chancellor himself. He dies by spontaneous combustion. And Dickens describes what remains with this extraordinary, almost forensic calm. The narrator says — and I think this sentence is one of the great sentences in the novel — "Oh, horror, he IS here!" That emphasis on IS — that italicised present tense — is doing something devastating. The horror isn't that he's gone. The horror is that this — this ash, this char, this residue — this IS him. That's the novel's logic in a single word: the system burns people down until only the residue of them is left. And then it calls that nothing.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That connects to something I keep returning to with Bleak House — the way it uses what I'd call accumulative tragedy. There's a character — Miss Flite — who appears throughout the novel as a kind of eccentric, slightly comic figure haunting the courts of Chancery. But at one point she sits down and simply tells Esther what Chancery did to her family. And the effect is devastating. She says — and listen to the rhythm here, the terrible repetition — "I heard, as I had often heard before, that this was all the work of Chancery. When I got better, I went to look at the monster. And then I found out how it was, and I was drawn to stay there." 'Drawn' — that word does all the work. Father drawn. Brother drawn. Sister drawn. And then herself drawn. It's a gravitational metaphor. Chancery as a black hole. It doesn't need to be violent. It simply pulls.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That repetition — 'drawn, drawn, drawn' — is also the novel mapping a class mechanism with almost clinical precision. Notice how Miss Flite places each family member: father to bankruptcy and then debtors' prison, brother to drunkenness and rags, sister to something she won't name. Each stage is a step down the social ladder. Chancery doesn't just ruin you financially. It remakes you — degrades you — at every level. And then Miss Flite herself survives — if that's the word — as a court fixture, a human remnant, the living proof of what the system produces when it's done with you.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
There's another passage I want to bring in here — because it speaks directly to a different kind of sacrifice that Chancery's world demands. Jarndyce tells Esther about Lady Dedlock's sister — a woman who chose, in full knowledge and full grief, to vanish from the life of the man she loved. And the reason is bound up in pride, in honour — in a sense of self so rigid it becomes its own kind of cage. Jarndyce reads from her letter — or rather, conveys what it said — and the formulation is extraordinary: "she wrote him that from the date of that letter she died to him — as in literal truth she did — and that the resolution was exacted from her by her knowledge of his proud temper and his strained sense of honour, which were both her nature too." 'She died to him.' That's not metaphor — the passage insists it was literal truth. She willed herself into a kind of living death. And then the most crushing line: she 'would live in it and die in it.' She did both.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I find remarkable about that passage is its insistence on mutuality — on the way the woman's own pride is complicit in her destruction. What strikes me there is the grammatical shape of that passage — the way Dickens uses indirect speech to present Jo's testimony. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor is right about the formal brilliance, but I want to linger on the political content of those questions Nemo asks Jo. "Whether he slept sound at night, and how he bore cold and hunger, and whether he ever wished to die." These are not small-talk questions. These are the questions that no institution in the novel ever asks. The court doesn't ask. The church doesn't ask. The philanthropic societies certainly don't ask. The only person who asks is another outcast — a man so marginalised that the law won't even name him, only calling him Nemo: no one. Dickens is showing us that solidarity in this novel lives entirely outside the institutions designed to provide it.
