Daniel is absolutely right, and I want to add a third image — because there's a figure in this novel who I think holds the whole tension together. Tulkinghorn. Dickens describes his rooms as a kind of portrait of the man himself. And one detail has haunted me since the first time I read it aloud. "Everything that can have a lock has got one; no key is visible." Eight words to describe a man, a profession, an entire class that runs on secrets and withholds them as a form of power. What Jarndyce is trying to do — with Esther, with Bleak House — is precisely the opposite. He is trying to unlock something. The novel doesn't tell us he succeeds permanently. But the gesture is real, and Dickens renders it with complete seriousness.
