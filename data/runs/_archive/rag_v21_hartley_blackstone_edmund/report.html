Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The fog in the opening chapter is one of the most audacious technical gambits in Victorian fiction. Dickens doesn't just describe a fog — he enacts one. The syntax itself accumulates, clause upon clause, without resolution — the sentence refuses to arrive anywhere, just as the fog refuses to lift. And then, right at the heart of it, the Lord Chancellor appears — sitting in Lincoln's Inn Hall at the very source of it all. Dickens is telling us, in the most architectural way possible, that this fog has an origin — and the origin is the law.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is absolutely right to emphasise the structural point — but I think it's worth pausing on just how literal this indictment was. The Court of Chancery in 1852 was not a metaphor. It was a real institution that had been consuming people's lives — and inheritances — for generations, with virtually no mechanism for reform. Dickens had watched it happen to people he knew personally. When he writes about Richard Carstone later — when we see him under those Lincoln's Inn trees — the language is almost clinical in its precision. "On many such loungers have the speckled shadows of those trees often fallen; on the like bent head, the bitten nail, the lowering eye, the lingering step, the purposeless and dreamy air, the good consuming and consumed, the life turned sour." That catalogue — head, nail, eye, step, air — it's not poetry for its own sake. It's the medical symptomology of institutional harm — observed, recorded, and repeated across ten thousand ruined lives.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I wouldn't wish to diminish James's point — Chancery was genuinely monstrous, and Dickens knew it. But I confess I find the fog most interesting not as institutional critique, but as a condition of the human soul. Dickens, whatever his parliamentary enthusiasms, was first and always a moralist. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Edmund says about Leicester is important, and it connects to something structural that I keep coming back to. Bleak House has two narrators — the omniscient third-person voice, and Esther Summerson in the first person. And the omniscient narrator — the voice that gives us Leicester, that gives us Chancery at its most savage — operates at the level of the institution. Esther, by contrast, operates at the level of the individual, the particular, the felt. Dickens is already giving us, in the architecture of the narration itself, his answer to what resists the fog — and it isn't reform. It's human connection, rendered with absolute fidelity to how it actually feels from the inside.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'll grant Eleanor the structural elegance — but I do want to push back, gently, on the idea that the novel separates institution from individual quite so cleanly. Dickens keeps showing us how the institution gets inside people — how it colonises their thinking, their language, their self-image. Krook — the rag-and-bottle merchant — is the most brilliant emblem of this. He has internalised Chancery so completely that he calls himself the Lord Chancellor of his own shop. "We both grub on in a muddle," he says — speaking of himself and the real Lord Chancellor as though they are interchangeable. That four-word sentence is doing an extraordinary amount of work — it collapses the distinction between the satirical target and the comic grotesque. The system and the man who parodies it have become, morally, the same thing.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd want to add a layer of institutional reality here, because it sharpens both your points. Jarndyce in Chancery — the case, not the man — had by this period been in litigation for decades in the real Court of Chancery. There were genuine cases — Jennings v.
