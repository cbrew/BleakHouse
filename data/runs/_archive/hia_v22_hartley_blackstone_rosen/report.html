James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me is the precision of the disorientation Esther describes. The furniture is 'old-fashioned rather than old' — that distinction matters enormously. Old would imply a house that belongs to its history, that cannot be altered. Old-fashioned implies a choice — someone has curated this, assembled it with affection and a gentle eccentricity. And then there is that extraordinary sentence at the close of the passage — Esther builds it slowly, clause by clause. 'with its light, and warmth, and comfort; with its hospitable jingle, at a distance, of preparations for dinner; with the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw; and just wind enough without to sound a low accompaniment to everything we heard, were our first impressions of Bleak House' She withholds the subject of the sentence — 'our first impressions' — until the very end. It is a sentence that enacts arrival — you are held in suspension, taking it all in, before you are told what it means.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That syntactic suspension — James, yes, exactly — it's one of the most technically accomplished sentences Dickens ever gave Esther to speak. And notice what she is accumulating: sensory impressions, but never one at a time. Light and warmth and comfort — those are physical; then the jingle of dinner preparations — that's auditory, and it's at a distance. Then the face of Jarndyce — which is not a thing at all, it's a human presence — and finally, barely there, the wind outside. Outside threatens; inside shelters — and 'just wind enough without' keeps the outside present without allowing it to intrude. What Dickens is doing through Esther is teaching us how to feel about this house before anything terrible has happened in it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto the warmth of that reading for just a moment — and then complicate it. Because who is narrating this comfort? Esther — an illegitimate child, raised in shame, brought to this house as a dependent, a companion, practically a servant with better furniture. Her gratitude for Bleak House is real and moving — and it is also the gratitude of someone who has been taught that this is the best she can hope for. The quirky portraits, the sweet lavender in the drawers — these details are charming precisely because Esther has no claim to anything more formal. Jarndyce's generosity is genuine, but it is still generosity — which means it can be withdrawn.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel is right to press on that — and it brings me to the other atmosphere in this novel, which is the atmosphere of the Dedlock house. Because there is a passage in Chapter Seven that has haunted me since I first read it — the legend of the Ghost's Walk. A crippled ancestral lady, walking that terrace with increasing difficulty, until one day she falls — and when her husband comes to help her, she refuses him. And she says: 'I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!' That is a curse, delivered with absolute composure — and it is a structural prophecy for the entire novel. Pride, disgrace, calamity — those three words describe the arc of every Dedlock who follows her.

The repetition in that curse is extraordinary — 'I will walk here' stated three times, each time gaining weight. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about this scene is how carefully Jarndyce stages his own powerlessness. He says it directly — listen to how Dickens gives him this almost ostentatious self-effacement. "I am only your friend and distant kinsman. I have no power over you whatever." Now — that is not a man who has no power. That is a man performing powerlessness so as not to abuse the considerable power he has. The whole speech is structured as a kind of conditional gift: I will assume the best, but here is what happens if the assumption fails. And that conditional — "if you DO change" — is doing enormous structural work. It plants doubt without voicing it directly.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That structural conditionality is also, I think, precisely how a man of Jarndyce's legal formation would actually speak. He is a man of Chancery — or rather, a man trapped by Chancery. He thinks in hypotheticals. He hedges by disposition. But what interests me is what comes next — the advice he gives Richard about effort and Providence. "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner." The heathen waggoner — that is an allusion to Aesop, to the man who prays for help while his cart is stuck, without first putting his shoulder to the wheel. Jarndyce is telling Richard, with historical and moral precision, that passive expectation is not a virtue — it is a kind of superstition. Which, given what the Chancery suit will do to Richard, is remarkably prophetic counsel that goes entirely unheeded.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that's the bitter irony the novel keeps pressing on — Jarndyce's advice is impeccable, and it is completely inadequate. Richard is not a weak young man who simply fails to take good advice. He is a young man for whom the system has created an alternative logic. Look at what Richard actually says when he explains why he wants to enter Kenge's law office. "If I went into Kenge's office... I should have my eye on the — hum! — the forbidden ground — and should be able to study it, and master it." "The forbidden ground." He cannot even say 'the Chancery suit' plainly — he circumlocutes it, as though the suit itself has become unspeakable. The law is not a profession to Richard. It is a surveillance post. He wants to watch the suit, not practice. And here the novel is absolutely precise: the suit doesn't corrupt Richard from outside. It colonises his interiority. It becomes his purpose.
