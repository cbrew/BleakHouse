Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me about that passage is the structural confidence of it — Eliot lets the narrator interrupt the plot to offer a theory, and the theory is more interesting than the plot would be without it. Most novelists would simply show us Mr Tulliver riding off impulsively and let us draw our own conclusions. Eliot insists on the diagnosis — she wants us to understand the mechanism, not just observe the behaviour. And the skein metaphor is perfect — it's domestic, it's Midlands, it belongs to the world of these women who will spend the next few chapters sizing up their linen — and yet it illuminates something universal about how impulsive action confirms our worst fears rather than resolving them. Eliot is always doing that — reaching for the homely image that turns out to carry enormous weight.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to these childhood chapters is how precisely Eliot maps the emotional logic of a child without ever condescending to it. Take the moment when Tom favours Lucy over Maggie, and Maggie's affection for Lucy instantly curdled. Eliot gives us the exact mechanism of that transformation with a simile that is both tender and a little devastating. "Maggie would have thought a little while ago that she could never be cross with pretty little Lucy, any more than she could be cruel to a little white mouse." The white mouse is doing so much work there — it's not just a simile for innocence, it's a simile for harmless vulnerability, something you simply wouldn't think to hurt. And yet the very next sentence tells us Maggie is now contemplating slapping or pinching Lucy specifically to vex Tom. Eliot isn't mocking Maggie — she's showing us, with complete seriousness, how jealousy restructures the moral universe of a passionate child.

And then the reality of the gypsies arrives, and Eliot executes the collapse of the fantasy with gothic relish. Yes — this is the Oedipus passage, and it's breathtaking in its audacity. The context is almost absurdly mundane: Mr Tulliver is trying to raise five hundred pounds on bond, and he's determined not to borrow from anyone connected to his enemy Wakem. What strikes me, returning to the novel now, is how Eliot builds her emotional architecture on these moments of sudden reversal — a man who has resolved himself riding away, then stopping. There's a passage where Mr Tulliver has just made his decision, he's outside the yard gate, he's riding away — and then something stops him cold. "He checked his horse, and made it stand still in the same spot for two or three minutes, during which he turned his head from side to side in a melancholy way, as if he were looking at some painful object on more sides than one." That physical image — turning the head from side to side — does extraordinary work. It tells us he can't settle the pain into one direction, one object. It's everywhere — it's Maggie's future, it's his own mortality, it's the whole weight of what it means to be a father who cannot protect his child. And then he says it aloud — not to anyone — just aloud, to himself and the horse and the cold air. "Poor little wench! she'll have nobody but Tom, belike, when I'm gone." Six words of dialect carrying the whole novel's tragedy.
