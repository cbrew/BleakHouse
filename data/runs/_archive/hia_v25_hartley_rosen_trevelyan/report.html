Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to Bleak House is how ruthlessly Dickens controls what the reader is allowed to know and when. He gives us two narrators — an omniscient third-person voice and the first-person voice of Esther Summerson — and the gap between what they see is itself an argument about the novel's world. But look at what happens when that omniscient narrator turns its attention to Mrs. Piper in Chapter Eleven — a witness at an inquest. The prose performs her testimony — it becomes her: parentheses inside parentheses, no punctuation, a magnificent verbal chaos. Dickens tells us in the very first sentence that she has a good deal to say but not much to tell. "Why, Mrs. Piper has a good deal to say, chiefly in parentheses and without punctuation, but not much to tell." That single sentence is a complete critical essay on how gossip functions — and it is also very funny.

It is extraordinarily funny — and what I love is that the comedy is doing serious work at the same time. When you read Mrs. Piper aloud, you have to breathe somewhere — and there is nowhere to breathe. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Oliver is describing is exactly the function of neighbourhood rumour in a system that has no other mechanism for understanding poverty. The dead man — Nemo, nobody, a man without a name — is rendered monstrous by the community because the community has no language for a man who has simply been abandoned. But Dickens then cuts away from the inquest's fog of speculation to something far more precise — Jo's own testimony about the man. And what Jo tells us demolishes every assumption the court has been building. The man found Jo shivering in a doorway one winter night, questioned him, discovered he had not a friend in the world — and then said something that is one of the most devastating lines in the whole novel. "Neither have I. Not one! and gave him the price of a supper and a night's lodging." That exclamation mark after 'Not one' — it is not triumph, it is grief. Two people at the absolute bottom of a society recognising each other — and the man who has nothing giving what little he has.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And Dickens doesn't leave it there — he follows Jo to the cemetery after the inquest. What happens there is described in terms that are almost unbearably careful. Dickens uses 'it' — not 'he' — to describe Jo sweeping the step. Listen to this — because the pronoun choice is the whole argument. "It then, with an old broom it carries, softly sweeps the step and makes the archway clean." Society has so thoroughly dehumanised Jo that the novel's own prose briefly refuses to grant him a human pronoun. And yet the action he performs — sweeping the grave of the one man who was kind to him — is one of the most purely human gestures in the entire book.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to bring in the political counterpoint that Dickens places almost directly alongside all of this — because the juxtaposition is devastating. In Chapter Twelve we meet Lord Boodle, explaining to Sir Leicester Dedlock what is really wrong with the country. The governing class is in crisis — not because of poverty, not because of Jo sleeping in doorways — but because there is no suitable office for a man called Noodle. Dickens renders the entire political class as a set of ridiculous rhyming names — Coodle, Doodle, Foodle, Goodle — and it is both brilliant satire and a precise diagnostic. Because the names are interchangeable — and that is the point. These people are not individuals with convictions; they are a class reproducing itself. And the conclusion of Lord Boodle's analysis is — and Dickens writes this with perfect deadpan fury — "The country is shipwrecked, lost, and gone to pieces — because you can't provide for Noodle!" A boy sweeps a grave in the cold because one man showed him kindness — and Parliament is in crisis over a dinner-table placement.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me most about this sequence is the structural irony Dickens is building with such apparent gentleness. Jarndyce's speech to Richard and Ada is full of warmth — genuinely tender — and yet it is also, from our vantage point, a kind of elegy written before the death. Listen to what Jarndyce actually says — and note how carefully Dickens calibrates the rhythm of the paternal hope against the repeated retreat of that phrase 'afar off.' "I certainly did contemplate, afar off, the possibility of you and your pretty cousin here — don't be shy, Ada, don't be shy, my dear! — being in a mind to go through life together. It's Dickens placing a distance marker between what these young people are and what they might safely become — and quietly asking whether they will ever close that distance.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to stay with that image — the room darkening — because I think Dickens is being very precise about what is doing the darkening. It isn't fate, exactly. It's the suit. It's Chancery. The shadow that falls on Richard and Ada is an institutional shadow. Jarndyce knows this. That is why his counsel to Richard has such urgency — and such futility. He says — and this is the moral centre of his whole speech to Richard: "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner. Constancy in love is a good thing, but it means nothing, and is nothing, without constancy in every kind of effort." That is precisely the Protestant work ethic as moral philosophy — individual effort is the counter to institutional corruption. The problem, of course, is that Chancery will defeat Richard's efforts before they can ever find proper form. The institution swallows individuals whole.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver lands that beautifully — and it's confirmed, brutally, by Chapter Twenty-Three. By then, Richard's industry — as Esther carefully calls it — is entirely misdirected. Every effort has been bent into feeding the delusion. What Chancery does to Richard is not simply corrupt him — it replaces his will with its own logic.
