Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What a gift of a character to begin with. Tulkinghorn is, I think, one of the most frightening figures Dickens ever put on the page — and he barely moves. The horror of Tulkinghorn is precisely that nothing adheres to him — not place, not time, not feeling. Dickens gives us this extraordinary sentence — and I want to read it, because on the page it looks almost unremarkable, but spoken aloud it becomes something else entirely. "He walks into Chesney Wold as if it were next door to his chambers and returns to his chambers as if he had never been out of Lincoln's Inn Fields." That parallel construction — the two "as if" clauses — it's the grammar of a man who has erased distance itself from his experience. Chesney Wold and Lincoln's Inn Fields are the same non-place to him, because everywhere is simply where Tulkinghorn conducts his business. And then Dickens does it again — "He melted out of his turret-room" — melted, not left, not departed. The man doesn't move through space — he seeps through it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me is that Tulkinghorn's imperviousness to place is inseparable from his function. He is the keeper of aristocratic secrets — which means he is the instrument by which the ruling class maintains its grip on its own members. Dickens is precise about this — and Oliver, I think, pointed us directly at it with that word "melted." Tulkinghorn moves without friction because power has no friction — it simply flows where it needs to go. And there is a passage that makes this almost chillingly explicit — Dickens tells us he carries family secrets in every limb. "He carries family secrets in every limb of his body and every crease of his dress." Every crease of his dress — the secrets are not metaphorical, they are literally woven into the fabric of his being and his clothing. This is the man who services the Dedlock family, who holds Lady Dedlock's secret — and therefore holds her.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The historical reality behind Tulkinghorn is actually more unsettling than the fiction, in some respects. Family solicitors of this type in early Victorian England held extraordinary informal power — power that was entirely unregulated, entirely personal, and entirely dependent on what they knew. There was no professional oversight body, no code of conduct with enforcement behind it — you simply trusted your solicitor not to use what he held against you. Dickens captures exactly this ambiguity — and does so with a sentence that is almost a legal formulation. "Whether his whole soul is devoted to the great or whether he yields them nothing beyond the services he sells is his personal secret." "He keeps it, as he keeps the secrets of his clients; he is his own client in that matter, and will never betray himself." He is his own client — that is a precision that any Victorian lawyer would have recognised instantly, and flinched from. Because the moment your solicitor becomes his own client, you are no longer his principal — you are his material.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That phrase does enormous work — "if everything were known" — because the novel is structured precisely around what is not known, and who benefits from that ignorance. The fog in the opening pages isn't only meteorological — it is the systemic obscuring of accountability. Chancery runs on fog — on delay, on complexity, on the impossibility of ever seeing the whole picture clearly. And Tulkinghorn is one of the fog's primary agents — a man whose power derives entirely from what others cannot see. What is interesting — and Oliver's reading of that final line confirms it — is that Dickens is not entirely sure Tulkinghorn even believes in the class he serves. He may be a true cynic — a man who holds the aristocracy's secrets and holds the aristocracy itself in private contempt. Which makes him, if anything, more dangerous — not a zealot, but a functionary who will do whatever the system requires because the system is what pays.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That tension between service and cynicism is, I think, one of the genuinely modern things about Bleak House. The Victorian legal system was populated by Tulkinghorns — men whose loyalty was formally to their clients but whose actual power came from knowing far more than any client wanted them to know. And Chancery was the apex of this — a court so entangled in its own procedures that even its officers had ceased to understand what it was for. There's a small, dry exchange later in the novel where a character simply asks another whether he is a Chancery suitor — and the weight of that question is almost unbearable. "I believe you're a Chancery suitor, if I have heard correct?" — said with a kind of hushed recognition, as if identifying someone marked by an incurable disease. Because in Dickens' world, that is precisely what Chancery does — it marks you, it consumes you, and it never, ever lets you go.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
They are extraordinary, and they are also historically precise — which is the unsettling thing. Dickens isn't inventing grotesques for effect. He's describing what any visitor to the Court of Chancery in the 1840s would actually have seen. The little mad woman with her reticule full of paper matches — she was a recognisable type. Chancery suits lasted decades. Suitors outlasted their own sanity waiting for judgment. And Dickens catches something legally precise in that passage — the sallow prisoner trying to purge himself of contempt. That was a real procedural trap. You could be imprisoned for contempt of court in a Chancery matter, and the mechanism for release was so convoluted you might never find your way out. What Dickens understood — and this is the key thing — is that the system's cruelty wasn't incidental. It was structural. And then there's Tom Jarndyce, who gave the most precise diagnosis of what the court does to a human being. "It's being ground to bits in a slow mill; it's being roasted at a slow fire; it's being stung to death by single bees; it's being drowned by drops; it's going mad by grains." Five separate images of incremental destruction — and Tom Jarndyce eventually shot himself.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That catalogue of slow dying — I want to sit with it for a moment, because it's doing something very specific. Each image in that list is passive. The suitor isn't destroyed — he is ground, roasted, stung, drowned. The grammar of the sentence enacts the grammar of the system. Agency belongs entirely to the institution, never to the person inside it. And who gets destroyed by Chancery? Not the lawyers — they're drawing fees regardless of outcome. Not the Lord Chancellor — he yawns his way through Jarndyce and Jarndyce with magnificent indifference. The people destroyed are always the people who had something to lose — land, a legacy, a small inheritance — and couldn't afford to protect it. The law isn't broken in Bleak House. It's working exactly as designed — for those who designed it.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel is absolutely right — but I'd add that Dickens smuggles the indictment inside a kind of monstrous comedy. Think about that passage in the court — the lawyers yawning, the reporters who have long since given up, the mad old woman with her paper matches. Dickens makes you laugh at the absurdity first, and then the laughter curdles into something much darker. The short-hand writers decamp — Dickens' word — when Jarndyce comes on. Even the press has given up. Which is why I find the Skimpole passage so brilliantly wicked — Skimpole is the comedy of complicity. He says — and this is almost too perfectly phrased — that Chancery's abuses are, and I'll quote him directly: "very agreeable to me" — because they produce a charming young shepherd called Richard who transmutes legal misery into pastoral poetry. The aesthetic parasite. He won't defend the system, but he'll luxuriate in its by-products. Dickens despised Skimpole more than almost any character he ever created — and he made him irresistible on the page, which is the most damning thing he could do.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Skimpole position is historically very recognisable — and not only in the Victorian period. Every era produces its Skimpoles — people who are not ignorant of an unjust system but have arranged their sensibility so that its injustice arrives as entertainment. What makes him so lethal as a character is that he's not lying. He genuinely doesn't feel obliged to account for himself. The Poor Law reformers of Dickens' day faced this constantly — a comfortable class that could see suffering perfectly clearly and had simply decided it was not their problem. That's a more dangerous enemy than active malice, in some ways. Malice at least acknowledges the stakes.
