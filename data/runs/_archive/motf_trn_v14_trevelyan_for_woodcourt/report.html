James Blackstone (social_historian)
Yes — and this is where Eliot moves from social tragedy to something almost existential. Maggie wakes in the night after her unintended elopement with Stephen, and the novel gives us her conscience at full stretch. Eliot writes that the irrevocable wrong had been committed — "she had brought sorrow into the lives of others — into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love." 'Knit up' — that's a domestic image in a novel saturated with domesticity. But 'knit up' also implies the kind of bond that, when broken, leaves something frayed and permanently damaged. Eliot is not being sentimental — she's being forensic about the real cost of moral failure.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I find extraordinary in that passage is the legal texture of the language. Eliot uses words like 'irrevocable,' 'breach of faith,' 'outlawed' — this is the vocabulary of a court. She writes that Maggie had 'rent the ties that had given meaning to duty, and had made herself an outlawed soul.' 'Outlawed soul' — that is an astonishing compound. It takes a legal concept — banishment from the protection of the law — and applies it inward, to the self. Maggie has exiled herself from her own moral community — and Eliot finds exactly the right legal register for that.

What strikes me every time I return to the Tulliver passages is how economically Eliot loads a single line with an entire life's weight. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that defeat is entirely legible in historical terms — Tulliver is the small independent miller, the kind of man the enclosure and consolidation of the mid-century was grinding out of existence. The law didn't merely ruin him — it made the ruin feel like a personal moral failure, which is precisely what debtor culture required of its victims. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Tom is not wrong about the social mechanics, which is the uncomfortable part. Wakem senior is a man of property and legal power — his contempt for the Tullivers is structural, not personal. A marriage between Maggie and Philip would have been received, in the St Ogg's of this novel, with exactly the mockery Tom predicts. Tom's cruelty is that he deploys accurate sociological observation in the service of control — he dresses dominance up as protection. And this is, of course, precisely what men in positions of domestic authority were legally entitled to do in the 1830s.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The boat scene is the novel's great formal gamble, and I think Eliot wins it — barely, brilliantly. She needs us to understand how Maggie could drift past her own moral waymarks — literally, she drifts past the villages where she should have stopped. And she does it through the prose itself — the rhythm of the passage enacts the enchantment. "The breath of the young, unwearied day, the delicious rhythmic dip of the oars, the fragmentary song of a passing bird heard now and then, as if it were only the overflowing of brimful gladness, the sweet solitude of a twofold consciousness that was mingled into one by that grave, untiring gaze which need not be averted." 'A twofold consciousness mingled into one' — that is the definition of desire, and she gets it in eleven words. The sentence itself is breathless — subordinate clause after clause after clause — and you can only surrender to it.
