Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close, unhurried reading of Charles Dickens's last completed novel. Our Mutual Friend is a book about money — where it comes from, what it does to people, and what people will do to get it. It is also, perhaps, the strangest and most labyrinthine thing Dickens ever built. A fortune founded on dust heaps — literal mounds of Victorian London's waste — rises, changes hands, corrupts, and occasionally redeems. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The very first thing to say about this novel is that its premise is, on the face of it, completely absurd. A man inherits a vast fortune because his father spent his life accumulating the rubbish of London. And Dickens — with absolute seriousness and absolute comic genius simultaneously — takes that premise and runs with it for nine hundred pages. Now, our hero of the dust — Nicodemus Boffin — has installed himself in this grand mansion, and Dickens can't resist. He tells us that, like an eminently aristocratic family cheese, it was much too large for his wants, and bred an infinite amount of parasites. An aristocratic family cheese. I have read that line aloud professionally and I still cannot get through it without wanting to pause and simply admire it. The comic precision of it — the way it lands — is pure Dickens at the top of his game.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
One must acknowledge that the cheese is extraordinarily good. But I would resist the temptation to treat it merely as a comic flourish and move on. What is remarkable about the passage — and about Boffin from the beginning — is his contentment. He regards the parasites, the excess, the sheer incongruity of his situation — as, Dickens tells us, a sort of perpetual Legacy Duty. He pays it willingly, because Mrs Boffin enjoyed herself completely, and Miss Bella was delighted. That is not the portrait of a man corrupted by wealth. That is the portrait of a good man — made temporarily ridiculous by fortune, but not diminished by it.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me re-reading this passage — and I've read it many times — is that single phrase: the minion of fortune and the worm of the hour. That passage about power and low natures is one of the most direct moral pronouncements in the entire novel. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about these chapters is how Dickens insists on Harmon's self-examination as a moral act in itself. There is that extraordinary moment where Harmon interrogates himself aloud, checks the verdict, and confirms it. He says, and I want to read this carefully: 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife.' That triple repetition — shrinking, shrinking, mistrustful — is not rhetoric for an audience. It is a man taking his own measure, in private, with genuine dread of what he might find. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I take your point, Caroline, though I'd be cautious about pressing the modern frame too far onto the Victorian marriage plot. What Dickens is dramatising is not Harmon's failure to consult Bella — it is his terror of his own inheritance and its power to corrupt. The deception was, as the text tells us, conceived in distrust engendered by a wretched childhood — not in calculation or malice. The father and his wealth acted, in Dickens's own phrase, 'for evil — never yet for good' on everyone within their influence. That is the moral inheritance Harmon is trying to escape.
