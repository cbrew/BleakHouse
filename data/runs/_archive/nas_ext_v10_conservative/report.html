Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, coming to these early chapters, is how Gaskell constructs the North as a problem of perception. Margaret doesn't simply arrive and dislike Milton — she arrives and finds that her whole apparatus for reading the world no longer works. And that collision with Thornton in Chapter Ten is where we see it most nakedly. She's roused — Gaskell's word is wonderful, 'roused' — by what she takes as an aspersion on the South. And she fires back with something that is simultaneously a defence and an indictment. Listen to what she actually says to Thornton: "I see men here going about in the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care — who are not only sufferers but haters." That phrase — 'not only sufferers but haters' — is doing an enormous amount of work in a very small space. It tells us that Margaret sees something Thornton refuses to see: that injustice doesn't merely wound — it transforms.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take your point about perception, Eleanor, but I want to resist the temptation to read that scene as primarily sociological. What Gaskell is showing us is Margaret's character under the stress of a challenge — and crucially, her failure of self-command. Gaskell tells us she was 'angry with herself for having said so much.' That final retreat into silence is not a rhetorical flourish — it is a moral verdict on herself. The great Victorian novelists understood something that we have perhaps forgotten: that passion untempered by judgment is not virtue, however just the cause. Margaret is right about the haters and the sufferers. But Gaskell is equally interested in what her rightness costs her — in composure, in dignity, in the reader's respect.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And then there's the passage that I find almost unbearable — Margaret at the window in Chapter Five, looking up into the night sky. She's searching for God and finding nothing — or worse than nothing. Gaskell's language here is extraordinary — let me read it slowly, because it deserves that: "those never-ending depths of space, in their still serenity, were more mocking to her than any material bounds could be — shutting in the cries of earth's sufferers, which now might ascend into that infinite splendour of vastness and be lost — lost for ever, before they reached His throne." Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Thank you — yes, and this is one of those moments where Gaskell's craft is working so quietly you can almost miss it. She gives us Mrs. Shaw — the genteel widow of a general, now enjoying every comfort imaginable — and she renders her with this exquisitely light satirical touch. The key word for me is 'perplexed.' Mrs. Shaw, having every good of life, is genuinely baffled — perplexed — that she cannot find something to suffer about. And then Gaskell delivers this perfectly observed solution — listen to the rhythm of it: "she had been rather perplexed to find an anxiety, if not a sorrow. She had, however, of late settled upon her own health as a source of apprehension; she had a nervous little cough whenever she thought about it." That 'nervous little cough whenever she thought about it' is one of the funniest and most precise lines in the whole novel. It tells you everything about the southern world Margaret is escaping — a world where self-deception is not weakness but an art form.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take your point about the comedy, Eleanor, but I think the satirical reading risks flattening something that is really rather more interesting. Mrs. Shaw is not simply a figure of fun. She represents a very real — and very human — tendency: the need to cast oneself as a victim of necessity rather than an agent of desire. Gaskell says she 'preferred being compelled to gratify herself by some other person's command or desire' — she 'really did persuade herself that she was submitting to some hard external necessity.' Now, that is not merely comic. That is Gaskell identifying a failure of moral honesty — a failure to own one's choices — which she will contrast, throughout the novel, with characters who do own them. Thornton, whatever his faults, owns everything he does.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's breathtaking, actually. Gaskell is doing something technically very difficult: she's giving us Margaret's interiority and Thornton's perception of her simultaneously, in the same paragraph. We know Margaret is simply tired — she 'would much rather have remained silent' — but we watch Thornton reading her tiredness as contempt. And then there's this extraordinary physical description — Gaskell slows the whole scene down to paint Margaret as Thornton sees her: "her round white flexile throat rising out of the full, yet lithe figure; her lips moving so slightly as she spoke, not breaking the cold serene look of her face with any variation from the one lovely haughty curve; her eyes, with their soft gloom, meeting his with quiet maiden freedom." That word 'flexile' — supple, yielding — placed right next to 'haughty curve': it's a portrait of something irresistible precisely because it seems so indifferent. Thornton is falling in love and hating himself for it in the same breath.
