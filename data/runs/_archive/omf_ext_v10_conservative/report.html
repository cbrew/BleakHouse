Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It feels like nothing else in Victorian fiction — and I mean that precisely. Dickens gives us John Harmon working through his own past in real time, almost interrogating himself, as though he doesn't quite trust his own memory. There's a structural tic Dickens deploys here that I find quite brilliant: the self-correction. Harmon asks himself, 'Is that so?' and then immediately answers, 'That is exactly so.' It's a mind verifying its own testimony — like a witness in a courtroom who also happens to be the judge. And then there's the extraordinary accumulation of that central sentence — listen to how it builds. 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife.' That repeated 'shrinking' — it's not decoration, it's a physical sensation. The word enacts what it describes — a man recoiling from his own inheritance, from his own name.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think Eleanor is right to fix on the moral weight of that sentence, though I'd frame it slightly differently. What strikes me is not the technique, but the character it reveals. Harmon is a young man who has been left a great fortune and finds it, quite genuinely, a burden rather than a blessing. That is not a sociological observation about the corrupting nature of wealth — it is a portrait of a conscience. He fears, quite specifically, that money is already making him avaricious — that the disease has begun before he even knows it. Dickens calls it 'mistrustful that I was already growing avaricious' — that adverb 'already' is the knife in the sentence. It suggests the corruption is not hypothetical but present — quietly underway, like rot in timber.

That connects beautifully to the second passage — because Dickens immediately moves from this raw self-examination to something altogether more plotted and practical. Harmon explains how he and this stranger, Radfoot, hatched their scheme — to disguise themselves as sailors and observe Bella Wilfer from close quarters. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I confess the contrivance of that plot has always tested my patience slightly — though Dickens earns it, eventually. What interests me more is what the scheme reveals about Harmon's state of mind. He wants to know whether Bella Wilfer is mercenary before he presents himself — he wants to test her character anonymously, from a safe remove. There is something both admirable and slightly cowardly in that impulse — and Dickens, I think, sees both things clearly. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Which brings us, rather naturally, to Charley Hexam — who performs a self he has constructed with enormous effort, only to find it cracking under him. The passage from chapter fifty-seven — Charley confronting Bradley Headstone — is one of the coldest, most uncomfortable scenes in the novel. Charley has clawed his way out of his origins — he is educated, ambitious, respectable — and now he sees all of that threatened by Bradley's violence. And his complaint is not moral outrage — it is something far more self-interested and therefore more honest. He says to Bradley — and this is extraordinary — 'how do you know that, pursuing the ends of your own violent temper, you have not laid me open to suspicion?' His entire grievance is reputational — not that a man has been nearly murdered, but that Charley's good name might be smirched by association. Dickens gives him no redemptive irony — Charley means every word, and that is damning.

What strikes me first about Harmon's decision is how Dickens frames it — not as heroic resolution, but as something that crept up on him. There's a passage in the later chapter that captures this with devastating precision. 'He had lapsed into the condition in which he found himself, as many a man lapses into many a condition, without perceiving the accumulative power of its separate circumstances.' Therefore, the conditional clause he proposed was, that, if the minion should return in his late sneaking manner, and if, being closely watched, he should be found to possess himself of anything, no matter what, the sharp sword impending over his head should be instantly shown him, he should be strictly examined as to what he knew or suspected, should be severely handled by them his masters, and should be kept in a state of abject moral bondage and slavery until the time when they should see fit to permit him to purchase his freedom at the price of half his possessions. If, said Mr Wegg by way of peroration, he had erred in saying only ‘Halves!’ he trusted to his comrade, brother, and partner not to hesitate to set him right, and to reprove his weakness. It might be more according to the rights of things, to say Two-thirds; it might be more according to the rights of things, to say Three-fourths. On those points he was ever open to correction.
