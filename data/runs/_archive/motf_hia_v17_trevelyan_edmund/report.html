Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to this novel is how early Eliot establishes the core tragedy — and she does it through comedy. There's that wonderful scene with Maggie and the Latin Grammar — Tom's Latin Grammar, which supposedly no girls can learn. And Maggie, of course, finds it immediately absorbing — she skips straight past the rules to the examples. Eliot's description of what captivates her is one of the most precise accounts of a certain kind of literary imagination I've ever read. She sets it up so carefully before she gives you the image — listen to this. "These mysterious sentences, snatched from an unknown context,—like strange horns of beasts, and leaves of unknown plants, brought from some far-off region,—gave boundless scope to her imagination." Strange horns of beasts, leaves of unknown plants — that is a child's mind working at full stretch. And the sorrow built into the comedy is that this magnificent instrument is going to be given absolutely nothing adequate to work on.

I take your point about comedy, Eleanor, though I think we should be careful not to let structural admiration muffle the moral substance. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right to flag that passage, and I would add that Eliot's satirical intelligence here is deployed in the service of a deeper moral observation. What she is describing, beneath the comedy of the camel and the feather, is a man who has surrendered his will to a cluster of resentments. And chief among those resentments is his hatred of Wakem — which is not merely personal but almost mythological in its intensity. Eliot tells us, with her characteristic deadpan, that Wakem's rascality was of a peculiarly aggravated kind — the kind that placed itself in opposition to Mr Tulliver's interests and opinions. That is the comic sting, of course — that the definition of injustice in Tulliver's mind is whatever inconveniences him personally. It is the portrait of a man whose honest self-belief has curdled, under pressure, into something very close to monomania.

It is extraordinary, isn't it — two sentences carrying a whole critique of the legal system. And the key is that Eliot affirms his honesty before she dismantles his reasoning. And then comes the image that crystallises it all: There is the whole corruption laid bare — honesty that has philosophically accommodated itself to the very knavery it despises.

This, he considered, was the only basis of solid instruction; all other means of education were mere charlatanism, and could produce nothing better than smatterers.
