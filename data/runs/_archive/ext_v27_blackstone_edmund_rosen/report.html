Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The opening of Bleak House is, I think, among the most audacious in all of English prose fiction. Dickens does not begin with a character, not with a scene in the conventional sense, not even with a complete sentence. He begins with a word: London. Just that — a noun dropped into silence, like a stone into a very deep well. And then this extraordinary sentence arrives — and I want to read it, because it deserves to be heard properly. "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A Megalosaurus — waddling up Holborn Hill. Dickens has, in a single image, driven London back to the primordial — and the joke is perfect because it is also completely serious. This is not mere atmosphere-painting. This is a theological statement about what civilisation has become.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right that there's a theological dimension — but I'd want to hold that alongside something very specific and material. Michaelmas term, as Dickens signals immediately — this is the legal term when Chancery is in session. The fog and the law arrive together from the novel's very first breath. The Court of Chancery in the 1850s was a genuine catastrophe — cases that ran for decades, estates entirely consumed by legal costs. Dickens isn't exaggerating Jarndyce and Jarndyce for comic effect. He is, if anything, understating it. Notice what Dickens does with the mud — foot passengers "adding new deposits to the crust upon crust of mud, sticking at those points tenaciously to the pavement, and accumulating at compound interest." Compound interest — in a description of mud. That's an economic metaphor buried in filth, and it is entirely deliberate. The city is literally accruing corruption, layer upon layer, and no one can pay off the debt.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James has put his finger on something I think is central — the fog is not decorative, it is structural. Dickens shows us a city where every class is degraded by this atmosphere — dogs, horses, foot passengers — and yet the degradation is not equally distributed. The fog obscures — and what it obscures, systematically, is the relationship between cause and effect. Consider what Dickens gives us later — a man called Nemo, lying in a filthy room. "Through the general sickliness and faintness, and the odour of stale tobacco, there comes into the lawyer's mouth the bitter, vapid taste of opium." The fog has a taste by the time it reaches that room. It is the taste of a life utterly expended and utterly discarded. And the only witness is Tulkinghorn — the lawyer — who tastes it in his own mouth and feels, we are told, nothing in particular. That is the system working exactly as designed.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I'll grant Daniel his point about Tulkinghorn — the man is a remarkable creation precisely because his coldness is absolute, and Dickens renders it without flinching. But I'd resist the idea that atmosphere in Dickens is merely — merely — a political instrument. There's something here that goes deeper than class critique — something almost eschatological. The soot, Dickens tells us, is "gone into mourning, one might imagine, for the death of the sun." The death of the sun. Not the failure of an institution — the extinction of light itself. Dickens is writing in a tradition that includes Dante and Milton, not only Edwin Chadwick's sanitary reports.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I don't think those things are mutually exclusive — the eschatological and the institutional can occupy the same image. And the image that haunts me most in this opening movement of the novel is not the fog over London — it's the ghost on the terrace at Chesney Wold. Dickens gives us the legend of the crippled lady — and her curse, delivered to her husband in the moment of her collapse, is extraordinary. "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" That is a curse perfectly calibrated to the Dedlock story — pride humbled, disgrace incoming. The ghost doesn't haunt because of the supernatural — she haunts because the institution of aristocratic pride creates its own nemesis. The fog reaches Chesney Wold too. It just arrives dressed as a ghost.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James's reading of that curse is exactly right — and I'd add one thing. The crippled lady walked that terrace in pain, every day, without complaint — and no one in the house spoke of it. Dickens tells us she "never spoke to any one of being crippled or of being in pain" — the house simply accommodated her suffering and moved on. That is the Dedlock house in miniature — and, I'd argue, the English class system in miniature. Suffering is absorbed, unseen, until it erupts — as a curse, as a scandal, as a death. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me first is not the pathos — though the pathos is overwhelming — but the precision of the prose. Dickens gives us Jo's testimony in reported speech, and the effect is extraordinary — both clinical and devastating. Consider what we're told, in that flat, accumulative way: "No father, no mother, no friends. Never been to school." Three words, three words, three words — and then four — and you have an entire life's desolation laid out like an inventory. What is remarkable is that Dickens does not sentimentalise this — not yet. He is recording, not pleading — and that restraint makes the plea all the more powerful.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The legal dimension here is worth dwelling on, because it is not incidental — it is the whole mechanism. Jo is rejected as a witness because he cannot demonstrate sufficient understanding of the oath — he does not know what will be done to him, in any formal religious sense, if he lies. And yet Dickens shows us, with some care, that Jo does know something about truth and consequence. He knows, as the passage puts it, that: "it'll be something wery bad to punish him, and serve him right — and so he'll tell the truth." The irony is exquisite and savage: the boy who cannot be sworn in to give evidence is the one character in the room whose moral instinct about truth-telling is entirely sound. The law's exclusion of Jo is not an accident or a hard case — it is a structural feature.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James is right that it's structural, and I want to push on exactly what that structure is doing. The law doesn't exclude Jo by accident, or even by indifference — it excludes him because he has nothing the system recognises as social standing. No name beyond a single syllable. No family. No school. No property. No church membership to ground an oath. He is, in the most literal Victorian legal sense, nobody. And then we get to what Jo does know — and it concerns the dead man, Nemo. Dickens gives us the scene of their first meeting — that cold winter night — and the single line Nemo speaks is everything. "Neither have I. Not one!" Two men at the absolute bottom of the social order, recognising each other across the darkness — that is the solidarity the system cannot account for, and cannot permit.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
This is where the inquest scene earns its comedy too, and I think we shouldn't let it pass. Mrs. Piper's testimony is placed immediately before Jo's, and the contrast is devastating. She is accepted as a credible witness — she has a husband, a child, a home, a name — and what she produces is this: "the plaintive — so Mrs. Piper insists on calling the deceased — was reported to have sold himself." Rumour, malapropism, a dream about a pickaxe — this is what the court admits as evidence about the dead man's character. The legal system credits the garrulous property-owner and discredits the silent, truthful pauper. That is not a satirical exaggeration — that is approximately how it worked.

Edmund is right about the rhetorical inversion, and I'd add a historical layer. The brickmaker's monologue is also a precise documentary account of what poverty in a brickmaking settlement actually looked like in mid-Victorian England. What makes Dickens extraordinary is that he embeds this social fact in dialect speech, so it arrives as the character's own testimony rather than a reformer's report. The grammar is broken; the evidence is not.

Edmund frames that as character — but I'd say Esther's counsel is itself a kind of innocence. She believes constancy and fortitude are sufficient — that love and duty, held firmly enough, can overcome a corrupting institution. The novel will prove her wrong on Richard's behalf — not because he lacks good impulses, but because the system is designed to convert hope into dependency. And that image of the sun clouding over as they leave — that is not just atmosphere. Dickens writes: "The room darkened as they went out, and the sun was clouded over." That is a verdict — written in light.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me about all three passages is how early Dickens places his markers. Richard's trajectory is established before he has properly begun — the languid disposition, the absent vocation, the shadowed departure. The court does not simply destroy its victims — it does so with a terrible patience, over years. And the novel holds both ideas simultaneously — that Richard had goodness in him, and that goodness was never going to be enough.
