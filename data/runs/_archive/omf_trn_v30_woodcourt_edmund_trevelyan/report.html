Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
It is a set piece — and the extraordinary thing is that Dickens hides how ferocious it is inside the comedy. The rhythm of it — fifty-seven churches, forty-two parsonage houses, seven-and-twenty organs — that's not prose, that's a comic auctioneer calling bids on human decency. And then right in the middle of that catalogue, there's this magnificent piece of documentary theatre. "Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet." Six words of pure visual comedy before we've even opened the envelope. The duke hasn't written a letter to Boffin — he's sent a lithographed form letter, presenting, as Dickens puts it, 'but a pale individuality of an address.' The Steward's fee is three guineas — and Dickens calls that 'thoughtful in the postscript.' The contempt in that word 'thoughtful' is absolute — and delivered with a perfectly straight face.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me is the moral architecture beneath the comedy. Dickens is not merely laughing at the Duke of Linseed — he is diagnosing a civilization in which charity has become a transaction, an exchange of social credibility for money. The duke offers Boffin 'popularity and distinction' in return for three guineas. Boffin is being invited to purchase respectability — and the invitation arrives from the highest reaches of the social order. That, I think, is what Dickens truly finds intolerable — not poverty, but the corruption of the forms by which society is supposed to hold itself together.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I want to linger on the individual beggars passage, because I think it's doing something even more uncomfortable. Dickens gives us a taxonomy — the inspired beggar, the nobly independent beggar, the beggar on horseback — and each category is beautifully observed. But there's a line that stops me every time I read it. "They call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf." The cruelty of that simile — 'minced veal to a calf' — is breathtaking. It reduces the desperate paperwork of poverty to something butchered and ground up beyond recognition. Dickens is laughing — and then suddenly you feel the edge of the blade.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
There is, though, a counter-current worth acknowledging. Dickens's satire here is directed at the machinery of false charity — but Boffin himself is a genuinely good man. The flood of impostors does not — at least not yet — corrupt the Golden Dustman's instinct for goodness. And we should bear in mind what the will reveals about how this fortune was built. Wegg reads aloud a will in which old Harmon, having — as the document puts it — 'never made friends, and ever had a rebellious family,' leaves the Little Mound to Boffin and everything else to the Crown. "Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown." 'Quite enough for him.' The contempt of that phrase, directed at a loyal servant — it tells us everything about the moral wilderness from which this fortune emerges.

What strikes me every time I return to this passage is the sheer accumulation of the syntax. Dickens doesn't give Harmon a clean reason for coming back — he gives him a pile of mistrusts. Listen to this — and I mean really listen to the repetition. 'I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife.' The boat went on, under the arching trees, and over their tranquil shadows in the water. The bargeman skulking on the opposite bank of the stream, went on after it. Sparkles of light showed Riderhood when and where the rower dipped his blades, until, even as he stood idly watching, the sun went down and the landscape was dyed red. And then the red had the appearance of fading out of it and mounting up to Heaven, as we say that blood, guiltily shed, does.
