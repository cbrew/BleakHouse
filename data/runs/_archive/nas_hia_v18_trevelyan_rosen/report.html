Delighted to be here — I've been living with this novel in my ears for thirty years, and it still catches me off guard. What strikes me first, every time, is how sharply Gaskell draws Margaret's contradictions — even Margaret herself sees them. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd only add that what Margaret is really examining there is her own class position — and finding it unstable. She despised people for showing emotion — that's a marker of her genteel formation, her Helstone upbringing. But Milton is pulling her across a boundary she didn't know existed. And that tension between refinement and real human need runs through every scene in this opening. Take the passage with Bessy Higgins — dying slowly from fluff in her lungs from the cotton mills. She asks Margaret whether such a life is worth caring for — and then murmurs: "They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more: neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat." A dying mill-worker reaching for Revelation because nothing in her actual world offers comfort. That's the system Gaskell is describing — and she doesn't flinch from it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Yes — because it's where the novel's private grief becomes impossible to separate from questions of authority and justice. Mrs. Hale is recounting the moment they learn that Frederick is called a traitor — a mutineer. She goes to meet her husband on the road from Southampton, and the image of him returning is devastating. "His arms hanging loose down, his head sunk, and walking heavily along, as if every step was a labour and a trouble." Gaskell gives you the body before she gives you the words — because the body already says everything. And then what follows — Mr. Hale unable to speak, holding his wife, weeping in a strange muffled groaning voice. This is a family being broken by an institution — the Navy, the law of mutiny — that has no interest in whether Frederick was right or wrong.

Gaskell is an absolute master of the ironically precise 'if.' She puts you inside Margaret's consciousness and Thornton's simultaneously, via a single conditional clause. And what strikes me as a performer is that the whole scene has the quality of a very good piece of stage direction. He goes upstairs two steps at a time — urgency. She's telling every future actor exactly how to play this man. Every note is there.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And yet — the reconciliation the novel offers is not a resolution of what Margaret named in that parlour. The men on the streets of Milton — ground down, sufferers and haters — they are still there at the novel's end. The union between Margaret and Thornton is genuinely moving. I don't dismiss it. But Gaskell is too honest a novelist to suggest that two people falling in love solves a structural problem. What the novel does — and this is its radical achievement — is refuse to let you forget both things at once.

“You are mistaken,” said Margaret, roused by the aspersion on her beloved South to a fond vehemence of defence, that brought the colour into her cheeks and the angry tears into her eyes. “You do not know anything about the South. If there is less adventure or less progress—I suppose I must not say less excitement—from the gambling spirit of trade, which seems requisite to force out these wonderful inventions, there is less suffering also. I see men here going about in the streets who look ground down by some pinching sorrow or care—who are not only sufferers but haters. Now, in the South we have our poor, but there is not that terrible expression in their countenances of a sullen sense of injustice which I see here. You do not know the South, Mr. Thornton,” she concluded, collapsing into a determined silence, and angry with herself for having said so much.
