The thing about Gaskell — and I've spent a considerable amount of my professional life inside her sentences — is that she has no fear of the ugly. Most Victorian novelists, when they must show you a corpse, look away at the critical moment. Gaskell looks straight at it. There's a passage in chapter thirty-six — the discovery of Boucher — that I find almost unbearable to read aloud, but impossible not to. "Owing to the position in which he had been found lying, his face was swollen and discoloured; besides, his skin was stained by the water in the brook, which had been used for dyeing purposes. The fore part of his head was bald; but the hair grew thin and long behind, and every separate lock was a conduit for water." Every separate lock was a conduit for water. That's not description — that's observation so precise it becomes compassion.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right to dwell on that passage, and I would add — what's remarkable is not the grotesque detail itself, but what Margaret does immediately afterwards. She steps forward and covers the dead man's face with her handkerchief. Gaskell calls it, with perfect plainness, her "pious office." That is the moral imagination at work. Margaret doesn't philosophise about industrial poverty or assign blame to the system. She acts — quietly, instinctively, humanely. That is what Gaskell means to show us about character — it reveals itself not in speech, but in a single gesture.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And grief in this novel is never straightforward, is it. There's a passage later — chapter forty-two — when Margaret's aunt Shaw arrives after the deaths, and Margaret has been in this strange suspended state: white, motionless, tearless. And then Gaskell gives us this extraordinary sentence about what finally breaks her open. "All thoughts of quiet habitual love, of tenderness for years, of relationship to the dead, all that inexplicable likeness in look, tone, and gesture, that seem to belong to one family, and which reminded Margaret so forcibly at this moment of her mother, came in to melt and soften her numbed heart into the overflow of warm tears." That accumulation — love, years, likeness, gesture — it's like a wave that's been building for three chapters finally breaking. And I defy anyone to read it aloud without feeling it in the chest.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one must also consider the point of view here. Because Gaskell gives us, in the same stretch of chapters, Thornton's interiority — and it is entirely different in register. For Margaret, Milton is the place where she suffered. For Thornton, those same eighteen months were — and Gaskell chooses this phrase with great care — "so unspeakably precious, down to its very bitterness, which was worth all the rest of life's sweetness." The same period, the same rooms, the same streets — two irreconcilable experiences. That is not sociology — that is tragedy, in the classical sense.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And then there's Helstone — which I think is the passage that most haunts me personally, chapter forty-six. Margaret returns to the place of her childhood and it has changed — but not dramatically, not obviously. Gaskell puts it like this — and it's one of the great sentences in Victorian prose, I would argue — "the natural mutations brought by days and months and years, which carry us on imperceptibly from childhood to youth, and thence through manhood to age, whence we drop like fruit, fully ripe, into the quiet mother earth." Whence we drop like fruit, fully ripe. There's no terror in it — just the gentlest possible acceptance. Gaskell is writing about death the way a great gardener might — as the natural conclusion of a season.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
She writes a scene that belongs, in spirit, to stage comedy — and she knows it. You have Thornton, the formidable manufacturer, reduced to watching a bracelet slide down a woman's wrist. Not once — repeatedly. Gaskell tells us he watched the push and the fall with such fixity that he almost exclaimed aloud. "There it goes again!" That exclamation point is doing the work of a whole interior monologue. Gaskell drops us inside his head for one second and then she's gone again — and the comedy is complete. Then there's the sugar-tongs business, which is the real masterpiece of the passage. She hands him his tea — and Gaskell writes this — "She handed him his cup of tea with the proud air of an unwilling slave; but her eye caught the moment when he was ready for another cup." An unwilling slave serving tea. That's six words that contain the entire comedy of their first fifty pages.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The comedy is perfectly observed, and I wouldn't diminish it for a moment. But what strikes me is that Gaskell embeds it entirely within Margaret's sense of duty. Her head aches throughout this scene. She is not there by choice — she is there because her father must not seem neglected. Gaskell says she was resolved — that is a very deliberate word — to throw herself into the breach. So beneath the comedy of the bracelet and the proud serving-girl, there is a portrait of character under quiet strain. And that, I think, is what separates Gaskell's comedy from mere social satire. It is always rooted in moral situation.
