Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The Dust Heaps are one of the great central metaphors in all of Victorian fiction, and Dickens knew exactly what he was doing. Dust, in this novel, is wealth — accumulated, inherited, fought over. And the fortune's origin in literal refuse makes explicit what capitalism usually works very hard to disguise — that the accumulation of wealth is always also an accumulation of someone else's waste and labour. What I find remarkable is how the novel frames what happens when that fortune passes to the Boffins. Wegg reads out the terms of the old miser Harmon's will with a kind of gleeful contempt. 'Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown.' 'Quite enough for him' — that sneer is the voice of capital judging its own servants. Boffin gets the small heap — barely a crumb from the table — and the system gets the rest.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
'Ignorant and unpolished' — that phrase does a great deal of work, and not entirely in the Boffins' favour, is the honest reading. The Victorian class system had a very particular anxiety about new money — about people who had accumulated wealth without acquiring the cultural apparatus that was supposed to accompany it. And Dickens satirises that anxiety brilliantly in what happens to the Boffins the moment their fortune becomes known. The novel's description of the charitable correspondence flooding in is one of the great comic inventions of the book. 'Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet.' Each word there is doing heavy satirical lifting — 'fat', 'private', 'double', 'ducal'. The letter is of course lithographed by the hundred — the 'pale individuality of an address' is priceless. In historical reality, this was precisely how Victorian charitable fundraising worked — the aristocracy lending their names, at a fixed fee, to lend legitimacy to causes they had no particular interest in.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James has put his finger on something that runs through the whole novel — the way that charity and class operate as a single mechanism. The Duke of Linseed's stewardship fee is three guineas — that's the price of admission to respectability. What Dickens understands is that charity is not the opposite of exploitation — it is one of its most efficient instruments. The Boffins' moral straightness — that 'religious sense of duty' the narrator describes — is precisely what makes them vulnerable to this machinery. Good people, in Dickens's world, are always the first to be exploited by systems designed for bad ones.

The dilemma Dickens constructs in chapter thirty-one is, in legal terms, a genuine double bind — and it's worth being precise about why. If Harmon comes to life and accepts the inheritance, he must, as the passage puts it, do 'the former' — the shame of buying her. James Blackstone (social_historian)
There's something almost legally precise about that list — disinterestedness, uprightness, tenderness, goodness — each word a rebuke to a specific vice the novel has catalogued. Disinterestedness against the Veneerings' calculated social climbing. Uprightness against Wegg's conniving and Riderhood's blackmail. Tenderness and goodness against a whole world that has monetised both. And yet — this is the historian in me speaking — the Boffins' goodness does not change the structure. The dust heaps are still there. The fortune still exists. Goodness, in this novel, operates through inheritance — not against it.
