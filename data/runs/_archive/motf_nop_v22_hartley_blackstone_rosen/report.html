James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is absolutely right about that yearning, and I'd want to give it a very specific historical location. The 1820s and 30s — when the novel is set — were a period in which the question of what to do with clever girls was genuinely unresolved. There were no institutions for them. The mechanics' institutes, the literary societies, the grammar schools — all closed. Tom goes to Mr. Stelling's to learn Latin and Euclid — useless, as it turns out, for a boy who'll need to understand commerce — but it is an education, of a kind. Maggie tags along, absorbs everything faster than Tom does, and is told — gently, but firmly — that this is not for her. Eliot captures that perfectly in the Stelling chapters. Mr. Stelling's verdict on Maggie is that she has 'a quick, shallow mind,' that she would 'never go far into anything.' 'She would never go far into anything' — and the narrator's irony is devastating, because we have already seen that she goes further into everything than anyone around her. That gap between institutional verdict and actual human reality is something historians recognise very well. The records are full of it.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I'd add to both of those observations is that Maggie's hunger isn't just a personal characteristic — it's a structural problem that the novel diagnoses with real precision. Eliot is showing us a child who has been produced by her environment — the mill, the river, the provincial town — and who is simultaneously surplus to that environment's requirements. The Tulliver family is caught in that particular Victorian squeeze — they have pretensions to respectability, they're property-owning, but they are one bad season, one lawsuit, away from ruin. And what that means for Maggie — a daughter, not a son — is that her intelligence has literally no market value in this economy. Eliot is very clear about this. Think of how the novel opens — the narrator watching a girl on a bridge, gazing at the water. 'How she comes to be there is not so very difficult to divine' — that's the narrator, already placing us in the position of a society that presumes to know what a girl like Maggie is doing and why. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Daniel's point about the opening is crucial, because that frame — the dreaming narrator, the girl by the water — is doing something technically very bold. Eliot begins in a kind of waking dream. The narrator is watching, but the boundary between dreaming and remembering is deliberately blurred. And then — snap — we're inside the Tulliver household. The shift is almost cinematic. But what that opening establishes — before a word of plot — is that this is a story being told from loss. From after the flood, in every sense. The River Floss is not just a setting. It's the novel's emotional centre of gravity from the first page.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that river is also, if I may be prosaic for a moment, an economic fact. The mill exists because of the Floss. The Tulliver prosperity — such as it is — depends on water rights and milling rights that are, from the novel's first chapters, legally contested. Mr. Tulliver's great obsession — the lawsuit with Wakem — isn't a subplot. It is the mechanism by which the family's precarious position becomes catastrophic. Eliot understood — better than many novelists of her era — that financial ruin is not just a misfortune. It is a social verdict. It strips you of your place in the hierarchy. And for Maggie, who was already marginal, that ruin will simply make explicit what was always implicit — that she has no sanctioned place in St. Ogg's at all.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James puts his finger on something that I think is the novel's central, brutal insight — that for women of Maggie's class and era, respectability is the only currency. Not education, not intelligence, not moral seriousness — all the things Maggie actually possesses in abundance. The novel is going to put Maggie through a series of trials, and at each one, what counts against her is not her actions but her deviation from what a woman of her station is supposed to look like. The dark hair, the passionate nature, the restlessness — Eliot establishes all of these as marks against Maggie before she's done a single thing wrong. She is already, as a child, being read as transgressive by everyone around her. That is what makes this opening so devastating to re-read, knowing what's coming. The verdict on Maggie is already in — before she's lived her life.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And Eliot knew exactly what she was doing — because she was writing from the inside of that experience. The autobiographical pressure behind this novel is enormous. Mary Ann Evans had to become George Eliot — had to take a man's name — to be taken seriously as a thinker and a writer. Maggie is not George Eliot, but she's built from the same materials — the same hunger, the same frustration, the same impossible situation. And there's a moment — I find this almost unbearable — where the narrator steps back and says, in effect: I knew a child like this. I was that child. That intimacy between narrator and character is what gives this novel its peculiar, aching power.

What strikes me first is the specific texture of Tulliver's legal predicament — and how perfectly Eliot renders it. Tulliver is not a victim of some exceptional villainy on Wakem's part — he's a victim of the ordinary operation of chancery and civil litigation in this period. The man goes to law to settle a dispute about water rights — about who controls the flow of a river — and the law consumes him entirely. Tulliver tells Mr. Riley early in the novel, with that magnificent stubbornness of his, that he means to try the law on it. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor's point about Tom is the one that deserves to be pressed, because Tom's relationship to the law is a study in a different kind of damage. Tom internalises not his father's belief in justice, but his father's humiliation — and he converts that into an obsessive drive to repay the debt and reclaim the mill. What's historically precise about that is the way Victorian commercial law structured debt as a matter of personal honour as well as financial liability. Tulliver has his creditors come to the mill — he pays them back himself, in person, shilling by shilling — because the humiliation has to be personally undone. Eliot observes that Tulliver, having paid every penny, then immediately strikes Wakem with his riding whip — he pays the debt and then takes his revenge in the same day. The sequence tells you everything about how the law operates as a psychic force, not just a financial one.
