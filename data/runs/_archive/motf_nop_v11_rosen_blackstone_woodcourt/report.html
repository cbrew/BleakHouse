James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel is right, and I'd add a layer of historical precision to that. Host (host)
Welcome back to The Mill on the Floss Unpacked. We've been circling the Tulliver household — its warmth, its pride, its precariousness. Now we turn to what George Eliot presents as the crushing machinery behind that precariousness: the law, and the man who wields it — John Wakem. Daniel, you've argued that in this novel the law is never neutral — it's always someone's instrument. I want to start there.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The law in this novel is one of the most brilliantly cold-eyed things Eliot ever wrote. Mr. Tulliver begins the novel convinced that the law is a realm of reason — that right will out if you pursue it far enough. And what does the novel methodically show us? That the law is not a system of justice but a system of property — and those without capital are simply consumed by it. Tulliver loses his lawsuit over the water rights on the Ripple, is ruined financially, and then — in the cruelest irony — is forced to work as a tenant miller under the very man who destroyed him. The law doesn't just defeat Tulliver — it converts him into a servant of his own defeat.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it's worth being precise about the historical scaffolding here, because Eliot is not exaggerating. Water rights litigation in the early nineteenth century was notoriously ruinous for small landholders. The legal costs alone could consume an estate before a judgment was ever reached. Wakem is a solicitor — not a judge, not a legislator — simply a man who knows how the machinery works and is entirely willing to use that knowledge against people like Tulliver. What's so devastating in the novel is that Wakem is not even presented as a cartoon villain. He is presented as a perfectly reasonable professional man doing what the system rewards.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That's what makes him so chilling to read — precisely that reasonableness. The Bible entry is crucial, legally and symbolically. Tulliver is in effect writing a deed of grievance — making it a document, making it heritable. He's trying to use the only instrument he has left — the family record — to do what the law would not do for him. And forcing Tom to swear on it is, in legal terms, the closest thing to binding testimony a man of his station could produce. It's a kind of counter-law — private, furious, utterly without power in the world — and that powerlessness is the point.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And what it does to Tom is what undoes Maggie. Tom internalises that oath completely — the injustice done to the Tulliver name becomes his entire moral universe. When Maggie later associates with Philip Wakem — Wakem's son — Tom treats it as an act of treason against the family. He invokes the memory of their father and his suffering as a kind of legal precedent — as though the wrong done to one generation must be maintained as law by the next. The law that crushed Mr. Tulliver gets reproduced inside Tom's character. It becomes a private tyranny — and Maggie is its first casualty.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to press on one more detail about Wakem's purchase of the mill — because it's so structurally elegant and so appalling. Tulliver himself stays on as the paid manager of what was his own mill. Eliot understood — perhaps more clearly than anyone writing in English in that century — that the violence of capitalism is not spectacular. It is administrative.

Class, Property, and Shame: Reading the Mill's Collapse (discussion)

Host (host)
Welcome back to The Mill on the Floss Unpacked. I'm your host, and today we're turning to one of the novel's great slow-motion catastrophes — the ruin of the Tulliver family. The loss of Dorlcote Mill isn't just a plot event — it is the engine that drives everything Maggie and Tom become. Joining me today are three exceptional readers of this novel. Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads novels as anatomies of class power — and the Tulliver bankruptcy is, for him, a case study in how institutions crush the vulnerable. Prof. James Blackstone is a legal and social historian — he'll tell us what a lawsuit like Tulliver's actually meant in the early nineteenth century, and who it benefited. And Ms. Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who has lived with this novel for decades and cares deeply about what it does to you on the page. Daniel, let's begin with you — because before we even get to the bankruptcy, there's something to be said about why Tulliver goes to law in the first place.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Yes — and that's exactly the right place to start, because Tulliver's lawsuit is not an irrational act, even though almost everyone in the novel treats it as one. He is defending a use-right — the water flow to his mill — against the encroachments of a more capitalised neighbour. That is a recognisable class conflict: the smaller, older form of property versus the newer, more aggressive form. What the novel is ruthless about is showing how the law itself is not a neutral arbiter — it is expensive, slow, and ultimately serves those who can afford to keep litigating. Tulliver cannot afford it. And that is not a personal failing — it is a structural fact. George Eliot is precise about this — she gives us a man who is proud, yes, and stubborn, yes — but who is also simply outgunned by an adversary with deeper pockets and better lawyers. The tragedy is that Tulliver interprets this as a personal defeat rather than a systemic one — and that misreading destroys his family.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Daniel is absolutely right about the legal economics, and it's worth being specific about what 'going to law' actually meant for a man of Tulliver's standing in this period. Chancery and common law suits in early-nineteenth-century England could drag on for years and cost sums that would dwarf the original property in dispute. The irony — and it is a bitter one — is that Tulliver actually employs a lawyer, Wakem, who subsequently becomes his mortgagee and effectively his landlord. The instrument of your legal defence becomes the instrument of your dispossession. That is not coincidence — that is how the system worked. Eliot understood this with extraordinary precision. She grew up in that world — her father was an estate agent who had seen exactly these mechanisms at work, families ground down by debt and legal obligation. So when Tulliver says, in that terrible moment of defeat, that he always told his wife he'd been too good-natured in trusting to other people — it reads as both self-delusion and a genuine observation about vulnerability.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me — and it's never quite left me since the first time I read it — is the scene where the household furniture is inventoried for sale. Eliot slows right down there. She lists the objects. The decanters, Mrs Tulliver's linen, the things she brought to her marriage — they become unbearable precisely because they are so ordinary. And Mrs Tulliver's grief over those objects is often played for mild comedy — she fusses, she repeats herself — but Eliot never lets you dismiss it. There is a line where she says the Bible and the large books 'ought to be' hers — that formulation, 'ought to be,' does a great deal of work. It is the language of moral entitlement speaking against legal dispossession. Eliot gives Mrs Tulliver the same dignity she gives Tulliver himself — the dignity of a person who has lost something real, not just symbolic.
Host (host)
That phrase — 'ought to be' — is wonderful, Caroline, because it marks exactly where property law and human feeling come apart. And Daniel, you've said the Tullivers misread their defeat as personal — but what about Tom? Because his response to the bankruptcy is, in some ways, more troubling than his father's.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Tom's response is troubling precisely because it is so legible — so thoroughly shaped by the ideology of his class position. He internalises the shame of the bankruptcy as a debt of honour — a personal obligation to be discharged through sheer will and self-denial. There is something almost admirable about his tenacity — Eliot is too honest to make it simply contemptible. But what he cannot do — what his entire formation makes it impossible for him to do — is ask why his father's mill was taken in the first place. The system that produced the bankruptcy is precisely the system Tom works himself half to death to re-enter. He will redeem the family name by becoming a successful man of business — which means becoming, in miniature, exactly what destroyed his father.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That's a sharp point, and it connects to something specific about the Dodson aunts that I think gets underread. The Dodsons are the novel's walking catechism of respectability — but their respectability is entirely organised around the proper transmission of property. Their linen, their silver, their carefully stipulated wills — all of it is a system for keeping things within the family network and out of the hands of creditors or unworthy in-laws. When Tulliver fails, the Dodsons are not simply cold — they are operating according to a perfectly coherent set of rules, rules that the market itself has written for people in their position. Eliot satirises them wonderfully — but she also shows that their caution is not merely moral smallness. It is rational self-preservation.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I love James's point about the Dodsons, because it touches on something Eliot is doing that is formally very risky — she keeps the comedy and the tragedy running simultaneously. When the family gathers after the bankruptcy — that great assembly of aunts and uncles who come to survey the damage — the comedy of their disagreements is exquisite. And then Eliot gives us that moment — one of her great cruel ironies — where Tulliver's Bible entry becomes the vessel for his hatred of Wakem. He writes his curse into the family Bible — the holiest book of family transmission — and makes Tom swear on it. That is the moment where the whole novel's emotional inheritance — what is passed down, what is owed — becomes permanently poisoned.
Host (host)
That Bible moment is astonishing — a sacred object turned into an instrument of vengeance and filial obligation all at once. We'll come back to Maggie's position in all of this — but before we do, I want to stay a moment longer on the question of shame. Because shame is doing enormous work in this novel — and it falls very differently on different members of the family. Daniel, you've touched on Tom's version of it — but what does shame look like from a structural reading of the novel?
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Shame, in this novel, is the subjective face of class injury. It is what the dispossessed person feels instead of anger — which is the more threatening emotion, politically speaking. Tom converts his family's humiliation into a project of restoration — which sounds noble, but it means he never questions the system. Tulliver himself, on the other hand, does feel something like rage — but it is entirely personalised. It is all Wakem. The great Marxist insight about the novel is that it gives its characters almost no language to describe systemic injury — they can only feel it as personal betrayal, personal failure, personal curse. Eliot understood this — I think she understood it better than she could fully articulate within the novel's form.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd add a specific legal dimension to the shame question — because losing a mill in this period was not merely a financial event. For a family like the Tullivers, the mill was the physical inscription of their place in the community — who they were in relation to their neighbours going back generations. When it is lost and then — in that almost unbearable irony — when Tulliver is kept on as manager under Wakem, he is made to enact his own subordination daily. That arrangement — working for the man who holds your mortgage — was not unusual. It was one of the standard instruments of rural debt peonage. Eliot's readers in 1860 would have recognised it immediately. It was not melodrama — it was reportage.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
James's point about daily enactment — that is exactly where Eliot's greatness as a novelist shows. She makes us feel the grind of it. Not in a single dramatic confrontation, but in the accumulation of small, corrosive moments. Tulliver's hatred of Wakem is, on one level, almost comic in its fixedness — it is the single note he cannot stop playing. But when he finally attacks Wakem on the road — that scene is genuinely shocking, because Eliot has made you understand how impossible it was to hold it in any longer. And then — he has a second stroke. He dies not long after. His rage literally kills him. It is the most devastating convergence of the emotional and the structural in the whole novel.

Host (host)

What she gets right is the peculiar mixture of absolute knowledge and absolute cruelty.
