Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me, coming back to this novel, is how immediately Dickens establishes his central irony — that money and rubbish are, in his world, the same substance. The dust heaps are the source of the Boffin fortune, and Dickens never lets you forget it. Look at how he introduces Nicodemus Boffin — the language is doing so much work at once. "The minion of fortune and the worm of the hour, or in less cutting language, Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, the Golden Dustman." That parenthetical — "or in less cutting language" — is Dickens winking at his own narrator. He's saying: I know exactly how savage this description is, and I'm choosing it deliberately. The narration already has a double consciousness — satirical and fond at the same time.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take your point about the narration, Eleanor, though I'd enter a small caveat. The fondness you identify is not merely authorial strategy — it reflects something genuine about Boffin's moral situation. Here is a man of essentially good nature who has been thrust, without preparation, into a position of grotesque excess. The image Dickens reaches for is perfectly chosen. "Like an eminently aristocratic family cheese, it was much too large for his wants, and bred an infinite amount of parasites." The aristocracy reduced to an overripe cheese — and the parasites who live off it. It is devastating, and it is also, I think, entirely just. Boffin is the novel's moral centre — not because he is clever, but because he is genuinely kind.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I keep returning to is what happens when Bella is alone in her room after all of this. Because Dickens gives her a scene of genuine self-interrogation — and it's both funny and quietly devastating. She goes to the looking-glass — her habitual tribunal — and she puts herself on trial. Now, too, was he cursed with a state of mind more wearing and more wearisome than remorse. He had no remorse; but the evildoer who can hold that avenger at bay, cannot escape the slower torture of incessantly doing the evil deed again and doing it more efficiently. In the defensive declarations and pretended confessions of murderers, the pursuing shadow of this torture may be traced through every lie they tell. If I had done it as alleged, is it conceivable that I would have made this and this mistake? If I had done it as alleged, should I have left that unguarded place which that false and wicked witness against me so infamously deposed to? The state of that wretch who continually finds the weak spots in his own crime, and strives to strengthen them when it is unchangeable, is a state that aggravates the offence by doing the deed a thousand times instead of once; but it is a state, too, that tauntingly visits the offence upon a sullen unrepentant nature with its heaviest punishment every time.
The narrator explores the psychological torment of murderers without remorse: they are compelled to endlessly re-enact and mentally perfect their crime, creating a torture worse than remorse itself.
