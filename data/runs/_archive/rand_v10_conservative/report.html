Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to the opening of Bleak House is that Dickens doesn't begin with a character or a story. He begins with a condition — a weather system that is also a moral atmosphere. The famous opening — 'Fog everywhere' — is not description. 'Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping.' That's incantation — it's a spell being cast over the reader before a single human being has appeared on the page. Dickens is teaching you how to read everything that follows — through atmosphere, through accumulation, through the weight of a repeated word. And that word is fog — which will never entirely lift.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is quite right that it is incantatory — though I'd resist the temptation to make the fog do too much ideological work. Dickens was a great rhetorician before he was a social reformer, and the fog is first and foremost a rhetorical instrument. What it creates is a world in which everything is obscured — including the moral truth about every character we are about to meet. Consider how it settles over Chancery — Dickens is quite specific about that. 'At the very heart of the fog, sits the Lord High Chancellor in his High Court of Chancery.' The fog is not capitalism — it is the human capacity for self-deception, dressed in legal robes. That is what Dickens understood with a clarity Dr. Johnson would have admired.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I love about that opening — and I've read it aloud more times than I can count — is that it genuinely disorients you. You don't know where you are in time. You don't know whose voice this is. The fog is inside the prose itself. And then — just when you think you've found a foothold — Dickens introduces a second narrator, Esther Summerson, and she sounds utterly different. Esther is hesitant, warm, self-deprecating — the fog becomes something she is moving through rather than something she embodies. The duality of the narration is actually the most thrilling structural gamble in Victorian fiction, and I think it still surprises readers who come to it fresh. You keep asking — whose story is this really?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline has put her finger on something I find technically astonishing — the decision to split the narration is not just structural, it's epistemological. The third-person narrator knows everything and judges freely — sardonic, omniscient, sometimes furious. Esther knows only what she has lived, and she mistrusts even that. Think about what Dickens does when characters like Lady Dedlock are carrying secrets they haven't yet disclosed. He gives us the third-person voice — which can say, with perfect calm, that Lady Dedlock 'is now decided, and prepared to throw it off.' We feel the weight of that secret — without yet being told what it is. That is the fog working at the level of character — and it's breathtaking craft.
