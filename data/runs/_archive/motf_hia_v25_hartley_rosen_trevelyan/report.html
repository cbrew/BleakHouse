And she gives him the caterpillars. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately about these early chapters is how Eliot refuses to sentimentalise childhood even as she clearly adores her subject. Look at what she does with the jealousy sequence involving Lucy. She sets up the narrator's voice to deliver something almost shocking about a little girl. "Maggie would have thought a little while ago that she could never be cross with pretty little Lucy, any more than she could be cruel to a little white mouse." That simile — the white mouse — does so much work in three words. It's delicate, it's helpless, it's almost absurdly small as a measure of cruelty — and yet the next sentence tells us Maggie is actively contemplating slapping and pinching Lucy. Eliot lets Maggie be genuinely, specifically unkind in her thoughts — and then immediately explains exactly why, without excusing it. That combination — moral precision with total sympathy — is the hallmark of the narration throughout.

The gypsy sequence is one of the great comic set pieces in Victorian fiction — and it pivots on a single dazzling moment of terror. Maggie arrives expecting to be received as an intellectual queen — she has genuinely calculated that the gypsies will be grateful for her superior knowledge. And then the fantasy curdles at terrifying speed. Eliot writes — and this is one of the most perfectly timed sentences she ever composed — that Maggie had begun to think "they meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking." "Gradual cooking." The bureaucratic specificity of that phrase — not just killed, not just cooked, but gradually cooked — is almost unbearable in its comic perfection. Eliot understands that genuine childhood terror operates exactly like that — with methodical, logical detail that makes it worse.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to sit with the resolution Maggie makes before she runs — because this is where Eliot shows us the full engine of Maggie's imagination. After Tom rejects her, Eliot tells us — and I love this formulation — that "Maggie's intentions, as usual, were on a larger scale than Tom imagined." That phrase — "as usual" — carries a whole biography of disappointment in two words. Tom's horizon is always smaller than Maggie's. He can reject the gypsy scheme because he has a world to inherit — a mill, a name, eventually a place in the commercial order of St Ogg's. Maggie has no such place — so her imagination has to build a world where she might be received, valued, respected. Even her plan to send her father a secret letter by a small gypsy is heartbreaking in its impossible logistical detail.
