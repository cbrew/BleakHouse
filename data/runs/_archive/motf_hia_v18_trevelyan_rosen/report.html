Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
It is one of the great comic set-ups in Victorian fiction — and George Eliot knows exactly what she is doing. The narrator gives us this magnificent opening sentence: Maggie's intentions, as usual, were on a larger scale than Tom imagined. "Maggie's intentions, as usual, were on a larger scale than Tom imagined." That "as usual" is doing extraordinary work — it is a tiny aside that tells you everything about this child and everyone around her. When I read aloud the passage where she resolves to send her father a letter via a small gypsy — who would "run away without telling where she was" — I can never quite keep a straight face. It is comedy, yes — but it is also completely serious to Maggie in that moment, and that doubleness is where the genius lives.

Daniel Rosen (social_historian)
I want to hold onto something Caroline said — about the words used to describe Maggie: gypsy, half wild. These are not neutral descriptors — they are the language a provincial bourgeois community uses to mark a child as not quite fitting, not quite manageable. What's devastating is that Maggie internalises it — she doesn't resist the label, she runs toward it. And then look at what happens when she actually reaches the gypsies — the fantasy collapses into something closer to gothic horror. The narrator tells us that she had begun to think — and this is extraordinary — "she had begun to think that they meant perhaps to kill her as soon as it was dark, and cut up her body for gradual cooking." "Gradual cooking." George Eliot is writing comedy of absolute precision there — but the fear underneath it is real. The place Maggie ran to for freedom turns out to be another place that terrifies her — which is, I'd argue, the novel's central mechanism in miniature.

Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
"Gradual cooking" — I have read that line out loud to audiences and waited for the laugh, and it always comes, and then there's a little catch of unease immediately after. What Daniel is pointing to is true — but I think Eliot is also doing something specifically theological in that passage. That is a child's theology — but it is also a child's privacy, a child's desperate inner life asserting itself even in the face of mortal terror.

I'd push on that a bit — because I think there's something structurally important happening in that scene, beyond the psychology. Strong feelings is putting it mildly — I think the Tom-at-school chapters are among the funniest and most painful things Eliot ever wrote. That's exactly right — and what Eliot shows is that Tom's certainty is not incidental to his social position, it is constituted by it. The man who inherits the right to judge does not need to examine his judgments. And Eliot connects this, I think, directly to the novel's great passage on the man of maxims — in Chapter 55.
