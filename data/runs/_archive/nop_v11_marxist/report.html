James Blackstone (social_historian)
And to understand what Dickens was aiming at, you need to know what the Court of Chancery actually was in 1852. It was a court of equity — which sounds benevolent, almost utopian. In theory it existed to provide remedies where common law was too rigid — to do what was fair, rather than merely what was legal. In practice it had become a system for generating costs — fees for solicitors, fees for clerks, fees for copying documents that need never have been copied — while cases dragged on for decades. There were real cases — Jennens v Jennens being the most famous — that ran for over a century and consumed entire family fortunes. Dickens was not exaggerating. He was, if anything, being polite.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Jo is, in the most precise sense, a product of the system Dickens is anatomizing. He sweeps a crossing in the shadow of the Court of Chancery, which is itself a machine for consuming resources and producing nothing. And Jo's function — his only sanctioned social function — is equally futile: he sweeps mud off a crossing so the same mud returns tomorrow. When the narrator describes Jo in Chapter Sixteen, the famous 'Move on' chapter, the language is devastating. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That chapter is one of the most legally precise passages in Victorian fiction, even if it reads like pure rhetoric. 'Move on' was not merely a policeman's habit — it was the operational logic of the Vagrancy Acts. If you were a child without settlement — without a parish that claimed you — you had no legal right to be anywhere. The Poor Law of 1834 was built on the concept of 'settlement': you could only receive relief in the parish where you were legally domiciled. Jo has no settlement — we're never told where he was born, who his parents were, what parish might claim him. That's not a biographical detail Dickens forgot to supply; it's the entire point — Jo is legally unlocatable. And Dickens captures this with that extraordinary repeated command — 'move on' — which is in fact the full extent of the state's engagement with Jo. 'Move on, Jo. It's always the same, Jo, move on.' James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the crime was entirely legible to contemporaries — that's what I'd want people to understand. Dickens was serializing Bleak House in 1852 and 1853, in the immediate wake of several Parliamentary reports on child mortality in London's slums. Readers of Household Words would have known that boys like Jo — crossing-sweepers, mudlarks, rag-pickers — were dying of typhus and cholera at extraordinary rates. Jo dies of disease — almost certainly something related to smallpox or typhus — contracted in the conditions Tom-all-Alone's itself creates. And Dickens is ferocious about the fact that the disease doesn't stay in the slum. 'There is not a drop of Tom's corrupted blood but propagates infection and contagion somewhere.' Verified (67%) It's epidemiology as moral argument — the body politic infects itself by ignoring its own wound. And Jo, before he dies, passes his infection on to Esther — literally bridging the social gulf the novel's dual narrative tracks.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That direct address is where I'd say Dickens exceeds the limits of his own politics. He was not, in any formal sense, a radical — he didn't believe in structural transformation, he believed in individual conscience. But what he creates in Jo — as character, as symbol, as accusation — goes beyond individual conscience. Jo forces the reader to ask not 'how can I help this boy?' but 'what kind of world produces this boy?' That is a systemic question — and it is, whether Dickens knew it or not, a revolutionary question.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd put it slightly differently — and I think this is where the historian's perspective cuts against the literary critic's. Dickens was asking systemic questions — but the answers he imagined were still personal: better individuals, better philanthropists, better hearts. The irony — and it's a deep one — is that the novel itself satirizes every form of institutionalized charity. Mrs. Jellyby's telescopic philanthropy, Mrs. Pardiggle's aggressive visitations — all of it is shown to be useless at best, harmful at worst. So Dickens demolishes both the structural response and the personal response — and leaves us with Jo's death and no clear exit. Which is, I'd argue, a more honest response to the situation than most political programmes of the era managed.

Daniel is entirely right about the institutional logic — and the historical record bears it out with uncomfortable precision. The Court of Chancery in the 1840s and 50s had suits that had run for generations. Some estates were genuinely consumed. Dickens was not exaggerating for effect. He was, if anything, restrained. But what I find most remarkable is how Dickens handles Lady Dedlock's end. She is found dead, dressed in the clothes of Jenny the brickmaker's wife — the woman whose dead child she once clasped in grief. The aristocrat dies in the costume of the poor. That is not sentiment. That is structural irony of a very high order. Dickens writes that Esther, seeing her mother fallen at the gate, recognises that she has been — and this is the phrase — "cold and dead" — the lady who had been cold in life, is cold now in death, and the two coldnesses are one.
