Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Both of you are right about the warmth — but I'd ask us to hold that warmth for a moment against where it sits in the novel's architecture. That line about the mirrors is what stops me — 'the mirrors accustomed to reflect them when they were a portion of herself.' Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What you're both circling, I think, is how this novel maps interiority onto interior space. And the Smallweed family gives us the inverse image of all of this. No warmth, no lavender in the drawers, no surprised birds on the walls. Dickens tells us the Smallweed family 'discarded all amusements, discountenanced all story-books, fairy-tales, fictions, and fables, and banished all levities whatsoever.' This isn't eccentricity — it's a programme. The elimination of imagination as a strategy for producing useful, extractable human beings. And the result — Dickens is merciless here — is this: 'The complete little men and women whom it has produced have been observed to bear a likeness to old monkeys with something depressing on their minds.' 'Old monkeys with something depressing on their minds' — Dickens is funny and furious at the same moment, which is exactly where his genius lives.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What moves me about that passage is the texture of the generosity. Jarndyce says outright: I have no power over you whatever. He's releasing them before they've even tried to leave. But then comes the harder counsel, the one Richard will need and ignore. "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner." "Constancy in love is a good thing, but it means nothing, and is nothing, without constancy in every kind of effort." That word — constancy — becomes the tragic key to everything that follows. Richard loves Ada with complete sincerity. That's never in doubt. What he cannot do — what he simply cannot do — is extend that love into the patient daily labour of being.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to sit with Jarndyce's counsel for a moment before we follow Richard's failure, because I think it contains something important. He tells Richard: if you entertain the supposition that success can be wrested from Fortune by fits and starts — leave that wrong idea here, or leave Ada here. That's Dickens ventriloquizing a Victorian ethic of steady meritocratic effort — the idea that earnest labour will be rewarded. But the brutal irony is that Chancery is specifically designed to defeat exactly that ethic. Richard's problem isn't laziness in any simple sense. The court has already infected his imagination. Why labour steadily at medicine or law when a single verdict might transform your life overnight?

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel's right — and you see it nakedly in the scene with Mrs. Badger, which is one of Dickens's most quietly devastating comic turns. Mrs. Badger is technically praising her late husbands at the time, but she slips in this clinical verdict on Richard — and she is absolutely correct. She says Richard lacks the positive interest in medicine that makes it a vocation. "Young men like Mr. Allan Woodcourt who take it from a strong interest in all that it can do will find some reward in it through a great deal of work for a very little money and through years of considerable endurance and disappointment." Notice the structural genius there — Woodcourt is placed right beside Richard as his foil. Same profession, entirely different relationship to it. One is fuelled by genuine calling; the other is merely passing through.

He writes that every dim little star revolving about her — from her maid to the manager of the Italian Opera — knows her weaknesses. And the trades people — the jewellers, the mercers, Mr. Sladdery the librarian — they are the real managers of the class. The image Dickens reaches for is extraordinary — let me read it exactly. "who, in hooking one, hook all and bear them off as Lemuel Gulliver bore away the stately fleet of the majestic Lilliput." The aristocracy as Lilliputian ships — towed by the trades they patronise, not the other way round. That's not satire for its own sake. That is a structural argument about how class actually reproduces itself.
