Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Dickens does in those opening pages is essentially illegal by every convention of the Victorian novel. He gives us no character, no story, no promise of comfort — just this great suffocating atmosphere pressing down on the page. And the fog isn't decoration — it is the argument. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is absolutely right that the fog is doing argumentative work, and it helps to know what Chancery actually was when Dickens wrote this. By the eighteen-fifties, the Court of Chancery had become a machine for converting human hope into legal fees, with no discernible output at the other end. Cases routinely outlasted the people who brought them. Jarndyce and Jarndyce, Dickens' central suit, was modelled on real cases — one had been running for decades before he put pen to paper. So when Dickens opens in fog, there's a precision to the metaphor that his original readers would have felt in their bones. This wasn't a satirical exaggeration — it was barely a metaphor at all.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me, every single time I read those opening pages aloud, is the rhythm — the way the sentences themselves enact the fog. They accumulate without arriving. Each clause adds another layer of murk rather than any clarification — and you're three pages in before you've quite noticed you've been held in suspension. And then into that fog walks Tulkinghorn, and the fog becomes a man. Listen to how Dickens describes him moving between his two worlds — Chesney Wold and Lincoln's Inn — as if geography simply does not apply to him.
