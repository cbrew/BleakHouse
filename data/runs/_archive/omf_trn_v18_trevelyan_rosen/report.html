Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked — a close, passionate, and occasionally contentious journey through one of the great novels of the English language. Our Mutual Friend was Charles Dickens's last completed novel, published in 1864 and 1865, and it is — by any serious measure — among his most extraordinary achievements. It is a novel obsessed with money: where it comes from, what it does to people, and what people will do to get it. It is a novel about dust — literal dust heaps that constitute a vast fortune — and about resurrection: bodies pulled from the Thames, identities shed and reclaimed. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me most forcefully about this novel — from the very first pages — is that its central fortune is built on filth. The dust heaps are refuse, waste, the discarded matter of a city — and yet they represent fabulous wealth. Dickens is telling us, right from the outset, exactly where capital comes from. And what's remarkable is that John Harmon — the heir to all this — already knows it, already feels it, before he even claims the inheritance. There is a passage in which Harmon, in his disguise, works through his own moral situation — and it is devastating in its clarity. He says: "I came back, shrinking from my father's money, shrinking from my father's memory, mistrustful of being forced on a mercenary wife." Every repetition of that word — shrinking — is doing enormous work. He is not simply reluctant to inherit — he is physically recoiling from what the money represents.

That passage is extraordinary to read aloud — the rhythm of it is almost liturgical. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I love about that Wegg passage — and what I think Daniel's reading of Harmon deepens — is the contrast Dickens holds in tension throughout this novel. Money destroys Harmon's inner life before he has even touched it. And money reduces Wegg — already a figure of comic degradation — to a state of physical helplessness. It's the same force, operating at different registers — tragic in one key, farcical in another. And then there's the passage with Wegg at the keyhole — which I find genuinely unsettling. Dickens writes that Wegg "laboured with a will at turning an imaginary grindstone outside the keyhole, while Mr Boffin stooped at it within." Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Caroline is exactly right, and I want to press that point further. Wegg is a blackmailer — a man with no capital except information — and the keyhole scene shows us class power running, briefly, in reverse. The rich man stoops; the poor man grinds. But Dickens makes it grotesque and comic rather than triumphant — which tells us something about the limits of his radicalism. The system is not overturned — it is briefly mocked, and then Wegg stumps home. What stays with me, though, is the figure of Eugene Wrayburn — a man of privilege so complete it has become a kind of paralysis. There is a passage in which the wretched figure of Jenny Wren's father shuffles past Eugene — and Dickens describes him as: "The miserable man, feebly rubbing the back of his faltering hands downward from the wrists, shuffled on to his post of disgrace." And Eugene barely notices him — "instinctively falling away from the disagreeable contact" — before lighting his cigar and departing. Poverty, in this novel, is something the comfortable classes fall away from — reflexively, without thought.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that loyalty — the Boffins' loyalty to young John against his own father — is what makes the inheritance so morally loaded. They didn't expect a penny of it. And when it falls to them anyway — because the heir is apparently dead — Mrs Boffin's first instinct isn't acquisition. It's to find an orphan child and rename him after the boy they lost. "Now that he is past all benefit of the money, and it's come to us, I should like to find some orphan child, and take the boy and adopt him and give him John's name, and provide for him." There is something almost liturgical in that impulse. The money cannot resurrect John Harmon, so you redeem it by giving a different child his name and his future. The Boffins are doing with capital what capital almost never does: turning it back toward the people it took everything from.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Riderhood's speech reveals is that the whole logic of disguise in this novel is class performance. Headstone dresses down — puts on the bargeman's clothes — to move through a world below his station and commit violence without accountability. Harmon dresses down as Rokesmith — the meek secretary — to observe the world he's entitled to inherit. In both cases, the disguise is a way of escaping the obligations that come with your assigned place in the class structure. The difference is that Harmon uses his freedom to fall in love. Headstone uses his to try to commit murder.
