Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Thank you — and I want to start with something that looks like pure Dickensian farce but is actually doing something quite serious. We have Sloppy — this large, ungainly young man who has been given a new black suit by the benevolent Mrs Boffin. The tailor has been instructed — with great seriousness, by Rokesmith himself — to conceal Sloppy's buttons. The system is deployed in full: the cunning of tailoring art, personal directions, the utmost resources of the craft. And it fails completely — because Sloppy's body simply refuses to be dressed up as something it is not. Listen to what Dickens does with that failure: "he now stood before the Council, a perfect Argus in the way of buttons: shining and winking and gleaming and twinkling out of a hundred of those eyes of bright metal, at the dazzled spectators." A hundred eyes of bright metal — it's comic, yes, but it is also the body of the poor exposing itself despite every institutional effort to cover it up. The novel keeps doing this: the charity dresses the poor in respectability, and the poor keep bursting out of it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
That image rewards a bit of historical grounding, I think. The black suit of mourning had a very specific class valence in mid-Victorian England — it was the costume of respectability, of people who observed the proper rituals of grief and social station. Dressing Sloppy in it is an act of social translation — the Boffins are trying to move him up, or at least make him legible to the middle-class world they now inhabit. But what Daniel is pointing at is precisely the limit of that project — the body won't cooperate. And Dickens, to his credit, makes that funny rather than mournful — which is its own kind of commentary on the whole charade.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That distinction matters enormously — because the novel also contains cruelty dressed up as comedy, and you have to know the difference. What saves the Sloppy scene is that the system is the joke, not Sloppy himself. And that becomes the template for the novel's deepest moral argument — which I think crystallises most powerfully when we come to Lizzie Hexam. Here is a woman at the very bottom of the social order, in love with a man — Eugene Wrayburn — who represents everything that system values and she does not. And she says something that I find genuinely startling in its selflessness — and also in what it reveals about the system that has shaped her. "I love him so much, and so dearly, that when I sometimes think my life may be but a weary one, I am proud of it and glad of it. I am proud and glad to suffer something for him, even though it is of no service to him, and he will never know of it or care for it." The poor woman has so thoroughly internalised her own expendability that she calls it pride. That is what the system produces — and Dickens is at least honest enough to let us see it.

And then there is the sheer gothic horror of what the money has already done — before any of this moral reckoning even begins. The passage where John Harmon — or the man we come to know as Rokesmith — discovers what has happened to his own supposed corpse is extraordinary. And then comes the realisation — and I want to read this exactly as Dickens wrote it, because the rhythm carries the horror: "I perceived that Radfoot had been murdered by some unknown hands for the money for which he would have murdered me, and that probably we had both been shot into the river from the same dark place into the same dark tide, when the stream ran deep and strong." Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Yes, and the passage that establishes this is one of the most quietly radical moments in the novel. Dickens is describing the Boffins in a moment of unusual candour — calling them, flat out, 'ignorant and unpolished.' But then he pivots immediately — and this is what matters — to their moral life. He says they have been guided, all their lives, by — and I want to read this carefully — 'a religious sense of duty and desire to do right.' Now, Dickens is making a claim here that would have stung his readers: that education and polish are entirely beside the point when it comes to moral worth. The class system told you that refinement was virtue. Dickens says: look at the Boffins. And then he ends with a line that operates almost like a theorem — a law of moral physics. 'For, Evil often stops short at itself and dies with the doer of it; but Good, never.' That 'never' is doing an enormous amount of work. It's almost defiant.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The test arrives very quickly, and it takes the form of the post. The moment Boffin's fortune becomes public knowledge, he is deluged — and the word isn't too strong — with requests for money. What Dickens anatomises here is the entire machinery of Victorian charity — or rather, the parasitic relationship that called itself charity. The Secretary opens these letters, and Dickens gives us this extraordinary satirical inventory. 'Fifty-seven churches to be erected with half-crowns, forty-two parsonage houses to be repaired with shillings, seven-and-twenty organs to be built with halfpence.' The comedy is in the absurd specificity — but beneath it is a real and accurate picture of how Victorian philanthropy operated. Newly wealthy men were systematically targeted by charitable organisations that were, in many cases, essentially patronage networks for the upper classes. And then there is the duke's letter, which is the gem of the passage.

And we should be clear about what Gaffer is doing, legally speaking. Thames mudlarks and river-finders in the mid-nineteenth century occupied a genuine — if precarious — legal niche. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I find most searching — most radical — is the passage James didn't get to, the train crossing the river. Dickens gives us this extraordinary image of industrial modernity obliterating everything in its path. Here is the sentence — and it is worth every word: A bomb-shell.
