Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The first thing you notice, when you actually read the opening of this novel aloud, is the dreaming quality of it. Eliot opens with a narrator watching the river — watching the mill wheel turning — and it feels like memory before it feels like story. Eliot has this gift — and it's almost unfair — of making you feel you've always known a character, within about three sentences. With Maggie, that feeling arrives almost immediately. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one has to be precise about the nature of Maggie's hunger, because Eliot is. It is not primarily a social grievance — it is a spiritual and intellectual hunger. Maggie wants to be loved, yes — passionately, extravagantly loved. But she also wants to understand. She wants the books in her father's study, she wants Philip Wakem to talk to her about music and painting, she wants a larger life than St Ogg's can offer. Eliot is quite careful to show us that the deprivation is not merely material — it is a deprivation of the spirit. Thomas à Kempis, when Maggie discovers him in the third book, is not simply a coping mechanism. It is a genuine philosophical response to the question of what a person does when the world refuses to give them what they need. Whether Eliot endorses that response is, of course, another matter entirely.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Edmund is right about the à Kempis moment — it's one of the most quietly devastating things in the novel. But I want to go back to the child Maggie for a moment, because there's something Eliot does in those early chapters that I find quite astonishing from a craft perspective. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Miss Woodcourt has identified the crux of it, I think. And it connects to something Dr Johnson understood very well — that literature's highest function is to enlarge our sympathies. What Eliot does in the childhood books is not merely charming. She is building the moral architecture for everything that follows. Every eccentricity of Maggie's character that will later cause her harm is also, Eliot shows us, evidence of her remarkable nature. The passionate attachments, the impulsiveness, the need for more than St Ogg's can give — these are not flaws to be corrected. They are the signs of a soul.

Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
The first thing you notice when you sit down to read this novel aloud — really aloud, performing it — is that Eliot has an absolutely lethal comic ear. The Dodson sisters alone would sustain a lesser writer's entire career. Mrs. Glegg, Mrs. Pullet, Mrs. Deane — they are a complete taxonomy of provincial respectability and wounded vanity. And Eliot loves them. That's what makes the comedy so rich — there's no contempt in it. Think of the scene where Mrs. Pullet weeps over her new bonnet — the bonnet she hasn't worn yet, the bonnet that is being saved, essentially, for a grander occasion than life will likely provide. That is a perfectly engineered comic set-piece, but it also tells you everything about a certain kind of life — a life organised around appearances and the fear of losing status.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is absolutely right about the generosity, and I think that is the key word. Eliot never writes about the Dodsons from above. She writes from inside their world — inside their logic — which is why the comedy never curdles into satire. The Dodsons have a code, a genuine moral code, however narrow it may be — and Eliot respects that narrowness as a form of seriousness. She tells us, in her own voice, that the Dodsons' way of life had a dignity and a consistency that we might do well not to dismiss. I find that authorial candour rather bracing in an age when provincial life is so easily mocked.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right, though I would frame it rather differently. The Mill on the Floss is, at its centre, a novel about the relationship between character and circumstance — and what Eliot insists upon, rather unfashionably, is that character is fate. She takes the phrase almost directly from Novalis — but she means something very specific by it. Maggie's doom is not imposed from outside. It flows from who she is — from her passionate nature, her hunger for the ideal, her inability to endure ordinary life. Eliot sets this out quite explicitly in the later chapters, when she reflects on the nature of moral struggle. She says of Maggie that she was "not a creature of conventional morality" but that she yearned for "something beyond the daily round" — that the conflict between duty and longing was not merely personal but almost cosmic in its weight.
