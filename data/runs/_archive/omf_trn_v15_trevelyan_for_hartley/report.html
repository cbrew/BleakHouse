Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Delighted to be here — and I should say at once that Our Mutual Friend is the Dickens novel I return to most. It has a darkness to it that the earlier books don't quite reach. But it is also — and this matters enormously — enormously funny. The comic writing is some of the finest he ever produced. Take the parade of beggars writing to Boffin after he comes into his fortune. Dickens describes their letters — their so-called documents — and he says, "they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf." Minced veal to a calf. That comparison is so precise, so devastating, and so absolutely delighted with itself. That is Dickens at full stretch.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me about that passage is the layering of it. It's comic, yes — but there's something genuinely bleak underneath. These are desperate people, dressed up in satirical costume. The daughters of general officers — long accustomed to every luxury of life, Dickens says, except spelling. That parenthetical — except spelling — is the kind of thing you re-read three times just for the pleasure of it. But then you think: these women are actually destitute. The joke and the tragedy occupy exactly the same space.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Caroline is right that the satire has a real bite to it — and the historical record bears that out. When a windfall of any kind became publicly known in Victorian England, the letters would arrive within days. The category Dickens calls the beggars of punctual business-habits — those who announce they will be cold in death at a quarter to one on Tuesday — is not pure invention. Threatening suicide in correspondence was a documented strategy of last resort. What Dickens understood — and this novel understands it better than almost any other — is that money creates a gravitational field. And those in its orbit will find any language, any posture, any fiction, to pull themselves closer. That's what Boffin's fortune unleashes — not generosity, but gravity.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Milveys are one of the novel's most quietly radical elements. Dickens describes them as representatives of hundreds of other good Christian pairs, as conscientious and as useful. That's a direct rebuke to those who caricatured Victorian clergy as either hypocrites or bloated sinecurists. The actual working clergy of the urban poor were, in many cases, genuinely exhausted and genuinely committed. What Dickens does here is honour the smallness of their work by insisting on its greatness — that is his exact phrasing. They merge the smallness of their work in its greatness — and feel in no danger of losing dignity when they adapt themselves to incomprehensible humbugs. That phrase — incomprehensible humbugs — is one of the finest things in the book.

There was an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James’s, when not in use, to whom the Veneerings were a source of blind confusion. The name of this article was Twemlow. Being first cousin to Lord Snigsworth, he was in frequent requisition, and at many houses might be said to represent the dining-table in its normal state. Mr and Mrs Veneering, for example, arranging a dinner, habitually started with Twemlow, and then put leaves in him, or added guests to him. Sometimes, the table consisted of Twemlow and half a dozen leaves; sometimes, of Twemlow and a dozen leaves; sometimes, Twemlow was pulled out to his utmost extent of twenty leaves. Mr and Mrs Veneering on occasions of ceremony faced each other in the centre of the board, and thus the parallel still held; for, it always happened that the more Twemlow was pulled out, the further he found himself from the center, and nearer to the sideboard at one end of the room, or the window-curtains at the other.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Twemlow is one of the great comic inventions in English fiction, and the joke is entirely structural. Dickens introduces him not as a person at all, but as an object — 'an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors.' 'an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James's, when not in use.' He is, quite literally, stored when not required by his social superiors. What makes it so precise is the central paradox of his social position. He has genuine aristocratic connection — first cousin to Lord Snigsworth — and that connection is the only thing anyone wants from him. Dickens then develops the metaphor with extraordinary care — the more Twemlow is 'pulled out,' the further he ends up from the centre of things. That is a very precise description of how ornamental figures in society actually function — useful precisely to the degree that they remain marginal.

And the Veneerings are the perfect contrast to Twemlow — they have no inherent social legitimacy whatsoever, but they have money, and in this novel money is trying very hard to become everything else. Which brings us, I think, to the Boffins — because Dickens gives us the opposite experiment. What happens when genuinely good people suddenly acquire enormous wealth? The letters that pour in to Boffin are one of the great satirical set pieces in English fiction — and historically, they are absolutely accurate. Newly wealthy men in Victorian England were besieged by exactly these solicitations — charitable bodies run by the aristocracy, which required wealthy subscribers to lend them social legitimacy while providing most of the money. Dickens catches the mechanism perfectly. There's a letter from the Duke of Linseed — and Dickens describes it with exquisite precision before we even read a word of it. 'Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet.' Five words that tell you everything — the weight of entitlement, the pretence of intimacy, the ducal seal doing the work that substance cannot. And then of course the letter turns out to be lithographed — mass-produced — 'presenting but a pale individuality of an address to Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, in quite another hand.' The Duke hasn't written to him at all.
