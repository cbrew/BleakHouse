Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about these passages is the sheer structural audacity of routing the novel's social satire through the Secretary's letterbox. Dickens doesn't show us the beggars directly — he shows us the paperwork they generate, which is a far more devastating choice. It creates distance, and distance becomes comic, and comedy becomes indictment — all in the same move. And then there's that list — fifty-seven churches, forty-two parsonage houses, twenty-seven organs — each denomination more absurd than the last. Dickens is constructing a kind of satirical arithmetic — adding up the moral bankruptcy of Victorian charity in precise, ludicrous figures. And then — the coup de grâce — that letter from the Duke of Linseed. "Large fat private double letter, sealed with ducal coronet." That word 'fat' is doing everything — it is physically repellent, morally bloated, and wickedly funny all at once.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Having read that passage aloud more than once, I can tell you that list of denominations is practically musical — it has a comic accelerando built right in. But what I love — what I genuinely love — is the postscript on the Duke's letter. Dickens calls it 'thoughtful in the postscript' — and the sarcasm is so straight-faced it takes a half-second to land. That half-second is everything in performance — you let the audience find the joke themselves, and the laugh is twice as good. Then the individual beggars in the next passage — Dickens categorises them with the thoroughness of a naturalist classifying beetles. The 'daughters of general officers, long accustomed to every luxury of life, except spelling' — that parenthesis is a tiny perfect dagger. And then the metaphor for their scraps of paperwork — he sets it up this way: "they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf." Minced veal to a calf — it's revolting, it's exact, and it's somehow completely right.

These are the corporate beggars. But there are, besides, the individual beggars; and how does the heart of the Secretary fail him when he has to cope with them! And they must be coped with to some extent, because they all enclose documents (they call their scraps documents; but they are, as to papers deserving the name, what minced veal is to a calf), the non-return of which would be their ruin. That is to say, they are utterly ruined now, but they would be more utterly ruined then. Among these correspondents are several daughters of general officers, long accustomed to every luxury of life (except spelling), who little thought, when their gallant fathers waged war in the Peninsula, that they would ever have to appeal to those whom Providence, in its inscrutable wisdom, has blessed with untold gold, and from among whom they select the name of Nicodemus Boffin, Esquire, for a maiden effort in this wise, understanding that he has such a heart as never was. The Secretary learns, too, that confidence between man and wife would seem to obtain but rarely when virtue is in distress, so numerous are the wives who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted husbands, who would never permit it; while, on the other hand, so numerous are the husbands who take up their pens to ask Mr Boffin for money without the knowledge of their devoted wives, who would instantly go out of their senses if they had the least suspicion of the circumstance. There are the inspired beggars, too. Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What keeps stopping me in these passages is the grief underneath the comedy. The 'inspired beggars' sitting over a fragment of candle that must soon go out — that image is genuinely desperate. Dickens is funny about them, yes — but the candle guttering in the dark is real poverty, and he doesn't let you forget it. And the suicidal beggars with their precise deadline — 'a quarter to one P.M. on Tuesday' — is farcical, but only just. That precision is the tell — it's the logic of someone who has run out of options and is trying to sound as if they haven't. What I find fascinating is how this connects to the will passage — to what old Harmon actually left behind. Wegg reads it out, and it's devastating in its brevity — here is the passage: "Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown." 'Never made friends.' 'Ever had a rebellious family.' The whole biography of a loveless man in two clauses.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Eugene's account of his father — M. R. F., the Most Respected Father — is one of those passages where Dickens's comedy is doing something structurally profound. The joke is about a father who assigns his children's identities before the children have had the impertinence to be born and develop personalities of their own. And the punchline is always the same — the child arrives, is forced into the designated role, and fails at it in precisely the way the role demanded. Listen to the rhythm of this — Dickens builds it like a joke with three setups before the real sting: That single word — shaky — does everything. It's an entire career collapse compressed into one adjective. And what Dickens is really setting up here is the broader theme of the novel: the tyranny of inherited roles — social, financial, moral.
