What strikes me first about these early passages is how Eliot announces her comic method right from the start. The narrator is performing a kind of mock-survey — as if Maggie's mind were a territory to be mapped by a slightly amused cartographer. James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's worth pausing on what Eliot is quietly telling us about a girl's education in the 1820s and 1830s. Maggie has had one year at a local school in St Ogg's and access to a small shelf of books — that is not a failure of her family's love, that is the ordinary condition. Girls of her class and region simply did not have the access to systematic education that her brother Tom — however badly he fares at it — is given as a matter of course. The comedy Eliot constructs is exquisite, but the social fact underneath it is rather bleak. A child who reads the dictionary because she has so few other books, and who still outthinks the boy receiving a formal classical education — what does that say about the system?

Edmund's point connects beautifully to what happens in the gypsy episode, where that same mixture of acumen and blind dreams leads Maggie into genuine gothic terror. The narrator takes us from comedy to something considerably darker with almost no warning. That contrast becomes even more poignant when you set it against Eliot's great meditation on home and memory in the same chapter. The passage about Tom returning home for the holidays — the light in the parlour, the grate, the fire-irons — it reaches for something very deep. What strikes me immediately about the Glegg episode is the narrative timing — Eliot sets up Mrs Glegg in a state of magnificent self-congratulation. James Blackstone (social_historian)
Tulliver's Wakem is one of the great paranoid portraits in Victorian fiction — and what makes it brilliant is how plausible it is made to seem. Eliot piles up the grievances with that characteristic parenthetical patience: the irrigation, the dam, the right of road, the bridge. Each one individually might be coincidence — together they have the texture of persecution. And then she cuts to the visceral image — listen to the voice here. 'A hook-nosed glib fellow! as cool as a cucumber, — always looking so sure of his game!' That is not the narrator's voice — that is Tulliver's contempt erupting into the prose. For a legal historian, it is also a remarkably precise portrait of what a confident, well-connected attorney could mean to a small-propertied man in this period.

James, that's exactly right — and it connects directly to what happens when we meet Tom at school, which is one of the passages I find most technically astonishing in the whole novel. Eliot describes Tom's humiliation under Stelling's teaching, and she does something genuinely counterintuitive: she shows his suffering by making him more like a girl. That's already a provocation — but then she follows it with this piercing portrait of a boy trying to pray his way out of educational failure. I'd push back ever so slightly on that reading of rigidity. Eliot is very careful to say that Tom had no brute rebellion in his nature — the human sensibilities predominated. What he lacks is not moral seriousness but imaginative latitude — he cannot conceive of solutions not already given to him by the world he knows. That is a profoundly Burkean failing: the man who cannot learn from precedent he has never been shown. And then Eliot shows us the cost to Maggie — immediately, viscerally — in a passage that I think is among the most psychologically precise she ever wrote. Maggie goes upstairs — and the narration tracks what happens inside her with extraordinary granularity. And historically, that sentence is a précis of what actually happened to thousands of men in Tulliver's position in the first half of the nineteenth century. What strikes me first is the choreography of it — the physical hesitation before the emotional one. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right about the craft — but I want to dwell on what the moment is actually about morally. Mr Tulliver is not a good man in any simple sense — he is proud, litigious, often a fool of his own passions. And yet Eliot gives him this — a moment of pure, unguarded love. He turns his horse and rides slowly back, and then speaks aloud — to no one, to himself, to the empty lane. 'Poor little wench! she'll have nobody but Tom, belike, when I'm gone.' That word — 'belike' — is old English, tentative, almost tender in its uncertainty. He does not say Tom will care for her — only that she will have Tom, and that this is perhaps not enough. This is what great moral fiction does — it redeems a flawed character not by correcting him, but by showing us the love he carries despite himself.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund's reading is generous, and I share it — but I can't let pass what Tulliver's love actually reveals about Maggie's position. He loves her — that's unmistakable — and yet the content of his love is essentially a recognition of her helplessness. She will have nobody but Tom — meaning: a woman in Maggie's world has no independent recourse, no institution, no property, no standing. Tulliver's grief is real, but it is also a precise social document — he names the system that will fail her even as he mourns it. Eliot is not sentimental about this — the narrator calls him a man relapsing into the sense that this is a puzzling world. A puzzling world — not a cruel one, not an unjust one in Tulliver's vocabulary. He lacks the language for the injustice he half-perceives — and that gap between feeling and articulation is one of the saddest things in the novel.

James, that's exactly right — and it connects to something Eliot does consistently with her male characters in this novel. They feel deeply but cannot translate feeling into understanding — least of all understanding of the women they love. Structurally, this passage does something devastating: it places Tulliver's realisation just outside the sight-line of the farm-buildings. Before he reached the next turning, which would take him out of sight — Eliot is precise about this threshold. Once out of sight, the thought might have passed — but the dilapidated farm still in view holds him in the gravity of what he is riding away from. The landscape is not background — it is active, it is the mechanism of moral recollection.

I'd add only this — and I say it as someone not easily given to sentiment about fictional characters. There is something almost Shakespearean in Tulliver's trajectory — the man of strong will gradually undone by a world that outruns his comprehension. And his final articulate act here — speaking aloud to no one — has the quality of a soliloquy.
