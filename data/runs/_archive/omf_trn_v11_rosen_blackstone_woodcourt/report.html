Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me immediately about this novel is that Dickens refuses the usual alibi. In so many Victorian novels, the plot hinges on the idea that goodness will be rewarded — that virtue has its own economy. Our Mutual Friend interrogates that assumption from the very first pages. Take the Boffins — Noddy and Henrietta — who have spent their lives in service to a hard and exploitative master. Dickens describes them with a precision that cuts both ways. "The hard wrathful and sordid nature that had wrung as much work out of them as could be got in their best days, for as little money as could be paid to hurry on their worst." That sentence is a whole theory of labour in a subordinate clause. Maximum extraction, minimum pay — and Dickens knows exactly what he's describing.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the remarkable thing is that the master's cruelty doesn't corrupt the Boffins — but Dickens refuses to make that a comfortable observation. He's not saying goodness neutralises exploitation. He's saying that moral integrity survives the system — but only just, and with enormous cost. The line that follows is one of Dickens's most extraordinary moral pronouncements. "For, Evil often stops short at itself and dies with the doer of it; but Good, never." There's almost a biblical cadence to that — and it's absolutely deliberate. Dickens is insisting that what the powerful do to the powerless does not exhaust the moral story.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Exactly — and nowhere does Dickens expose the danger more savagely than in his portrait of the school system. Charley Hexam's school is one of the great satirical set-pieces in the novel. Dickens's target is not ignorance — it's the hypocrisy of a system that pretends to educate while actually performing social control. The narrator describes the whole establishment as being pervaded by — and he chooses this phrase deliberately — "a grimly ludicrous pretence that every pupil was childish and innocent." 'Grimly ludicrous' — two words that shouldn't coexist and yet are absolutely perfect together. Because the comedy of this passage is real — it's genuinely funny — and the grimness is real too.

It was a school for all ages, and for both sexes. The latter were kept apart, and the former were partitioned off into square assortments. But, all the place was pervaded by a grimly ludicrous pretence that every pupil was childish and innocent. This pretence, much favoured by the lady-visitors, led to the ghastliest absurdities. Several swaggering sinners had written their own biographies in the same strain; it always appearing from the lessons of those very boastful persons, that you were to do good, not because it was good, but because you were to make a good thing of it. Contrariwise, the adult pupils were taught to read (if they could learn) out of the New Testament; and by dint of stumbling over the syllables and keeping their bewildered eyes on the particular syllables coming round to their turn, were as absolutely ignorant of the sublime history, as if they had never seen or heard of it. An exceedingly and confoundingly perplexing jumble of a school, in fact, where black spirits and grey, red spirits and white, jumbled jumbled jumbled jumbled, jumbled every night. And particularly every Sunday night. For then, an inclined plane of unfortunate infants would be handed over to the prosiest and worst of all the teachers with good intentions, whom nobody older would endure. Who, taking his stand on the floor before them as chief executioner, would be attended by a conventional volunteer boy as executioner’s assistant. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The ragged school movement in the 1860s was genuinely well-intentioned by many of its participants — and that's precisely what Dickens finds so infuriating. Good intentions administered badly can be more damaging than indifference, because they carry the additional insult of self-congratulation. And Dickens captures that perfectly when he describes the moral literature — where you were to do good not because it was good, but because — "you were to make a good thing of it." Virtue as investment strategy — a Victorian ideology Dickens despised with extraordinary consistency.

And then there's the river — which is where all of this finally becomes something darker than satire. The Thames in this novel is not a backdrop — it is an active force. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That armour reading is exactly right — but I want to stress what the system is actually doing here, beneath the comedy. The father pre-assigns roles not out of love, but out of class management — maintaining the family's position within a hierarchy that requires each son to perform a designated function. When those roles fail — the shaky pillar, the non-circumnavigating naval officer — the system doesn't collapse. The sons absorb the failure privately. And this is the invisible cruelty Dickens keeps returning to: the institution endures while individuals are broken by it.
