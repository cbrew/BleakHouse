Thank you — and yes, the range is remarkable, and I think that range is itself the point. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to press on something beneath the comedy here, because the comedy has teeth. Maggie has only been to school a year. She reads the dictionary because she has so few books. The narrator frames this as charming eccentricity, and it is — but it is also the product of a specific material deprivation. The Tullivers are tradespeople. The proper education, the books, the tutor — that belongs to someone else. And crucially — all of this applies double for a girl. The line about "a small female of eight or nine" in "these days" is biting precisely because the improved education of girls it gestures at is still not available to Maggie's class.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That juxtaposition — terror and intellectual inquiry happening simultaneously — is Eliot's way of showing us that Maggie's mind cannot be switched off. It is not a charming quirk. It is a structural feature. And I think it leads directly into the education passages — because the question of who gets to use their mind is exactly what Eliot is circling. Tom is sent to Mr Stelling to be educated. Maggie is not. And what Eliot shows us is not merely that this is unfair, but that the system produces absurdity — it educates the wrong child in the wrong things.
