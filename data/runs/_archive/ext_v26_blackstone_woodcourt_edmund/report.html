Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Caroline is right to fix on that sentence — it performs what it describes. The wind sounding a low accompaniment: Dickens folds the natural world into the domestic one without effort, without strain. But I want to draw attention to the face of its generous master brightening everything we saw. The house as Jarndyce — that is the moral point. Atmosphere, in Dickens, is never merely pictorial — it is the externalisation of a character's inner condition. Jarndyce's generosity literally illuminates the building — those windows softened by shadows of curtains, shining out upon the starlight night. The quaint variety of the furnishings — birds staring at trout, portrait brothers separated by rooms — is the signature of an intelligence that delights in the particular, the individual, the unrepeatable. And that, I would argue, is the novel's deepest conviction: that persons matter more than systems.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I won't quarrel with Edmund on the particulars — the passage is glorious — but I'd set it against a very different kind of house that shadows this novel. Because before we had Bleak House the refuge, Dickens gives us Chesney Wold — the Dedlock estate, which has its own legend, its own ghost, its own curse. There is a crippled woman in its past who is told to keep silent about her suffering — and refuses to. She drops on the terrace pavement, and when her husband comes to lift her, she repulses him and delivers what I can only call a constitutional indictment of an entire class. "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" The gothic machinery is deliberate — but it is also historically precise. An aristocratic house built on the suppression of inconvenient suffering — that is not only a ghost story, that is a description of the English landed gentry's entire social compact. She walked until she could not walk — and then she promised to keep walking.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
There is a third kind of house in this opening movement of the novel — and it is the most fragile. The household that Jarndyce assembles around him — Esther, Richard, Ada — is not built on inheritance or on stone. It is built on confidence — his word — and on the possibility of love. When Jarndyce addresses Richard and Ada about the possibility he had long contemplated, he is extraordinarily careful with his language. "When I contemplated these relations between us four which have so brightened my life and so invested it with new interests and pleasures, I certainly did contemplate, afar off, the possibility of you and your pretty cousin here (don't be shy, Ada, don't be shy, my dear!) being in a mind to go through life together." Afar off — he uses it twice in that speech. It is a man trying very hard not to press what he has imagined upon those he loves — and succeeding, just barely.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Dickens is dramatising here is something very specific to Richard's social position. Richard is a young gentleman of good family but no fortune — dependent, entirely, on the outcome of Jarndyce and Jarndyce. That case was a real type — Chancery suits that dragged for generations, consuming the very estate they were meant to distribute. So Mr. Jarndyce's advice to Richard in Chapter Thirteen isn't just benevolent counsel — it's a diagnosis of a structural trap. He says, and I find this extraordinary in its precision: "Trust in nothing but in Providence and your own efforts. Never separate the two, like the heathen waggoner." The heathen waggoner — that's the man who prays to Hercules to free his stuck cart rather than putting his own shoulder to the wheel. Jarndyce sees exactly what Richard is becoming — a man waiting for Fortune to do the work effort should do.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
James is quite right that Jarndyce diagnoses the condition — but I'd press a little on the word structural. What Dickens gives us in Richard is not a victim of circumstance but a failure of will — and that distinction matters enormously. Jarndyce is magnificently clear about this — listen to what follows that injunction: "If you entertain the supposition that any real success, in great things or in small, ever was or could be, ever will or can be, wrested from Fortune by fits and starts, leave that wrong idea here or leave your cousin Ada here." Dickens, as always, is more interested in character than in legislation.

Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I want to bring in a figure who stands at the opposite end of this moral spectrum — Mr. Tulkinghorn. Because Dickens, I think, is not only tracing Richard's decline — he is meditating throughout this novel on what it means to be utterly unreadable. Tulkinghorn, in Chapter Eleven, is described with one of the most devastating similes in the novel. "As easily might the tone of a delicate musical instrument be inferred from its case, as the tone of Mr. Tulkinghorn from his case." The man has shown nothing but his shell — patience, impatience, attention, abstraction — none of it can be read. Richard is destroyed partly because he cannot conceal anything — his soul is entirely on his face. Tulkinghorn is destroyed partly because he conceals everything — and concealment in this novel is a form of corruption. Dickens is running both trajectories simultaneously, and the contrast is mordant.

Edmund makes a point I find genuinely compelling — the compound interest image is remarkable precisely because it's economic language applied to filth. In 1852, when Bleak House began appearing in serial parts, the mud of London streets was a genuine public-health catastrophe — it was, among other things, the vector for cholera. The institution Dickens is attacking in Bleak House — the Court of Chancery — was not a fiction or an exaggeration. He says, in effect: "Is my daughter a-washin? Yes, she IS a-washin. Look at the water. Smell it! That's wot we drinks. How do you like it, and what do you think of gin instead! An't my place dirty? Yes, it is dirty—it's nat'rally dirty, and it's nat'rally onwholesome; and we've had five dirty and onwholesome children, as is all dead infants, and so much the better for them, and for us besides." That final clause — "so much the better for them" — is the kind of thing that a comfortable reader wants to flinch from. But it is also, on its own terms, a perfectly coherent moral position. What gets me every time is the rhythm of it. That repeated structure — the question, then the answer, then the unbearable elaboration. He's doing a catechism. What strikes me, pulling all of this together, is how different the novel's two trajectories of ruin actually are. The brickmaker is ruined by a society that gives him undrinkable water and then hands him a tract. Richard is ruined by an expectation — by Jarndyce and Jarndyce and the terrible possibility that one day it might be resolved in his favour.
