James Blackstone (social_historian)
That phrase is worth pausing on for a moment, because in the 1850s it carried a very specific social freight. Trade was trade — it was not simply a lower social station, it was a different moral universe in the eyes of the gentry class. James Blackstone (social_historian)
That detail about the daughter is not incidental — it's almost a clinical observation. The cotton mills of mid-Victorian England produced lung disease in workers at a rate that was well documented by the time Gaskell was writing. Byssinosis — what they called brown lung — was endemic, and it killed young women in particular because they were disproportionately employed as spinners. When Gaskell writes that this girl is still more unhealthy than her already careworn father, she's encoding a public health reality that her readers would have recognised. The novel makes you feel it before it makes you think it — which is precisely how fiction does what journalism cannot.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The migration you're describing, Oliver, is historically precise — and historically alarming, from the perspective of the mill owners. In the early 1850s, when Gaskell was writing, a strike crowd in the street outside a mill wasn't metaphor — it was genuine physical danger, to property, to persons, to capital. The legal framework for peaceful assembly was fragile, and the line between demonstration and riot was frequently drawn after the fact, by the class that owned the magistracy.
