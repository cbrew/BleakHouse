Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The fog opening is one of those passages that, when you first encounter it, you think: this is unlike anything I have read before. And when you've read it fifty times — which I have, in preparation and performance — you still think that. Dickens doesn't open with a character, doesn't open with a scene in the conventional sense. He opens with a condition — a state of the world. Listen to how it begins — and I do mean listen, because this is prose written for the voice. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That word "defiled" — he doesn't let London off lightly, does he. The fog doesn't just obscure things; it participates in the corruption of the city. And then he does something extraordinary — he descends. From the grand panorama of the river, the marshes, the heights — down, down, down to the toes and fingers of a shivering little apprentice boy on deck. The fog is everywhere — and nowhere is it crueller than at the very bottom of the social order.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Oliver's describing — that descent from panorama to the individual body — is one of Dickens's most characteristic structural moves, and it's doing enormous work here. Because the fog passage isn't just atmospheric throat-clearing before the plot begins — it is the argument of the novel, stated in full before a single character has been named. Fog is Chancery. Fog is the law. Fog is the system that blankets everyone and injures those least able to protect themselves. And then Dickens makes it explicit — almost gleefully explicit — in the passage that follows, when he gives us Temple Bar. "The raw afternoon is rawest, and the dense fog is densest, and the muddy streets are muddiest near that leaden-headed old obstruction, appropriate ornament for the threshold of a leaden-headed old corporation, Temple Bar." The superlatives — rawest, densest, muddiest — they accumulate like the fog itself, pressing down. And "leaden-headed" — repeated twice in a single clause — that's not accident, that's a bludgeon. Dickens has decided that the law is not merely ineffective — it is constitutionally, organically dense.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
A diagnosis — yes, and delivered with that parenthetical snap: "a great (and dirty) city." Those brackets are the voice of a man who can barely contain his contempt for the pretensions of the place. And then — and I always savour this — after all that vast, sweeping, cosmic fog, Dickens puts us into a balloon. "Chance people on the bridges peeping over the parapets into a nether sky of fog, with fog all round them, as if they were up in a balloon and hanging in the misty clouds." That image of the balloon — of people suspended, unable to see up or down — is the condition of everyone in Chancery. They are hanging in the misty clouds of a suit that will outlast their lives. Dickens has, in four paragraphs, without a single named character, told us exactly what this novel is about.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I read that Chancery scene in Chapter Twenty-Four is the architecture of the sentence Dickens builds around Esther's bewilderment. It's a sequence of infinitive phrases — 'to see,' 'to think,' 'to consider,' 'to behold' — stacked one on top of the other like floors of a building that keeps rising past any reasonable height. Each phrase adds another layer of contradiction between the court's composure and the human wreckage it produces. And then, after all that accumulated pressure, Dickens detonates the whole construction with this: "to behold the Lord Chancellor and the whole array of practitioners under him looking at one another and at the spectators as if nobody had ever heard that all over England the name in which they were assembled was a bitter jest, was held in universal horror, contempt, and indignation" That phrase — 'a bitter jest' — does everything. It tells us that the scandal of Chancery is not merely known — it is universal, it is a matter of common contempt — and yet the court proceeds as if the knowledge does not exist. The institution has learned to look straight through its own infamy.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That's the trap sprung perfectly by Chapter Thirty-Seven, where Richard tries to articulate what the suit does to his sense of time. He's defending himself to Esther, trying to explain why he can't simply settle into a life, and he reaches for this image: "If you were living in an unfinished house, liable to have the roof put on or taken off — to be from top to bottom pulled down or built up — to-morrow, next day, next week, next month, next year — you would find it hard to rest or settle." Notice how the rhythm of that list — 'to-morrow, next day, next week, next month, next year' — stretches out across time like the suit itself. Richard's argument sounds reasonable in the moment — almost persuasive. But Dickens has constructed the image so precisely that it simultaneously demolishes Richard's position: you choose to live in an unfinished house if you move in before it's built. The metaphor is self-incriminating, and Richard can't see it.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
This is one of the most extraordinary pieces of psychological analysis in all of Dickens, and it comes from the omniscient narrator — which is itself significant. Esther couldn't say this. She's too close, too loving. So Dickens deploys the cold analytical eye of the third-person voice to dissect what has happened to Richard's mind. And the diagnosis is this: "injustice breeds injustice; the fighting with shadows and being defeated by them necessitates the setting up of substances to combat" The suit is a shadow — impalpable, impossible to grasp or fight directly. So the mind, in its desperation, finds a body to carry the abstraction: Jarndyce. The man who tried hardest to save Richard becomes the object onto which Richard's ruin is projected. Dickens understood — with a novelist's precision and a diagnostician's ruthlessness — how corruption reroutes itself through the ego.

And what's extraordinary about that rhetorical move is the precision of the architecture. Dickens is writing in a mode — the mock-ceremonial — where the comedy and the condemnation are structurally inseparable. Notice the repeated phrase "ought to be — as here they are" — that little sting of disappointed expectation fulfilled.
