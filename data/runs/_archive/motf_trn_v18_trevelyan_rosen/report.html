Oliver Trevelyan (literary_critic)
Delighted to be here — this is a novel I've lived inside for months at a time, and it never stops surprising me. What strikes me, every time, is how physical this novel is — the emotions are bodily, they happen in the chest and the stomach and the limbs. There's a moment I keep returning to — the scene where Maggie and Tom encounter Philip Wakem suddenly on a narrow path. Eliot writes that her imagination, always rushing extravagantly beyond an immediate impression, saw her tall, strong brother grasping the feeble Philip bodily, crushing him and trampling on him. That word — extravagantly — is doing everything there. Eliot doesn't say Maggie feared Tom would hurt Philip — she says her imagination ran extravagantly ahead of the moment. That excess of imagination is Maggie's great gift and her great affliction — and Eliot knew exactly what she was doing putting it right there, in that word.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to stay with that scene a moment, because I think there's something we risk missing if we read it only as tragedy of feeling. Tom is enforcing something — not just brotherly authority but the entire social logic that says Philip Wakem, the son of the enemy, the deformed boy from the wrong family, is forbidden. The terror in Maggie's face is the terror of someone who has broken the law — not the law of parliament, but the law of class and kin. And Philip's body — feeble, as Eliot says — is precisely the body the social order has decided counts for less. The imagined crushing is grotesque — but it's also Maggie's unconscious recognition that the world Tom represents genuinely would crush Philip if it could.

Daniel's right — and Eliot returns to that logic of the body much later, in a passage I find almost unbearably beautiful in the way it catches the texture of desire. Stephen Guest is stroking Maggie's dog — ostensibly a perfectly innocent act — and Eliot tells us what is actually happening inside him. The irrevocable wrong that must blot her life had been committed; she had brought sorrow into the lives of others,—into the lives that were knit up with hers by trust and love. The feeling of a few short weeks had hurried her into the sins her nature had most recoiled from,—breach of faith and cruel selfishness; she had rent the ties that had given meaning to duty, and had made herself an outlawed soul, with no guide but the wayward choice of her own passion. And where would that lead her? Where had it led her now? She had said she would rather die than fall into that temptation. She felt it now,—now that the consequences of such a fall had come before the outward act was completed. There was at least this fruit from all her years of striving after the highest and best,—that her soul though betrayed, beguiled, ensnared, could never deliberately consent to a choice of the lower. And a choice of what? O God! not a choice of joy, but of conscious cruelty and hardness; for could she ever cease to see before her Lucy and Philip, with their murdered trust and hopes? Her life with Stephen could have no sacredness; she must forever sink and wander vaguely, driven by uncertain impulse; for she had let go the clue of life,—that clue which once in the far-off years her young need had clutched so strongly. She had renounced all delights then, before she knew them, before they had come within her reach. Philip had been right when he told her that she knew nothing of renunciation; she had thought it was quiet ecstasy; she saw it face to face now,—that sad, patient, loving strength which holds the clue of life,—and saw that the thorns were forever pressing on its brow. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What George Eliot gives us in that scene is not merely a brother being unkind. It is the full apparatus of social contempt being deployed against a man who has committed the unforgivable offence of existing outside the norm. Tom doesn't just threaten Philip — he weaponises Philip's body against him. Listen to how Eliot renders this: "Your puny, miserable body, that ought to have put some modesty into your mind, shall not protect you. I'll thrash you; I'll hold you up to public scorn. Who wouldn't laugh at the idea of your turning lover to a fine girl?" Tom's logic is perfectly legible from inside the social order he represents. Philip is deformed, therefore unworthy of desire, therefore his love is an imposition, a kind of transgression. And the threat of public ridicule is the ultimate weapon — because in this world, exposure means annihilation.
