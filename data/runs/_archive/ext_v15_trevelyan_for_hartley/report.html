What strikes me every time I return to this novel — and I have returned to it many times — is how immediately theatrical it is. Dickens doesn't ease you in. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to pull in the other direction for a moment — not away from that warmth, but underneath it. Because the novel is also giving us, at exactly this same moment, the crippled woman walking the Dedlock terrace — and her curse. The contrast is not accidental — Dickens is setting up two kinds of house, two kinds of inheritance. Bleak House, oddly, is the one that feels like a home. Chesney Wold, for all its grandeur, is haunted before we've even arrived. Listen to the words the crippled lady speaks from the pavement where she falls. "I will die here where I have walked. And I will walk here, though I am in my grave. I will walk here until the pride of this house is humbled. And when calamity or when disgrace is coming to it, let the Dedlocks listen for my step!" That is not melodrama — or not only melodrama. It is the language of institutional pride being called to account by personal suffering.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Jo is the precise legal zero of this novel. He possesses nothing — not property, not education, not standing before the law. The courts of Chancery consumed the fortunes of middle-class families over decades. Jo doesn't even exist to those courts. And at the same moment, Dickens shows us lawyers defending their system with magnificent circularity. Kenge's defence of Vholes is one of the purest specimens of institutional self-preservation in all of literature. "Sir, that class of practitioners would be swept from the face of the earth." The argument is not 'this law is just.' The argument is 'Mr. Vholes is respectable, and therefore the law must remain.' That is not satire at a distance. That is a Parliamentary inquiry, reported in mock blue-book style — Dickens knows exactly how these committees work, and exactly how they fail.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Right, and this is where Dickens does something absolutely devilish — he gives us Mrs. Piper. She is a witness at the inquest into Nemo's death, and she testifies — if you can call it that — in what the novel describes as chiefly parentheses and without punctuation. Dickens is writing prose that performs its own argument — the chaos of her speech is the chaos of a community that knows nothing and suspects everything. Listen to this — and I want you to hear the rhythm of it, because it's extraordinary. "the plaintive — so Mrs. Piper insists on calling the deceased — was reported to have sold himself." She can't even say the word correctly — 'plaintive' for 'plaintiff' — and yet she insists on it. There's something wonderful and terrible there — the legal language she half-remembers is itself a kind of corruption of the original. And that malapropism — 'Methoozellers,' 'feariocious' — every mangled word is a tiny portrait of a mind shaped by poverty and exclusion.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The inquest procedure itself is doing a great deal of work here that a modern reader might miss. In Dickens's England, coroner's inquests were frequently farcical — witnesses called without any meaningful examination, testimony taken in whatever form it arrived. Mrs. Piper's performance isn't Dickens exaggerating for effect — it's Dickens reporting a system that functioned almost exactly like that. And the detail that makes me stop every time is the boy. The novel tells us that one cold winter night, when Jo was shivering in a doorway, Nemo came back, questioned him, found he had not a friend in the world. "Neither have I. Not one!" and gave him the price of a supper and a night's lodging. Four words — 'Neither have I. Not one!' — and an entire social history of Victorian poverty is contained in them. Two people at the absolute bottom of a society that has decided not to see them — finding each other in a doorway.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And that, I think, is where Dickens is at his very greatest — that moment of recognition between two people the world has discarded. There's no sentimentality in it — Dickens doesn't linger, doesn't explain. He just gives you the action: the man turned, came back, questioned him, gave him what he had. And then there's Jo. If the brickmaker is the system's product made furious, Jo is the system's product made innocent — which is, in some ways, even harder to bear. His testimony at the inquest is among the most devastating passages of social protest in nineteenth-century English fiction. What it teaches me — and this has not dimmed across any re-reading — is the cost of invisibility. Dickens is meticulous about what it means to go unseen by the society that claims to be civilised.
