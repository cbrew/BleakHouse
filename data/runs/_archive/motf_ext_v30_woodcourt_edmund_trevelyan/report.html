Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, and every time, is that Eliot announces the novel's emotional register before she has given us a single character. The opening is an act of memory — the narrator watching the mill from a distance, across time. We are inside grief before we know the name of a single person who has been lost. That is a remarkable piece of novelistic confidence — to ask for feeling before you have earned it by story. And then into that atmosphere of longing and loss Eliot drops Mr Tulliver — a man of great feeling and almost no wisdom. The tragedy, as I read it, is not social — it is moral and temperamental. A father who loves his daughter fiercely and understands almost nothing about what she needs.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Edmund is right about Mr Tulliver, though I'd add — with the greatest affection — that he is also magnificently, endearingly absurd. Eliot gives us this portrait of a man who is perfectly capable of clear thinking in calm water and utterly at sea the moment feeling takes the wheel. And she describes that mechanism — that psychology — with a metaphor so perfect it makes you want to applaud. "for getting a strong impression that a skein is tangled there is nothing like snatching hastily at a single thread." That is Eliot at her most wickedly precise — diagnostic without being cruel. She loves this man even as she dissects him. And for a performer, that double note — affection and irony held simultaneously — is the whole game.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I keep coming back to, reading this novel for what must be the eighth or ninth time, is how early we feel the weight of what is going to be lost. Eliot lets it fall — just for a moment, in a single line — when Mr Tulliver speaks to Maggie. "Ay, my little wench, but I shall never live twice o'er." And the reversal — when the gypsies become terrifying — is one of the funniest and most psychologically acute things in the book. That detail is not merely comic — it is the signature of the whole character. Maggie's mind cannot be suppressed even by extremity of feeling. There's a passage I want to set alongside this — Tom's reaction after Maggie is fetched away from his school. I want to begin with a small moment — a child's arm thrust out in mud — because Eliot uses it to say something quite extraordinary about the gap between passion and action. The passage has been lodged in my mind for decades. Eliot reaches for the language of classical tragedy, and she does so with a kind of knowing comedy. She writes, and I must read this in full: "There were passions at war in Maggie at that moment to have made a tragedy, if tragedies were made by passion only; but the essential τι μέγεθος which was present in the passion was wanting to the action; the utmost Maggie could do, with a fierce thrust of her small brown arm, was to push poor little pink-and-white Lucy into the cow-trodden mud." That Greek phrase — τι μέγεθος — the essential greatness — comes straight from the Poetics. Aristotle's criterion for tragedy: magnitude of action proportionate to magnitude of feeling. Maggie has the feeling in full — but can only muster a push into the mud.

In short, there was in this family a peculiar tradition as to what was the right thing in household management and social demeanour, and the only bitter circumstance attending this superiority was a painful inability to approve the condiments or the conduct of families ungoverned by the Dodson tradition. A female Dodson, when in “strange houses,” always ate dry bread with her tea, and declined any sort of preserves, having no confidence in the butter, and thinking that the preserves had probably begun to ferment from want of due sugar and boiling.
