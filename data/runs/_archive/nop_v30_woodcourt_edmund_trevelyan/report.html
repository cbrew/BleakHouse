Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver is quite right about the theatrical quality, and I wouldn't quarrel with the observation about corruption — so far as it goes. But I think we should be careful not to flatten this extraordinary passage into a simple diagram — city bad, country good. The fog is everywhere, after all. It reaches — and Dickens is quite specific — into the Essex marshes, the Kentish heights. It is not a metropolitan affliction only. What Dickens is constructing, I would argue, is an image of universal obscuration — of moral blindness that belongs to no one class, no one institution. He then brings that fog indoors — and this is the masterstroke — into the High Court of Chancery itself. "Never can there come fog too thick, never can there come mud and mire too deep, to assort with the groping and floundering condition of this High Court of Chancery." Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
I'm glad Oliver mentions the absurdity, because I think this is where the purely political reading begins to strain. If the fog were simply a denunciation of the legal system — if it were journalism — it would not be funny. But the Lord Chancellor sitting in his foggy glory is comic — and Dickens knows it. Comedy, Dr. Johnson reminds us, is a mirror held up to the follies of mankind. Dickens holds up the mirror very high — high enough to catch not just Chancery but the whole of human self-delusion. What the fog hides, ultimately, is not a conspiracy but a condition — the condition of not seeing clearly. And what the novel then asks — brilliantly — is which of these characters will find their way through it, and which will be consumed.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That question of who finds their way through it — I think it haunts the whole novel. And it's worth saying: the fog isn't just atmospheric backdrop. It returns — physically — throughout the novel. Every time we enter Chancery, every time we move through Lincoln's Inn, we feel it close around us again. And there's the counterpoint, which Dickens establishes almost immediately — Esther. The moment Esther begins to narrate in her own voice — that clear, self-deprecating, utterly honest voice — the fog lifts, just slightly. She says, "I have a great deal of difficulty in beginning to write my portion of these pages" — and it's so quietly human that it cuts right through all that smoke. Dickens has given us two narrators — the omniscient, fog-drenched, present-tense one; and Esther, who writes from memory, from survival. That is an extraordinary structural decision.

Host (host)
We turn now to one of Bleak House's most painful portraits — Richard Carstone. He arrives in the novel as a young man of charm and real promise — handsome, warm-hearted, genuinely loveable. And yet Dickens arranges his destruction with the patience and inevitability of a Greek tragedy. Edmund, I want to start with you — because I suspect you see Richard rather differently from the way the novel's social critics tend to frame him.
Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Quite differently, yes — and I think the difference matters enormously. There is a persistent tendency in discussions of Richard Carstone to make him a victim — a victim of Chancery, of the system, of forces larger than himself. And Dickens certainly provides the materials for that reading. But I think it is — if I may put it plainly — a rather comfortable reading, and not the one Dickens is actually writing. Richard has every advantage — the guardianship of Jarndyce, the love of Ada, the friendship of Esther. He has, in Jarndyce, a man of exemplary wisdom and disinterested affection standing beside him at every turning. What undoes Richard is not the Court of Chancery — it is the deficiency of will that Chancery merely reveals and accelerates. Dickens gives us the key early, when Esther observes Richard's readiness to be pleased with any profession — surgeon, lawyer, soldier — with equal enthusiasm and equal brevity. "He had been eight years at a public school and had learnt, I understood, to make Latin verses of several sorts, in the most admirable manner." Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Oliver has it exactly right, and I think it's why the novel can't be resolved into a single verdict. Bleak House holds its contradictions deliberately — the private happiness and the public outrage live together, unreconciled, by design. And what I love most, on a purely craft level, is how Dickens ties the two narrative strands together not through plot mechanics but through image. The fog of the opening chapter never quite lifts — it reappears throughout, thinner perhaps, never gone. But the last image Dickens grants Esther is light — the new Bleak House in sunshine, the children running, her husband looking at her with the same love he showed when she was changed. And Esther, characteristically, questions even that happiness — she breaks off and says she can hardly believe she could be lovelier than before.
