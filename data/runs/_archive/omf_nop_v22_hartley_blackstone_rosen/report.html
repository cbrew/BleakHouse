Eleanor — as a novelist yourself, what do you make of that opening move?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's one of the great opening gambits in the English novel, I think — and it works on so many levels simultaneously. Dickens drops us into darkness and motion before we know any of the characters' names or circumstances. What's so brilliant about that first chapter is the way the river becomes immediately moral — not just a setting, but a system. Gaffer Hexam makes his living from the dead, and Dickens describes him with this extraordinary ambivalence — neither condemning him nor sentimentalising him. He tells his daughter Lizzie that the river has been very good to them — it's fed and clothed them. And there's something in that pride — that fierce, unwholesome pride — that Dickens never quite lets us dismiss. What strikes me structurally is that he's already establishing the novel's central metaphor: everything of value in this world comes from somewhere disgusting. The river gives up bodies and the dust heaps give up fortunes, and the question is who gets to wash their hands of the source.
Host (host)
James, you've spent a lot of time with the historical reality of what those dust heaps actually were. For listeners who don't know — what was the Harmon fortune actually built on?
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The dust heaps were a genuinely significant industry in mid-Victorian London — and significantly more lucrative than most people today would imagine. Dust, in this context, means the ash and cinder from domestic coal fires, but also household waste of every description — vegetable matter, bones, rags, and what the Victorians delicately called 'soil.' The cinder could be sold to brickmakers; the bones went to fertiliser manufacturers; rags went back into paper; the finer dust was sifted and used in brick mortar. A large dust heap could be worth tens of thousands of pounds — which is precisely what Dickens is telling us about old Harmon's estate. What I find historically sharp about the novel's premise is how accurately it captures something about Victorian capital formation: the great fortunes were almost always built on something the polite world preferred not to look at directly. Dickens intuited that perfectly — and he gives old Harmon this quality of aggressive miserliness that attaches to the muck itself, as if the man has become indistinguishable from his business.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James is right, and I'd push it a step further. What Dickens is staging in this novel's basic architecture is the way capitalism depends on mystification — on the active concealment of where value comes from. The Veneerings are the purest expression of this — they are literally new money performing old money, sitting at a dinner table surrounded by objects they've just acquired. Dickens describes their house as if it's a theatrical set — all gloss and reflection, the great looking glass above the sideboard reflecting the guests back at themselves. The guests are only ever seen as reflections — they have no substance, no history, no interiority that the novel grants them. And that's the point — in a world organised around the circulation of money, people become interchangeable images of themselves. Dickens understood that before Marx had fully articulated the theory of commodity fetishism — which I think is one of the things that makes him genuinely radical, rather than just reformist.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The Veneering dinner is extraordinary as a piece of craft, though — I want to pick up on what Daniel said about reflections, because Dickens makes that almost pathologically literal. The great looking glass runs throughout those early chapters like a recurring stage direction — and what it reflects is always surfaces, never depths. There's that wonderful passage where Dickens describes the Veneering guests as 'the bran-new' everything — bran-new bride, bran-new house, bran-new furniture. The repetition of 'bran-new' is doing something almost satirical in its relentlessness — it refuses to let the reader settle into the Veneerings' world as normal. Technically, this is free indirect discourse being weaponised — the narrator adopts the Veneerings' own self-congratulatory register, and thereby exposes it. It's the same technique he uses with Mrs. Merdle in Little Dorrit, but here it's sharper, more sustained, almost operatic in its contempt.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The legal dimension of the Harmon will is worth pausing on here, because it's not merely a plot device — it's a precise historical instrument. The condition that John Harmon must marry a specific woman to inherit was a recognised — if legally contested — form of testamentary condition in Victorian law. It treats the heir's personal life as an extension of the property itself — and that's exactly what Dickens wants us to feel. The will doesn't just control the money — it controls the body, the marriage, the identity of the person who inherits. John Harmon's decision to travel under a false identity and observe Bella Wilfer before revealing himself is, in this light, a completely comprehensible act of self-preservation. He has been sold, as a child, into a transaction — and he is trying to find out who he is before the transaction completes.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And Bella Wilfer has been sold into the same transaction from the other side — which is what makes her such a complicated figure in the novel's moral architecture. She is openly, almost defiantly, materialistic in the early chapters — she wants money, she says so, she's not embarrassed about it. And I think Dickens grants her that honesty as a kind of radical transparency that the Veneerings and Podsnap conspicuously lack. She knows she's a commodity — she says at one point that she wishes she could be bought — and naming that is more honest than pretending the market doesn't exist. The novel's arc for Bella is about whether she can find a way to want something that the market hasn't already put a price on. That's a genuinely difficult question, and I think Dickens is more honest about its difficulty than he sometimes gets credit for.
Host (host)
That tension between honesty and appetite — between knowing the system and being caught inside it — feels like it runs through almost every major character in the book. And that, I think, is exactly what we'll be tracing over this series — the way Our Mutual Friend maps an entire society's relationship to money, mud, and self-deception. We're going to take a short break, and when we come back we'll be going deeper into the river world — Gaffer Hexam, Rogue Riderhood, and the question of what it means to make a living from death.

A System Built on Bones: How Dickens Anatomizes Class Power (deep_dive)

Host (host)
Welcome to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked, the show where we pull apart one of Dickens's most extraordinary novels — and find that it pulls back. I'm your host, and today we're going deep into the architecture of class in Our Mutual Friend — what Dickens called, in effect, a system built on bones. With me are three extraordinary readers of this novel. Dr. Eleanor Hartley is a novelist and creative writing teacher who cares passionately about the architecture of sentences — how Dickens constructs an effect, word by word. Prof. James Blackstone is a legal and social historian who knows exactly what these institutions — the dust heaps, the river trade, the Chancery-adjacent world of wills — actually looked like from the inside. And Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads every novel as an anatomy of class power — and finds, in Dickens, a writer more radical than he perhaps intended to be. The novel opens, famously, on the Thames — on a man and his daughter dredging the river for corpses to rob. It is, from its very first image, a novel about what people are willing to do to survive — and what the system requires them to do. Daniel, let's start with you — because that opening image feels like a thesis statement for everything that follows.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It absolutely is a thesis statement — and Dickens knew it. Gaffer Hexam and his daughter Lizzie are harvesting the dead from the Thames. That word — harvesting — is the right one, because Dickens frames it as labor, as an economy, as a system of extraction. The corpses produce income. The river is a factory floor. And what Dickens establishes immediately is that the very bottom of this society — the people who literally live off the dead — are morally serious people. Gaffer has a code: he won't rob a living man. The system has degraded the conditions of his life to the absolute limit — and yet he maintains an ethics within that degradation. That's Dickens's radical move: to show dignity and moral seriousness at precisely the point where the middle-class reader would expect to find only squalor.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And the craft choice that makes that possible — that forces the reader to take Gaffer seriously — is the narration's absolute refusal of condescension. Dickens describes the river scene with a kind of terrible beauty — the boat, the tow-rope, the dark water — before he tells us what they're actually doing. He makes you see it as landscape first, labor second, and horror third. That sequencing is doing enormous work. By the time we understand what Gaffer is retrieving, we've already been made to inhabit his world — we're in the boat with him. There's a passage where Dickens writes about the boat trailing what he calls 'some object' in the water — and the deliberate vagueness of 'some object' is breathtaking. He describes it as 'something that he towed' — and that evasion is the reader being protected, and confronted, at the same time. The sentence withholds the word 'corpse' the way a skilled narrator withholds any terrible truth — so that when you finally arrive at it, you feel the weight of the whole journey.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
What makes that opening image historically precise, as well as artistically charged, is that this was a real occupation. River-finders, or 'dredgermen,' worked the Thames in the 1860s exactly as Dickens describes — retrieving bodies, claiming the contents of their pockets as a recognized salvage right. The coroner's inquest system meant that identifying and delivering a body could generate a small fee — so there was, in the most literal sense, a market for corpses. Dickens isn't exaggerating for Gothic effect. He's reporting. And the dust heaps — old Harmon's fortune — are equally precise. Victorian dust contractors processed household waste — including ash, bones, rags, and old metal — and sold it on to brick makers, farmers for fertilizer, paper mills. A successful dust contractor could accumulate very substantial wealth — and old Harmon did exactly that, dying and leaving the whole edifice of it to be fought over. The fortune, in other words, was literally built on other people's refuse — on what society had thrown away.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That's the central metaphor of the novel, isn't it — and Dickens presses it with absolute relentlessness. Every fortune in this novel is built on something degraded, something discarded, something dead. The Veneerings' money is new and of entirely mysterious provenance — it simply appears, gleaming, with no visible origin. The Podsnap fortune is rooted in insurance — a system that profits from risk and misfortune. And Dickens is very precise about the Veneerings' dinner table — that great curved mirror, that table full of faces reflected back at themselves. He writes of the Veneerings as 'brand-new people in a brand-new house in a bran-new quarter of London.' Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The name 'Veneering' is almost too on the nose — and yet it works, because Dickens earns it. The allegorical name would feel cheap if the characterization were thin — but the Veneerings are exquisitely rendered. That dinner-table scene, with the great looking-glass above the sideboard — Dickens uses it as a kind of surveillance apparatus. Every guest is reflected. Everyone is watching everyone else be watched. And the narrator's voice in those scenes is pure satirical distance — he keeps calling the guests 'the Buffers' and 'the Podsnaps' as though they're species, not people. It's a technique Dickens inherited from Jonson — the comedy of humours — but he's using it here in the service of social anatomy rather than pure comedy. These people aren't reduced to types because they're not important — they're reduced to types because that's what the system has done to them.

Podsnap's great gesture — the sweep of the arm that removes unpleasant facts from the field of vision — is one of the most efficient satirical images in all of Victorian fiction. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And what's remarkable is that Dickens positions the people crushed by Podsnappery — Betty Higden, for instance — as the novel's most lucid critics of the system. Betty Higden — this old woman, this child-minder, this person the system regards as utterly disposable — is in absolute terror of the workhouse. Not because she's confused about the Poor Law — but because she understands it perfectly. She knows that to enter the workhouse is to surrender the last shred of autonomy, the last claim to personhood. Dickens has her say something to the effect that she would rather die free in a ditch than be buried in parish charity. And that's not sentiment — that's a class analysis. She is articulating, in the language of pride and dignity, what the system actually does to the poor: it doesn't just take their money, it takes their selfhood. And she dies on the road, escaping it. Dickens doesn't soften that.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The Betty Higden strand is where Dickens's anger is most undisguised — and I think it's also where the novel's structure is most revealing. Because Dickens cuts between Betty Higden's flight and the Veneering dinner parties with almost brutal editorial speed. The juxtaposition is the argument. He doesn't need to state it. On one page, Podsnap sweeps the poor from the table with a gesture. On the next, Betty Higden is literally dying in a field. The architecture of the novel IS the indictment. Dickens understood that structure is rhetoric.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The New Poor Law of 1834 is the specific institution being anatomized in that strand, and it's worth being precise about how brutal it was. The 'less eligibility' principle meant that conditions inside the workhouse were deliberately kept worse than the worst conditions outside it. The theory was that if outdoor relief were available, the poor would be idle — so you made the alternative so degrading that only the truly desperate would seek it. Families were separated on entry. Men, women, and children housed apart. Oakum picking. Stone breaking. Betty Higden's terror is not irrational. It is the most rational possible response to a system specifically designed to be terrifying.
Host (host)
There's another strand of class mobility in the novel that I want to bring in here — the story of Bella Wilfer and the Boffins, and what money does to people who didn't have it. Because Dickens seems equally interested in the corrupting power of wealth on those who acquire it unexpectedly — the Golden Dustman experiment. Daniel, does that strand complicate the class analysis — or confirm it?
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It confirms it — but in the most interesting possible way, because the Boffins are Dickens's great counter-example. They inherit the Harmon dust fortune and they do not become Veneerings. They remain, in their instincts, humane. And yet Dickens can't quite trust that — so he puts them through the experiment of Mr. Boffin's apparent corruption. Boffin begins collecting biographies of misers — Dancer, Elwes, Hopkins — and the novel lets us believe, for a long time, that wealth is doing what it always does. That it's grinding him down into a type — the miser, the hoarder, the man who loves gold more than people. The revelation that it was an elaborate performance — to test Bella, to educate her — is both Dickens's most sentimentally convenient plot device and his most ideologically revealing one. It reveals his anxiety: he wants the Boffins to be good — he needs good people to be able to survive wealth. But the very contrivance of the plot device shows how hard he has to work to make that survival plausible.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Bella Wilfer's arc is where I think the novel is doing its subtlest class work — and it's easily misread as a simple reform narrative. She begins the novel as someone who openly, almost defiantly, wants to marry for money. And Dickens doesn't immediately punish her for that — he gives her self-knowledge enough to know why she wants it. She's grown up watching her family's genteel poverty — the shabbiness, the pretension, the constant grating anxiety about keeping up appearances. Her desire for money is not greed — it's a rational response to having watched money's absence destroy dignity. The craft achievement is that Dickens makes her reform feel earned rather than imposed — she doesn't give up her desire for security, she learns to locate it in a person rather than a fortune. Though Daniel is right that the plot has to do a great deal of heavy lifting to deliver that conclusion.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Lammles are the obverse of the Bella story — and I think they're underrated as a class portrait. Two fortune hunters who each believed the other had money, married for it, and discovered on their honeymoon that neither of them has a penny. The scene on the beach at Shanklin — where they face each other and acknowledge the deception — is one of the most bleakly funny scenes in Victorian fiction. And their solution — to form a partnership in fraud, to exploit their social position to prey on others — is Dickens saying: this is what the system produces. People with the social performance of wealth but none of the substance — they have to become predators, because the alternative is social extinction. In a society organized entirely around the appearance of money, those who have only the appearance must use it ruthlessly or be destroyed by it.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The Lammles bring us to Fledgeby — and Fledgeby is, for my money, the novel's most purely sinister class figure. He's a usurer who hides behind Riah — an elderly Jewish man — using Riah's visible presence to absorb the anger and contempt that the moneylending business generates. The structure is precise: Fledgeby profits from human misery, but he makes a member of a persecuted minority take the reputational cost. He outsources the hatred that his own exploitation generates — and that is a very sophisticated picture of how class power actually functions. It doesn't just extract value from below — it deflects the resentment that extraction produces onto a scapegoat. Riah is one of Dickens's attempts to make amends for Fagin — to write a sympathetic Jewish character — and the characterization is genuinely dignified. But what's most powerful is the structural position Riah occupies: the face of exploitation that is not its beneficiary.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Eugene Wrayburn is the figure I want to end this strand on — because he's the class portrait that most resists easy reading. He's a barrister from the professional class who does nothing — who is sustained by family money while affecting total indifference to everything. His friendship with Mortimer Lightwood is one of the most affectionately drawn male partnerships in Victorian fiction. But Eugene's idleness is itself a class statement — it's what he can afford to feel, that nothing matters. His pursuit of Lizzie Hexam — a working-class woman — is morally ambiguous for most of the novel. Is it genuine feeling or is it the entitled predation of a man who assumes he can have what he wants? Dickens resolves it through near-death — Bradley Headstone's attack on Eugene by the river — which forces a kind of moral seriousness onto him. But the reader is left with the sense that Dickens couldn't quite work out whether Eugene deserved Lizzie — and that unease is the most honest thing about the ending.
Host (host)
Bradley Headstone — who is himself one of the most devastating portraits of class aspiration curdling into violence in all of Dickens. A schoolmaster who has clawed his way up from poverty and finds that the system still refuses to fully receive him. That particular story — the violence done by a meritocracy that admits you just far enough to make you despise where you came from — feels extraordinarily contemporary. James, I know you want to say something about Headstone before we move on.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Headstone is the novel's portrait of what the new elementary school system actually produced — and it's not flattering to those who designed it. The expansion of popular education in mid-Victorian England was real and significant — but the schools it created were instruments of social control as much as liberation. You were taught just enough to be useful — to keep accounts, to follow instructions, to respect authority. Headstone has been formed by that system — he is mechanically competent, desperately ambitious, and totally without the ease or cultural confidence that his class superiors take for granted. Dickens writes of him as wearing his decent black coat 'as if it were a disguise' — the clothes don't fit the man, and the class doesn't fit the clothes. He is a man permanently in costume, permanently performing a social position that the system sold to him as achievable and then refused to fully honor. The violence, when it comes, is overdetermined — it's what happens when aspiration is systematically thwarted and has nowhere to go but inward, and then outward.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And Charley Hexam — Bradley's prize pupil — is the even darker version of that story. Charley uses education to escape his class — and in doing so, turns on his own sister, on his own origins, with real contempt. He is ashamed of Lizzie. He is ashamed of the river and the dredging. He wants to be rid of everything that made him. And Dickens is merciless about this — he doesn't sentimentalize Charley's social climbing or let him off with good intentions. The system that formed Charley required him to despise the class he came from as the price of admission to the one above — and he paid it. That's the mechanism Dickens exposes most ferociously in this novel: class aspiration as a system for producing self-hatred and then weaponizing it.

We've covered an enormous amount of ground in this segment — from the Thames to the dust heaps, from Podsnappery to Bradley Headstone — and what strikes me is how the novel's anatomy of class is also an anatomy of psychology. Close Reading: The Dust Heaps as Social Truth (close_reading)

Host (host)
Welcome back to Our Mutual Friend Unpacked. We've been circling the dust heaps since the very first pages of this novel — those mounds of refuse outside London that Noddy Boffin inherits. And I want to slow right down now and ask what they actually are — not just as plot machinery, but as meaning. Eleanor, you've written about the symbolic architecture of Victorian fiction — where do the dust heaps sit in this novel's design?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
They sit right at the centre — structurally and symbolically — which is exactly where Dickens puts the things he most wants us to wrestle with. The dust heaps are introduced almost before we have characters to care about — they precede character, which is a remarkable inversion. Normally a novelist builds a world around people. Dickens builds people around a world of refuse. And what is in those heaps? Ash, cinders, bones, rags — the discarded matter of industrial London. Dickens understood — and this is the craft stroke I find breathtaking — that the heap is capital in its rawest, most undisguised form. Wealth as literal rubbish. The fortune that drives the entire plot is indistinguishable, in material terms, from what you throw away. That's not a metaphor Dickens stumbled into — that is a controlled, deliberate architectural choice sustained across the whole novel.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And it's historically precise, which is what gives the symbol its grip. Dust yards were a real and significant industry in mid-Victorian London — the sorting of household ash and refuse generated substantial fortunes. The dust was sifted for fine cinder that could be sold for brick-making, for bones rendered into fertiliser, for rags sold to paper mills. Harmon senior — old Boffin's original master — built his fortune entirely on this trade, and contemporaries would have recognised the type immediately. What Dickens adds — and here Eleanor is right about the craft — is the moral valence. He takes a real, well-understood economic phenomenon and turns it into an accusation. The Veneerings are building their drawing room on the same foundations as the dust heap — they just can't see the heap beneath the velvet.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James puts his finger on something essential — but I'd push it further. The dust heap isn't just a symbol of tainted wealth. It's Dickens's model of how value works under capitalism at large. Value is extracted from what others have discarded or cannot access — and the extractor stands apart, clean, respectable. Think of Silas Wegg installed atop the mound to guard it. That image is almost grotesquely comic — a man of no real power sitting on wealth he can never possess. He conspires to steal it, and his conspiracy is completely pathetic — the heap defeats him as surely as it defeated old Harmon. Dickens shows us that proximity to capital does not confer power. It confers corruption and delusion. And crucially, the people who actually sift through rubbish — the dustmen, the bone-pickers, Gaffer Hexam on the river — they remain outside the system of inheritance entirely.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The Wegg business is worth dwelling on from a craft angle, because Dickens uses it to do something structurally very bold. He gives us Boffin's apparent transformation into a miser — hoarding books about misers, becoming cruel to Bella — and it looks, for a time, as though the dust heap has contaminated its heir. The dust heap seems to be claiming another victim, just as it claimed old Harmon who died a bitter, isolated man. But then the revelation comes — it was performance all along. Boffin was protecting John Harmon and testing Bella. Now, some readers find that resolution too neat — a sentimental escape from the novel's own darkest logic. And I think they have a point — but I also think Dickens knows it, and buries his awareness of that escape in the texture of the prose around it. The heap isn't neatly resolved. It's sold, dispersed — but the money it generates keeps circulating through every relationship in the novel to the very last page.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The dispersal is the point, I think. It's what makes the heap more than a gothic set piece. In reality, when a dust contractor died, the yard was indeed sold and the proceeds divided — it was an estate like any other estate, legally speaking. The law of inheritance made no distinction between a fortune built on ash and a fortune built on land or cotton. And that legal indifference is precisely what the novel is indicting. Old Harmon's will — its conditions, its cruelties, its power over living people — is the law of property given a human face, and it is not a kind face. Dickens understood that the heap and the will together form a single machine — the machine for reproducing inequality across generations.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James's phrase — reproducing inequality across generations — is exactly right, and it brings me to what I think is the novel's most radical moment around the heap. When the dust heaps are finally sold and the money released, the people who laboured in and around them — the Boffins, Mr Venus, even Wegg in his deluded way — don't rise. The Boffins rise, but they do so precisely by remaining generous and essentially pre-capitalist in their instincts — they give the money away rather than accumulate it. Dickens can only imagine escape from the heap's logic through a kind of willed innocence — which is moving, but it is also a limitation of vision. The novel sees the problem with complete clarity and then retreats from it at the structural level — which is itself a form of social truth. It tells us what the system is, and it tells us — quietly, in the very shape of its resolution — that it cannot imagine the system otherwise.

And yet — and I want to hold onto this — Dickens does give us one image of genuine transformation around the dust heap that isn't sentimental. Host (host)

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first, every time I return to this novel, is how audaciously Dickens plants his central deception in plain sight. John Harmon is dead — or so the novel insists, and so the other characters believe — and yet here he is, walking the streets as John Rokesmith, secretary to the Boffins. The plot hinges on the will of old Harmon, which stipulates that John can only inherit the dust mounds if he marries Bella Wilfer — a woman he has never met. That is an extraordinary premise: a fortune that legally compels a marriage, treating a human being as a contractual condition of inheritance. Dickens uses John's self-narrated confession in the third book — the chapter called 'A Piece of Work' — to let us hear Harmon reason through his own doubled existence. He describes himself as a man who has been murdered, and yet lives — and I think that doubling is the beating heart of the entire novel's architecture.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right to focus on that will, because in legal terms it is a genuinely monstrous document. Conditional testamentary bequests of precisely this kind — wealth contingent on a named marriage — were entirely enforceable in mid-Victorian English law. The testator's power to reach beyond the grave and shape the living arrangements of his heirs was considered, in equity, an expression of property rights — not a violation of personal liberty. What Dickens captures with such unnerving precision is that Harmon senior has essentially purchased Bella Wilfer in advance — she just doesn't know it yet. And the novel never lets us forget the Wilfer family's near-destitution — Bella's desire for money is not vanity, it is survival, and the law has made her a commodity in someone else's estate.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
James has identified the mechanism, and I want to push on it — because what old Harmon's will reveals is that capital doesn't die with the capitalist. The dust mounds themselves are already a kind of congealed labour — the accumulated filth and waste of a city, transformed into a fortune. And now that fortune reaches forward through the law to govern the next generation — to determine who marries whom, who inherits, who is excluded. John Harmon's decision to disguise himself is, in one sense, a refusal of that inheritance — an attempt to step outside the system and evaluate it from the margins. But Dickens is too honest a novelist to let that refusal be clean — because Rokesmith still works for the Boffins, he is still inside the economy of the dustheap. He cannot simply opt out — the system has already inscribed him, and the novel knows this even when its hero does not.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)

James Blackstone (social_historian)

They said little else as they followed. He went on before them as an ugly Fate might have done, and they kept him in view, and would have been glad enough to lose sight of him. But on he went before them, always at the same distance, and the same rate. Aslant against the hard implacable weather and the rough wind, he was no more to be driven back than hurried forward, but held on like an advancing Destiny. There came, when they were about midway on their journey, a heavy rush of hail, which in a few minutes pelted the streets clear, and whitened them. It made no difference to him. A man’s life being to be taken and the price of it got, the hailstones to arrest the purpose must lie larger and deeper than those. He crashed through them, leaving marks in the fast-melting slush that were mere shapeless holes; one might have fancied, following, that the very fashion of humanity had departed from his feet.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And his death — locked together with Rogue Riderhood in the lock-keeper's weir — is one of the most terrifying endings in Victorian fiction. Two men who have been used and discarded by the same social machinery, destroying each other at the bottom of the water. There is no redemption there — only the logic of the system played out to its conclusion. And I think Dickens knew that ending was truer than any of the marriages and restorations in the final chapters. Great novelists are always more honest in their dark passages than in their happy ones.
