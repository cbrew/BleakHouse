Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The thing that strikes you immediately, reading Our Mutual Friend aloud — which I've done, in its entirety, more than once — is how much Dickens is enjoying himself. There's a glee to the darkness here that you don't quite get anywhere else. And the Boffins — Mr and Mrs Boffin — arrive in the novel as this magnificent comic gift. They are good people trying to become fashionable people, and the novel watches their failure with enormous affection. There's a moment — just a sentence, really — where Dickens delivers the whole verdict on them. Mr Boffin says to his wife: "Yes; and it's pleasant to know that you are Mrs Boffin, and it's been a pleasant thing to know this many and many a year!" And then Dickens adds — and this is the punchline — "It was ruin to Mrs Boffin's aspirations, but, having so spoken, they sat side by side, a hopelessly Unfashionable pair." That capital U on Unfashionable — Dickens puts it there deliberately. He's granting them a title — a better one, he implies, than any fashionable society could bestow.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto that word — trustworthy — because it's the hinge on which everything turns in this part of the novel. The old man — John Harmon Senior, the dust contractor, the dead Jailer of Harmony Jail as Dickens calls him — this man has spent his life treating everyone as purchasable. And the one thing his money cannot buy is the Boffins' integrity. Dickens is very precise about this. He writes that the old miser had perceived "the powerlessness of all his wealth to buy them if he had addressed himself to the attempt." That is an extraordinary sentence to find in a Victorian novel. Dickens is saying that the logic of capital — that everything has its price — has a limit. And what breaks that logic is not heroism or grand rebellion — it's just two faithful servants being decent people across many years. That's more subversive than it looks.

And what I love is that Eugene tells this story with complete affection for his father — he says so explicitly. He can't help being amused. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eugene is fascinating to me precisely because he knows all of this and does nothing with the knowledge. He sees the system perfectly — its absurdity, its arbitrariness — and his response is irony. A third brother pitched into the Navy because M. R. F. wasn't put out by the surprise of his early arrival. A mechanical genius decreed for the youngest when he was half an hour old. These are destinies assigned before the person exists. That is the class system in its purest form — identity as inheritance, personhood as property. And Dickens knew it — the man who wrote about the Boffins' inalienable decency knew exactly what it was that the system couldn't buy and couldn't plan for.

Yes — and Dickens doesn't soften it or hold it at arm's length. He gives you Gaffer Hexam hauling himself back into the boat, arms wet, hands dirty from a corpse in the river. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
This is where the novel stops being satirical and becomes something closer to indictment. Betty Higden is fleeing the workhouse — fleeing the Poor Law system — and she's dying as she does it. And Dickens imagines the river itself speaking to her — not as a threat, but as an offer. The passage describes the young river above the city — still clean, still playful — and then the voice it speaks with. The Relieving Officer — that's the workhouse official, the embodiment of institutional cruelty — being answered by death itself, which promises to do the job more mercifully. Dickens is saying, with cold fury, that Victorian welfare had made death preferable to charity.
