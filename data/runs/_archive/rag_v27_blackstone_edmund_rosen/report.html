Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
The fog is the first and most sustained piece of prose in the novel, and one ought simply to read it before theorising about it. Dickens opens with a description so relentless, so technically accomplished, that it functions first as an aesthetic experience — before it functions as anything else. The fog is everywhere, and it is specific — it is in the Essex marshes, it is in the Kentish heights, it is on the river, it is in the court. Dickens writes — and I think this sentence is worth attending to carefully — "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping." The word 'defiled' is doing extraordinary work there — this is not neutral weather, but Dickens is a novelist, not a pamphleteer. He does not tell us what the fog means. He shows us the fog, and trusts us to feel it.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right that the fog is a technical achievement — but I think it helps to know where Dickens actually places you by the end of that opening page. The fog settles, and the novel settles with it, at the doors of the High Court of Chancery. Now — Chancery in 1852 was not simply a slow court. It was a court in which cases could last longer than a human lifetime. The costs were structured such that the longer a case ran, the more the lawyers earned — and the parties, meanwhile, were simply consumed. And this is why the figure of Tom Jarndyce — who appears very early, through Krook's account of him — is so historically resonant. Krook quotes him directly, and the passage is devastating in its precision. "For, says he, it's being ground to bits in a slow mill; it's being roasted at a slow fire; it's being stung to death by single bees; it's being drowned by drops; it's going mad by grains." Five separate metaphors for the same experience — and every one of them describes something incremental, something that kills you not all at once but grain by grain. That is not a literary device. That is a precise description of what Chancery did.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me about that passage from Krook is the detail that Tom Jarndyce went about warning the little shopkeepers — not the barristers, not the clerks, but the small people drawn into the court's orbit. He knows who the system's victims will be. He knows because he is becoming one. And Dickens is very careful about this — Chancery does not destroy everyone equally. The lawyers thrive. Mr. Tulkinghorn thrives. Mr. Vholes will thrive. The institution produces winners as surely as it produces losers. Now — Edmund will say the fog is simply a literary device, and I respect that reading enormously. But consider what the fog specifically obscures. Consider what cannot be seen through it. There is a moment early in Esther's narration that I keep returning to — when Krook writes out the letters J-A-R-N-D-Y-C-E, and then writes Bleak House, and then laughs. "These, in some astonishment, I also read; and he laughed again." Esther cannot yet read what she is reading. She recognises the words, but not the weight of them. And Krook laughs — because he, the illiterate rag-and-bone man who has taught himself letters by watching the court next door, understands the system better than its victims do. The fog, for me, is what the powerful maintain around the mechanisms of their power — not accidental, not atmospheric, but structural.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Lord Chancellor's line, when we first encounter it in Esther's narration, is almost painfully mundane for something so consequential. He is turning over leaves — the leaves of a file, presumably — and he says, without ceremony: "The Jarndyce in question is Jarndyce of Bleak House." That sentence contains three human lives — Ada's, Richard's, Esther's — and it is delivered as a bureaucratic identification. This is the historical texture Dickens gets absolutely right. Power, in institutions, speaks in exactly this tone — flat, referential, without affect. The fog has a voice, and it sounds like that.

Almost fond is precisely right, and I think that precision matters enormously. What strikes me in all of this is how successfully the aristocratic world insulates itself — not through cruelty, exactly, but through elaborate systems of managed distance. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Refusal is the right word. And it sits in permanent, uncomfortable tension with Jarndyce's generosity. Because generosity — private, individual, beautiful as it is — cannot close the gap that the system keeps reopening. And there's a figure in this novel who embodies that gap with terrible precision — and it isn't Gridley, and it isn't Richard. It is Tulkinghorn. Sitting in his locked room, in the dusk, with his sealing-wax and his secrets. Dickens gives us a description of that room that is, I would argue, the novel's most precise image of how power actually operates. He writes — and the detail is everything: "Everything that can have a lock has got one; no key is visible." Eight words. And they describe not just a room — but an entire structure of concealment. The genius of Bleak House is that Dickens knew this, and could not quite bring himself to say it was irremediable — but the evidence he assembled says it for him.
