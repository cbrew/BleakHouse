Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What Dickens does in those opening pages is almost brutal in its economy. We see Gaffer Hexam haul himself back into the boat, arms wet and dirty, and we're told he held something in his right hand and washed that in the river too. It was money. Two words, placed alone — and they carry the weight of everything that follows. And then Dickens gives us the ritual. It's a dark parody of a blessing, performed over coins taken from a dead body. Dickens is showing us, before the first chapter is done, that in this world money and death are inseparable.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That dignity point is important, Edmund, and I don't want to flatten it. But the novel is equally interested in what the respectable world looks like from the other end — and that is where Twemlow becomes so devastating. The Veneerings represent the new money, and Dickens introduces their world through a piece of furniture. Not metaphorically — literally. Twemlow, a man of good birth and thin means, is described as, and I'm reading verbatim here: "an innocent piece of dinner-furniture that went upon easy castors and was kept over a livery stable-yard in Duke Street, Saint James's, when not in use." Stored like a table when not required — that is what aristocratic connection means in the Veneering economy. And then the metaphor extends with horrible precision. "Being first cousin to Lord Snigsworth, he was in frequent requisition, and at many houses might be said to represent the dining-table in its normal state." The further Twemlow is pulled out to accommodate more guests, the further he finds himself from the centre. Social use and social exclusion operating by the same mechanism — that is Dickens's point, and it is made through geometry.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that question of where goodness lands — whether poverty ennobles or degrades — runs right through to a remarkable scene involving Bella Wilfer. Bella has refused a wealthy match and returned to her father in a plain dress — and her father, the Cherub, responds with one of the novel's great set-pieces. He calls her 'this mercenary young person distantly related to myself' — and the phrase recurs almost like a liturgical response. Here is the heart of it: "I admire this mercenary young person distantly related to myself, more in this dress than if she had come to me in China silks, Cashmere shawls, and Golconda diamonds." And the blessing that follows is extraordinary in its directness: "she brings you a good fortune when she brings you the poverty she has accepted for your sake and the honest truth's." Poverty as fortune — Dickens inverts the novel's central currency and asks us to take it seriously. Whether that inversion is radical or consoling is a question the novel never quite settles.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor puts her finger on something important, and I want to linger on it. What Dickens is describing is a character in whom the capacity for trust has been almost entirely destroyed by upbringing. The deception is not cynicism — it is a form of terrified caution in a man who has only ever seen money do harm. And Dickens frames the dilemma with the precision of a moralist. Harmon asks himself whether it is for him to have, as the text says, 'the shame of buying her, or the meanness of punishing her.' Those two words — shame and meanness — are perfectly chosen and morally distinct. Shame attaches to the buyer; meanness attaches to the man who uses power to wound. Harmon is caught between two forms of moral failure, and Dickens refuses to let him off either horn of the dilemma.

Edmund is right about the moral precision, and I don't want to flatten that. But I think we have to notice what Harmon's scheme actually is, at the level of social practice.
