Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me immediately about this passage is how Gaskell distributes the cause of suffering across three separate forces almost simultaneously. There is the life itself — its rhythm, so unlike Helstone — then the air, physically described as deprived of all revivifying principle, and then the domestic worries pressing in their new and sordid form. Gaskell builds her case with the patient accumulation of a barrister, not a sentimentalist. And then she earns the emotional revelation — Margaret slipping into her mother's room and catching something almost unbearable. She caught a few words which were evidently a prayer for strength and patience to endure severe bodily suffering. That word endure is doing enormous work — not relief, not cure, simply endurance. Gaskell understands that the specific word in a prayer is a kind of diagnosis.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right about the accumulation, and I'd add that what Gaskell is really doing here is establishing the terms of Margaret's moral education. Margaret does not despair — she plans. She lies awake, as Gaskell tells us, devising practical remedies — a servant, relief for Dixon, the reorganisation of the household. That is the English tradition of active virtue — not lamentation, but application of the will to circumstance. What I find more interesting, however, is the broken bond between mother and daughter — Margaret yearned, as Gaskell says, to re-unite the bond of intimate confidence. There is something almost tragic in that phrasing — re-unite, as though a rupture has already occurred, long before illness arrives.

I think that passage reveals something more fundamental than a calibrated compass — it reveals Margaret's understanding of what gentility actually requires. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
There is a temptation — and I have seen it yielded to in any number of critical accounts — to read the ending as a resolution of the North-South dialectic. Margaret represents Southern sensibility, Thornton the Northern industrial ethic — they marry, synthesis is achieved, full stop. I think this is far too neat — and Gaskell is far too honest a novelist to give us that. What she gives us instead is two people who have been genuinely changed by their collision with each other — who have earned the right to silence together. The social questions remain open. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I think that's the mark of Gaskell's seriousness as an artist — she doesn't resolve what cannot honestly be resolved. Margaret's journey is not from error to correction — it's from rigidity to complexity. She doesn't abandon her values — she discovers that they were always more entangled than she allowed.
