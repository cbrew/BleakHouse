Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The thing that strikes me immediately about how Dickens introduces the Dust Heap fortune is that he never lets it be merely picturesque. The Mounds are always in motion — being dug into, surveilled, schemed over — they are active participants in the plot. And then there is Wegg reading out old Harmon's will, this document that sets everything in train, and the language of it is extraordinary. "Inasmuch as he has never made friends, and has ever had a rebellious family, he, John Harmon, gives to Nicodemus Boffin the Little Mound, which is quite enough for him, and gives the whole rest and residue of his property to the Crown." That phrase — quite enough for him — is a dead man reaching across the grave to deliver one final, withering verdict on a man he apparently despised. Dickens compresses an entire relationship — and an entire social philosophy — into six words. That is the architecture I find endlessly arresting in this novel: the legal instrument that is also a character study.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right to focus on that phrase, and the legal form matters enormously here as well. In mid-Victorian England, a man of property had near-absolute testamentary freedom — he could leave his estate to whomever he wished, on whatever terms, with relatively little constraint. So old Harmon's decision to leave the bulk to the Crown — rather than to any person — is not legally eccentric, but it is socially devastating. It is an act of contempt dressed in legal language — the most respectable possible costume for spite. And what Dickens understands, which many novelists would miss, is that the document outlives its author and goes on making mischief long after he is in the ground.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I want to add is that the will is only the beginning of the extortion economy that Dickens builds in these scenes. Wegg doesn't just find the will — he immediately converts it into a weapon, a piece of leverage in what is frankly a blackmail operation. And Dickens describes Wegg's scheme with this extraordinary mock-parliamentary rhetoric — all those repeated 'That's — as though extortion could be dressed up as a resolution. Listen to the passage where Wegg names his price and then elaborates his plan for Boffin. "He considered it plain that such price was stateable in a single expressive word, and that the word was, 'Halves!'" And then, if Boffin attempts to take anything at all, the plan is that he should be — and these are Dickens's words — "kept in a state of abject moral bondage and slavery until the time when they should see fit to permit him to purchase his freedom at the price of half his possessions." Bondage. Slavery. Dickens uses those words deliberately, and they are not metaphors he deploys lightly. What Wegg is constructing is a miniature version of every exploitative relationship in the novel — with the moral vocabulary of freedom inverted to describe a purchase price.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Which brings us, rather beautifully, to the opposite pole of the novel's moral universe — R. Wilfer blessing his daughter's engagement. Where Wegg's language is procedural and self-serving, Wilfer's is entirely different — tender, almost liturgical. He calls Bella this 'mercenary young person distantly related to myself' — he is teasing, but with enormous love underneath the irony. And then he delivers this remarkable speech in which he says, in effect, that poverty freely chosen is the only true fortune. "My blessing on this engagement betwixt you, and she brings you a good fortune when she brings you the poverty she has accepted for your sake and the honest truth's!" In a novel obsessed with money, that sentence is almost shocking in its directness. Dickens is not sentimentalising poverty — he knows exactly what it costs — but he is insisting that there is a kind of integrity that no dust heap can purchase.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What he was returning to was the wreckage of a profoundly cruel household — and Dickens gives us that wreckage through Boffin's testimony, which is almost like a witness statement. Old Harmon is depicted not merely as miserly or eccentric but as physically violent — toward the people who tried to protect his own son. Boffin recounts the scene to Mortimer Lightwood, and the language is magnificently blunt: "I have seen him, after Mrs Boffin has given him her mind respecting the claims of the nat'ral affections, catch off Mrs Boffin's bonnet and send it spinning across the yard... and received flush on the temple. Which dropped her, Mr Lightwood. Dropped her." That repeated, bare clause — 'Dropped her' — is Boffin's version of legal evidence. He is testifying to a violence so matter-of-fact in that household that it requires no elaboration — just the plain fact, stated twice. And historically this is entirely credible: a wealthy patriarch in mid-Victorian England had near-absolute authority over his dependants, and the law offered very little remedy.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What strikes me is that this violence has a specific class logic. The Boffins are servants — utterly dependent on old Harmon — and yet they repeatedly intervene on behalf of the boy, at real cost to themselves. That's not just personal virtue — it's a structural inversion: the people with the least power are the ones exercising the most moral agency. And Dickens will build on this foundation later — because when Harmon returns and John Rokesmith moves into the Boffin household, he is watching to see whether this goodness is real or whether wealth has corrupted it. The disguise, in other words, is not just a plot mechanism — it's an ethical test administered by a man who has learned, from bitter experience, not to trust inherited wealth.

Yes — because this moment is one of the most quietly devastating pieces of dramatic irony in the whole novel. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the letter itself, when we read it, is a masterclass in how Victorian charity actually functioned — or rather, how it failed to. The steward system that Dickens satirises here was entirely real — and genuinely rotten. Charitable dinners in the mid-Victorian period were structured so that aristocratic names on the letterhead attracted wealthy donors, who paid a fee for the privilege of calling themselves Stewards. The money raised was largely consumed by the cost of the dinner itself — and by administrative overhead that kept a small professional class employed. Dickens nails it with that postscript — "The Steward's fee is limited to three Guineas" — because the postscript is where the transaction is finally revealed for what it is. And then the novel quietly notes that this intimate personal appeal was, in fact, lithographed by the hundred. The gap between the warmth of the address and the industrial scale of its production is precisely the gap between Victorian charity's self-image and its reality.
