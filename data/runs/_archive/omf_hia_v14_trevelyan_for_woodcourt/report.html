Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That structural contrast is deliberate and I think it's one of Dickens's most brilliant architectural decisions in this novel. He sets up two economies at once: the economy of actual survival — bodies, river, money scraped from the dead — and the economy of social performance, which is just as predatory but conducted with silver cutlery. And the Veneerings are perhaps the purest expression of that second economy. They are, famously, new — everything about them is new, right down to their friends. And Dickens finds a perfect instrument for puncturing them in Lady Tippins, who campaigns for Veneering's parliamentary seat while openly admitting the whole enterprise is a charade.

It's worth pausing on just how accurate that satire is, historically speaking. Pocket-Breaches is a rotten borough — the kind of constituency where a seat in Parliament really could be purchased, and everyone involved knew it, and everyone pretended otherwise. Dickens puts that pretence directly in Lady Tippins's mouth, and the effect is startling because she doesn't even bother to pretend she's pretending. And Dickens earns the right to that satirical cruelty, I think, because he also gives us figures who are genuinely morally serious — and does it without sentimentality. The Boffins are the great example of that. Mr. and Mrs. Boffin are ignorant, unpolished, entirely without social pretension — and yet they are the moral centre of the novel's universe. It does — and notice the capitalisation. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The advice Boffin gives her is not — and this is important — simply the opinion of a corrupt man. It was, by any rational calculation available to a young woman of Bella's situation in the 1860s, the correct strategy for survival. She has no profession. She has no capital. She has beauty and she has proximity to the Boffins' money — and both are, legally speaking, time-limited assets. When Dickens writes her staring at 'a dark cloud of suspicion, covetousness, and conceit, overshadowing the once open face' of Boffin, he's asking us to feel the horror of watching a good man corrode.
