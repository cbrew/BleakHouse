Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every single time is how Dickens refuses to give us a sentence in that first paragraph. He just gives us the noun, twice, like a hammer blow. The text reads — and I want to read it carefully because the rhythm is doing everything here — "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." That parenthetical — "and dirty" — is almost cheeky, and it arrives like a tiny sardonic cough in the middle of something tremendous. But notice what he's doing structurally: the fog is moving, accumulating, colonising every possible space. Up river, down river, out to the marshes, in among the ships — and then, crucially, into the bodies of people. The fog gets into the eyes and throats of the Greenwich pensioners, into the stem of a skipper's pipe, onto the fingers of a shivering apprentice boy. It's a brilliant architectural move — the paragraph enacts the thing it's describing.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Eleanor is right about the architectural quality, though I'd hesitate before we reach too quickly for the word 'structural.' What I find more remarkable is that Dickens begins not with a character, not with an event, but with a condition. The fog is the moral atmosphere of the world we are entering. And observe how quickly he connects it to an institution. The passage I find most powerful comes shortly after, when he turns the metaphor back on itself — listen to the balance of those clauses: "Never can there come fog too thick, never can there come mud and mire too deep, to assort with the groping and floundering condition which this High Court of Chancery, most pestilent of hoary sinners, holds this day in the sight of heaven and earth." "Most pestilent of hoary sinners" — that is not social criticism dressed as literature. That is a moral indictment, in the tradition of the Old Testament prophets. Dickens is not merely diagnosing an institution — he is pronouncing judgment upon it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I wouldn't quarrel with a word of that — and yet I'd gently enter a caution. It would be a mistake to read this as primarily a tract about legal reform. Dickens knew perfectly well that Chancery reform was already underway when the novel was serialised in 1852. Chancery is doing the work that a symbol does — it is a concentrating lens for human weakness, human greed, human passivity. The fog does not excuse Richard Carstone. It tests him.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Yes, and it is a passage that rewards very careful attention, because Esther's judgment here is unusually precise — precise in a way that Dickens rarely announced so openly. The system, as Esther describes it, treated Richard as it treated every other boy — addressed him, she says, in exactly the same manner as hundreds of others. And the consequence was not failure — quite the reverse. Richard dashed through his tasks with fair credit and often with distinction — which is precisely the danger. Then Dickens gives us this magnificent formulation, and I think one must hear it properly to feel its weight. "They were good qualities, without which no high place can be meritoriously won, but like fire and water, though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." Fire and water — excellent servants, very bad masters. That is the whole of Richard's tragedy contained in fourteen words. His qualities are real — Dickens insists on that — but they are in command where they ought to be commanded.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And what I find structurally brilliant about that passage is the way it sets up the whole novel's engine. Dickens is not blaming Richard, not exactly. He is placing the origin of the flaw outside Richard — in the system — and then watching what happens when that flaw meets Chancery. Chancery is itself a system that addresses everyone in exactly the same manner — indifferently, endlessly, to ruinous effect. So one deficient system produces a damaged person, and then a second system finishes the destruction. That's the architecture — and it is merciless.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take your point about the systems, Eleanor, though I would resist the implication that Richard is simply a victim of forces beyond him.
