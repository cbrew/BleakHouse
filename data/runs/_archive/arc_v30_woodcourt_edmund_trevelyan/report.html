Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
It is, and I think the audacity is often misread. People reach for the word 'symbolic' almost immediately — the fog stands for the law, the mud stands for corruption, and so on. But before it is symbolic of anything, it is a piece of prose written with extraordinary physical precision. Consider what Dickens does in the very first sentences — no verbs, almost no grammar, just nouns and weather accumulating like the mud itself. And then, without warning, he gives us this: "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." An extinct creature, walking through the streets of London as if perfectly at home. That is not a political metaphor — that is a novelist trusting the sheer strangeness of the world he is rendering.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to resist slightly the idea that this opening is primarily about the law. The fog descends upon everyone — the foot passengers jostling umbrellas, the horses splashed to their blinkers. Dickens is drawing a picture of human life as such — prone to confusion, to slipping, to ill temper. The Chancery is one institution caught in that fog — but it is not the fog itself.

That's a generous reading of the Court of Chancery, Edmund. Dickens, rather brilliantly, gives us his own view of the law's purpose a few hundred pages later — and he's not in a forgiving mood.
