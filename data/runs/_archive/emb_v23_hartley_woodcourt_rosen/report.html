Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to that first page is the sheer structural audacity of it. Dickens doesn't open with a character, or a plot, or even a complete sentence in the conventional sense. He opens with a noun: "London." Full stop. Then a series of verbless fragments — "Implacable November weather" — that create this sense of a world assembling itself out of chaos. And then comes this extraordinary sentence — one of the most thrilling opening gambits in all of fiction. "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A dinosaur. Waddling up Holborn Hill. In that single image, Dickens collapses geological time — he's saying London is as primordial as the earth itself, and the law that governs it just as ancient and just as monstrous.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eleanor is right about the geological time — but I'd push that reading one step further. The mud isn't just atmosphere. It's accumulation. Dickens uses the phrase "compound interest" — foot passengers adding "new deposits to the crust upon crust of mud, sticking at those points tenaciously, and accumulating at compound interest." That is a deliberately economic metaphor in the very first paragraph of a novel about inheritance and the law. The mud compounds. The case compounds. The suffering compounds. And the people at the bottom are crushed under the accumulated weight of it.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What gets me — and I've read this passage five times, as you said — is the fog. Dickens gives it this extraordinary repetitive incantation. Listen to the rhythm of it. "Fog everywhere. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline has named something crucial — that fog reaches everyone. Dickens moves the fog systematically through social strata — from the Essex marshes to Greenwich pensioners wheezing by their firesides, down to the shivering apprentice boy on deck. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And from the fog, Dickens moves us directly into Chancery — and this is where the novel declares its true subject. The Lord High Chancellor sitting in his foggy glory — that phrase alone — "a foggy glory" — is doing enormous work. The court is wrapped in the same fog as the streets, the same fog as the marshes, the same fog as the wretched apprentice boy. But the Chancellor's fog is crimson-curtained and upholstered. It is power dressed up as dignity. Then Dickens delivers what I consider the most devastating sentence in the opening — and one of the most devastating in Victorian literature. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." Every shire. Every madhouse. Every churchyard. Dickens is mapping the total geographic reach of this institution — and its total human cost. This isn't satire in the gentle sense. This is indictment.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I love about that passage — and Daniel, you've put your finger on the argument — is the structural audacity of the sentence itself. Dickens folds the satire directly into the syntax: the crowd is the grammatical object of the sentence, and the law is an obstacle clause that interrupts its movement. The sentence enacts what it describes — the crowd trying to move forward, the law throwing itself in the way. And then the pivot to the street mud — that's not decoration, that's equivalence. The law and the mud are kindred mysteries: both made of nobody knows what, both accumulating around us, both requiring periodic removal when they become unbearable. It's a metaphor that keeps its irony absolutely straight-faced, which makes it so much more devastating than a rant.

That tenderness is real — and it reaches its fullest expression in the Jo passages, which I think are among the most politically charged pages Dickens ever wrote. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The rhetorical form of that passage is remarkable — it's an anaphora, all those repeated "It must be" and "To" constructions, stacking impossible conditions one on another. Each "To" clause opens a new window onto what literacy means — to read, to write, to understand the postman, to follow what happens in a church. And Jo is systematically shut out of every single one. Eleanor is absolutely right about the word choice, and I want to push it one step further. Because what Dickens is doing at Chesney Wold is more than Gothic atmosphere — it's a reckoning. He's showing us that the aristocratic order doesn't collapse dramatically — it just quietly rots.
