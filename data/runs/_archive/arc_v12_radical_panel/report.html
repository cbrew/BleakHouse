Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Thank you — and yes, rage is exactly the right word, though Dickens disguises it as comedy. There's a passage in Chapter 39 that I think is one of the most devastating pieces of institutional satire in all of Victorian fiction. Dickens is describing how the law protects itself from reform — not through force, but through the invocation of respectability. Mr. Kenge is addressing a client who has the temerity to suggest that an unjust law might be repealed. "Alter this law, sir, and what will be the effect of your rash proceeding on a class of practitioners very worthily represented, allow me to say to you, by the opposite attorney in the case, Mr. Vholes?" And then comes the verdict, delivered with perfect parliamentary dignity: "Sir, that class of practitioners would be swept from the face of the earth." The social system, Kenge tells us, cannot afford to lose men like Mr. Vholes. That phrase — the social system — is doing enormous ideological work. It converts the interests of a parasitic profession into a universal necessity.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel, I won't quarrel with the satirical target — Dickens's contempt for Chancery is entirely earned and brilliantly executed. But I think what makes the Kenge passage so extraordinary is not the politics — it's the characterisation. Kenge is not a villain in the melodramatic sense. He genuinely believes what he is saying. That is the moral horror Dickens is pointing to — not corruption as venality, but corruption as sincere self-deception. The parliamentary inquiry that follows — with its mock question numbers running into the hundreds of thousands — is a masterpiece of comic timing that I think no amount of political annotation quite captures.

And what Dickens shows us — with extraordinary precision — is how that respectability functions as a shield protecting the entire apparatus. But I want to turn to a passage that shows the human cost of this system at its most devastating. In Chapter 24, Esther is watching Richard prepare to depart — and through his farewell words she hears echoes of Gridley, who has just died. Gridley — the man from Shropshire, consumed by Chancery — has spent his entire life in litigation. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
There's one more passage I want to bring in before we move on — and it opens the question of class in a very different register. In Chapter 55, Mrs. Rouncewell — the housekeeper at Chesney Wold — pleads with Lady Dedlock to speak whatever truth she knows, to help save her son George from an unjust accusation. What Dickens gives us is a woman of the serving class speaking directly across the vast social distance that separates her from her mistress. And she does so with absolute moral authority. Listen to how she addresses Lady Dedlock: "My Lady, you may have some proud or angry reasons for disdaining to utter something that you know; if so, pray, oh, pray, think of a faithful servant whose whole life has been passed in this family which she dearly loves, and relent, and help to clear my son!" That repetition — "pray, oh, pray" — is not melodrama. It is a woman deploying the only rhetorical resource available to her — sincerity, stripped of all social pretension.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Caroline is right about the imagery — but I want to resist the idea that this is simply fate acting on a flawed young man. Jarndyce's advice is admirable. But consider what system surrounds Richard the moment he steps out of that sunlit room. Chancery is not an unfortunate quirk. It is an institution designed to circulate money upward and defer justice indefinitely. Richard doesn't fall because he lacks constancy. I take your point about narrative justice — and I confess that closing image of Tulkinghorn is one of the finest things in the book. But I'd note that Dickens does not simply condemn and move on. The novel is full of figures who act well — Esther, Jarndyce, Allan Woodcourt — within the wreckage of the system. Nemo's gift to Jo — that supper, that lodging — is an act of private grace that the system cannot explain and cannot replicate. And that, I think, is where Dickens places whatever hope there is — not in reform, but in the human capacity for decency within systems designed to crush it.
