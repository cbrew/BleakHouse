I can remember, when I was a very little girl indeed, I used to say to my doll when we were alone together, “Now, Dolly, I am not clever, you know very well, and you must be patient with me, like a dear!” And so she used to sit propped up in a great arm-chair, with her beautiful complexion and rosy lips, staring at me—or not so much at me, I think, as at nothing—while I busily stitched away and told her every one of my secrets.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What stays with me changes each time. That's the mark of the great ones. But this time — this particular reading — it's Jarndyce. John Jarndyce, who loves Esther, who proposes to her, who then — without bitterness — gives her to the man she truly loves. He arranges the whole thing in secret. He furnishes the second Bleak House for them. He just — lets her go. Dickens almost never writes goodness without making it slightly comic or slightly daft. Jarndyce is the exception. He is just — good. Quietly, practically, self-effacingly good. And in a novel full of grotesques and monsters, that almost breaks you.
