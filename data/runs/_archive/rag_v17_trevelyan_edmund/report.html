Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
He's doing something quite radical for 1852, and I think we still underestimate how radical it is. The novel opens in a grammatically headless present tense — no subject, no verb in the conventional sense, just noun phrases accumulating like the fog itself. 'Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping.' There is no narrator yet in the grammatical sense — no one standing outside the fog, reporting on it. The fog is the narrator, or rather, the fog and the omniscient narrative voice are the same thing — both enveloping, both withholding. And then Dickens does something even more extraordinary — he places the Lord Chancellor at the very heart of the fog, on his High Court throne. The fog isn't decorative atmosphere. It is the novel's central argument, made visible before a single character has spoken.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take Eleanor's point about the structural daring — and I would not diminish it. But I am always slightly wary of reducing that fog to a single thesis. Dickens is doing something older than social commentary there — he is constructing a moral landscape, in the tradition of the great eighteenth-century satirists. Think of Johnson's London — the city as a place where virtue is obscured, where the honest man can barely see his hand before his face. What Jarndyce says about the Chancery properties bears this out with devastating plainness. 'It is a street of perishing blind houses, with their eyes stoned out, without a pane of glass, without so much as a window-frame, with the bare blank shutters tumbling from their hinges and falling asunder.' That is not a metaphor for an institution. That is a moral verdict on what happens when human greed and legal indifference are left unchecked for generations. The fog and those blind houses belong to the same imaginative world — a world where clarity, sight, and honest reckoning have been systematically destroyed.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that lethal quality extends to the portraits of the powerful — not just the criminal classes. Tulkinghorn is perhaps the novel's most frightening figure precisely because he is so understated — no theatrical villainy, just that terrible watchfulness. Dickens describes him in a way that's almost clinical — 'In manner, close and dry. In voice, husky and low. In face, watchful behind a blind.' Three parallel fragments, each stripped to its barest elements. There's no sentence there in the grammatical sense — just the man, reduced to attributes. It's the same technique as the fog opening — Dickens withholds the full syntactic package because these figures resist being fully known. The prose enacts the secrecy it's describing. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think that passage on Tulkinghorn also contains one of the novel's great dry jokes — one that rewards a careful second reading. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Yes, though I should like to resist the implication that Richard is simply a victim of Chancery. Jarndyce's advice is not merely prudential — it is moral. He tells the young people: all that Richard had to start him in the race of life has been ventured. That is not the language of a system devouring a passive innocent — that is a judgment on a young man who has made choices. Dickens understood, with the clarity of a moralist, that the suit becomes dangerous only when Richard gives it his will. There is a passage I keep returning to — Bucket, in chapter fifty-four, waiting to deliver devastating news to Sir Leicester. The narrator describes him as: "A famous whist-player for a large stake — say a hundred guineas certain — with the game in his hand, but with a high reputation involved in his playing his hand out to the last card in a masterly way." Now, that image of the card-player — masterly, controlled, never anxious — is the image Richard cannot inhabit. Bucket plays his hand to the last card with professional composure. Richard, by contrast, bets everything on a suit he cannot control — and cannot stop playing.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Oliver's right that the sympathy keeps shifting, and the dual narration is doing a lot of that work. Esther narrates Richard's decline with a sort of anguished, helpless tenderness — she never accuses him directly. There's a quiet passage late in the novel — chapter fifty — where Esther is arranging Caddy Jellyby's sick-room, making everything neat, making everything habitable. And after all that careful tending, Esther tells Caddy about Bleak House — about her future life — with a simplicity that reads as almost unbearably hard-won. The contrast with Richard's story at that same point in the novel is crushing. Esther builds — she arranges, she tends, she makes things cheerful against the odds. Richard dismantles — himself, his prospects, his love. And Dickens never underlines it — the comparison is structural, invisible in the architecture, but absolutely deliberate.

I grant Miss Hartley her architecture — it is beautiful and it is damning. But I think we should be careful not to let our sympathy for Richard excuse what Dickens shows us with great plainness. The Sir Leicester passages in this novel serve, I've always thought, as a kind of dark mirror to Richard. What I've always loved — and it only really hits you when you perform it — is the way Dickens describes his movement between worlds. Here — this passage from Chapter 42 — is as close to pure prose music as Dickens ever gets: "He walks into Chesney Wold as if it were next door to his chambers and returns to his chambers as if he had never been out of Lincoln's Inn Fields." The parallelism is perfect — 'as if... as if' — the same grammatical ghost on each side. And then — 'He melted out of his turret-room this morning, just as now, in the late twilight, he melts into his own square.' 'Melts.' Not walks, not arrives — melts. That single verb tells you everything about Tulkinghorn's nature — a man with no volume, no footprint, no humanity you can locate. He doesn't enter spaces — he infiltrates them.

Oliver's point about the verb 'melts' leads me directly to the other passage I can't stop thinking about — Mrs. Rouncewell and her son George. Because if Tulkinghorn represents language emptied of all feeling, this passage is its absolute opposite. Dickens gives us a woman who cannot speak — who dares not speak — and so he transfers all her emotion into her hands. Listen to what those hands say: "They speak of gratitude, of joy, of grief, of hope; of inextinguishable affection, cherished with no return since this stalwart man was a stripling." That catalogue — gratitude, joy, grief, hope — Dickens piles it up without apology, because the moment demands excess. But the killer phrase is 'cherished with no return' — all those years of love given into absence.
