Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Thank you — and what a novel to begin with, if you're going to talk about narrative architecture. Dickens is doing something structurally radical in Bleak House that I don't think he'd attempted before at this scale. Two narrators — the omniscient third-person voice and Esther Summerson in the first person — alternating throughout the entire novel. And the two voices aren't just different stylistically — they're doing entirely different emotional work. The omniscient narrator is where the anger lives — the Gothic darkness, the political fury, the lamentation. There's a passage I keep returning to — the death of the man known only as Nemo, watched in the dark as the Harmonic Meeting ends. Dickens writes: "Oh, if in brighter days the now-extinguished fire within him ever burned for one woman who held him in her heart, where is she, while these ashes are above the ground!" That exclamation mark is doing the work of an entire argument about anonymity and loss and the cruelty of social invisibility. He doesn't give Nemo a name, a history, a face — and then he mourns, with extraordinary tenderness, that nobody will mourn him.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I find so remarkable is how Dickens gets you to feel implicated in all of this. Snagsby is the everyman — respectable, decent, frightened — and we're walking beside him into the infernal gulf. There's no comfortable distance. Dickens won't let you be a tourist. And against all of that darkness — the slum, the dead man with no name — there's Richard Carstone, who is a different kind of ruin in the making. He has this moment of extraordinary self-awareness, confessing to Esther — and it's almost unbearable because he can see himself so clearly and change nothing. He says to her: "I wish to heaven I were a more constant sort of fellow... If I were a more constant sort of fellow, I should have held on either to Badger or to Kenge and Carboy like grim death, and should have begun to be steady and systematic by this time, and shouldn't be in debt, and—" And the sentence just — stops. That's Dickens at his most quietly devastating — not the great set-piece of Tom-all-Alone's, just a young man trailing off into a dash.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That dash is doing so much work, isn't it — it's a kind of syntactic embodiment of Richard's inconstancy. And it's narrated by Esther, which gives it yet another layer — she loves him, she sees the danger, and she reproduces his words exactly so that we can judge for ourselves. The Esther voice is the novel's great act of restraint — she very rarely editorialises, and that silence is its own form of commentary. And then there's the other pole entirely — Tulkinghorn, who is all control, all threat, all terrifying stillness. When he stops Lady Dedlock from leaving the room, the narration is almost forensic — he doesn't raise his voice, he doesn't move a hand or foot. He simply says: "Lady Dedlock, have the goodness to stop and hear me, or before you reach the staircase I shall ring the alarm-bell and rouse the house." The politeness of "have the goodness" beside that absolute threat — it's the grammar of power, and it's chilling.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
I want to start with Lady Dedlock, because the passage about her in Chapter Two is one of the great comic set-pieces in the whole novel — and it's almost invisible as comedy. The surface joke is simple: she thinks herself utterly inscrutable. In fact, every dim little star in her orbit — from her maid to the opera manager — has her completely figured out. But Dickens doesn't stop there — he goes on to describe what the jewellers and the mercers and the librarian actually do with this knowledge. And then he produces this extraordinary image — listen to this: "who, in hooking one, hook all and bear them off as Lemuel Gulliver bore away the stately fleet of the majestic Lilliput." Lemuel Gulliver carrying off the Lilliputian fleet — as a metaphor for a librarian managing his high connexion. The absurdity of the scale is the whole point — and the relish with which Dickens deploys it is absolutely delicious.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What I find so deft there is the structural reversal that underpins the whole joke. The passage presents itself as a sociological observation — this is how influence flows through a fashionable class. But the real subject is self-delusion — and more specifically, the complicity between the flattered and the flatterers. Dickens gives us those marvellous voices — Blaze and Sparkle the jewellers, Sheen and Gloss the mercers — and they're perfectly named, of course. But notice how each of them speaks with absolute confidence — not as toadies, but as craftsmen who have mastered their material. The joke is that Lady Dedlock is not the patron — she is the product.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Now — from the quietly devastating to the magnificently operatic. Boythorn. Lawrence Boythorn and his war with Sir Leicester Dedlock over a right of way — which in Boythorn's hands becomes something close to a military campaign, narrated with the gusto of a man who has never been happier in his life. The sheer inventory of escalation is the joke: the fire engine, the humane man traps, the split peas fired at legs, the gates chopped down and burned before breakfast. And then he reaches what I consider one of the great comic crescendos in the novel — the formal symmetry of his feud: "He brings actions for trespass; I bring actions for trespass. He brings actions for assault and battery; I defend them and continue to assault and batter. Ha, ha, ha!" The 'Ha, ha, ha!' is written into the text — it's a stage direction as much as punctuation. Boythorn is a man who has found his perfect vocation — righteous, indefatigable, and absolutely hilarious.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The comedy there works by formal symmetry — and that's a technique Dickens deploys again, very differently, in the Vholes passage. The parliamentary committee transcript in Chapter Thirty-Nine is a masterclass in institutional comedy.
