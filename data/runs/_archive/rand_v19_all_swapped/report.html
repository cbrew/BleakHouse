Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The first thing Dickens does — and this is so completely typical of the man — is refuse to begin with a character. He begins with a condition. Fog. Mud. The Court of Chancery in full, magnificent inertia. And when you read it aloud — which is the only honest way to read Dickens — you feel immediately that you're in the hands of someone who knows exactly what he's doing. But I want to talk about a rather different kind of voice in Bleak House — one that Dickens deploys with extraordinary satirical precision. I'm thinking of Mr. Chadband — that magnificent, oleaginous preacher who oozes sanctimony from every pore. There's a moment where Chadband pauses in full rhetorical flight, as though visited by the divine, and announces: "I hear a voice — is it a still small voice, my friends? I fear not, though I fain would hope so." Now — that phrase, "a still small voice," is a direct steal from the Book of Kings. Elijah hears God in a still small voice — it is the very image of genuine spiritual humility. And Chadband appropriates it — wraps it in that boneless, self-congratulatory syntax — and then immediately confesses he fears it isn't one. The comedy is perfect. The hypocrisy is total. And Dickens achieves it in a single sentence.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to single out Chadband — and the performance reading is exactly the illuminating one here. But I would press a little further. Chadband is not merely comic — he is morally diagnostic. Dickens is extraordinarily precise about the category of sin he represents — which is not ignorance, and not cruelty, but counterfeit piety. Burke would have recognised it instantly: the substitution of fine-sounding language for the actual moral work. The novel is full of such substitutions — Chancery itself is one vast institutional version of the same vice. Now — I notice we have a rather brief passage from the Rouncewell family assigned to me today, concerning a simple request to speak with a father. George says nothing more than: "I was looking for your father, sir. I wish to have a word with him." Seven words of plain English. And yet the emotional weight in that scene is immense. George Rouncewell is a man who has spent decades estranged from his family — a soldier, a rough man, not given to eloquence. And in that single quiet sentence, Dickens gives us — with absolute economy — the whole ache of a prodigal seeking reconciliation. This is what I mean by moral imagination. Not programme. Not polemic. Individual human longing, rendered with perfect restraint.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
No. And that is precisely the structure of the novel's argument. Dickens gives you the comedy — Chadband, the Smallweeds, the whole grotesque machinery — and then he cuts to Tom-all-Alone's and shows you what that machinery actually produces. There is a passage in Chapter 46 that I consider one of the most devastating pieces of prose in the English language. Jo — the crossing-sweeper, the boy who knows nothing, owns nothing, and belongs to no one — reappears in Tom-all-Alone's, utterly destroyed. And Dickens describes what the boy is wearing — which is to say, what passes for clothing on his body — in these terms: "Clothes made for what purpose, or of what material, it would be impossible to say. They look, in colour and in substance, like a bundle of rank leaves of swampy growth that rotted long ago." The clothes have become indistinguishable from decomposed vegetation. The boy himself is barely distinguishable from the waste the city has discarded. Edmund speaks of individual moral longing — and I honour that reading of George Rouncewell. But Jo is not an individual being tested by circumstance. Jo is what the system requires to exist — to keep existing. The fog isn't a literary device. It is a description of what deliberately unaddressed poverty looks like from the inside.

Richard's Slow Poison: Ambition and the Law (deep_dive)

Host (host)
Welcome back to Bleak House Unpacked. We've spent time with the fog, with Chancery, with the Dedlocks in their grand isolation. Now we turn to the character whose destruction is perhaps the novel's most painful slow burn: Richard Carstone. A young man of promise and charm, eaten alive by a lawsuit he didn't start and can't escape. Oliver Trevelyan has read Richard's lines in more recordings than he can likely count. Edmund Leigh sees in Richard something closer to a moral case study than a social victim. And Daniel Rosen reads Richard's ruin as the system doing exactly what it was designed to do. Oliver, you've lived inside this character for years — where do we begin with Richard?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
You begin with Richard the way Dickens begins with him — dazzled by him. He comes in all brightness and laughter and charm, this young man who might be anything. Surgeon, lawyer, soldier — he tries them all, because none of them matters as much as the case. And the tragedy — and it is a tragedy — is that we watch the brightness go out. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd push back on that, Edmund — respectfully, but firmly. To say the case merely magnifies a pre-existing flaw in Richard's character is to let the institution off the hook entirely. Host (host)

With me today are three readers who have made Bleak House their own in very different ways. Oliver Trevelyan has narrated more Dickens audiobooks than any living person, and hears the music of this prose with a performer's ear. Sir Edmund Leigh, retired from Oxford, reads Bleak House as a moral drama — a testing of character under the weight of circumstance. Let's begin with what I think is one of Dickens' great gifts — the grotesque bystander who somehow sees everything.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Krook is one of Dickens' supreme achievements, and I don't think that's said often enough. He's a parody of the Lord Chancellor — the Rag and Bottle Shop as Chancery in miniature — but he's also something stranger and older. He's the ancient accumulator, the man who hoards everything and can use nothing. And when something genuinely shocks him — when a secret surfaces that he was not prepared for — Dickens gives him the most magnificently economical reaction in the book. "Hi!" exclaimed the old man in a tone of thoughtful amazement — and with a wider stare than before — "Think of it!" "Think of it!" — that's all he says. Two words that somehow carry the weight of everything unspoken, everything that has been filed away in that shop for decades. Dickens was performing when he wrote that line — you can feel the stage timing, the pause before the second exclamation.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me about Krook is not simply the comedy — though the comedy is superb — but the moral function. He is astonishment without understanding — the capacity to be amazed, but not the capacity to act on what one knows. Dickens distributes those qualities very precisely through his characters. Krook sees, but cannot read — literally cannot read — and that illiteracy becomes his defining limitation. It's a quietly devastating portrait of a man who possesses everything and comprehends nothing — which is, come to think of it, Dickens' definition of the English governing class as well.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to bring in a passage that sits on the other side of Krook — the machinery of power acting on a living person. When George visits Smallweed and is referred on to Tulkinghorn, Dickens does something very quiet and very chilling. And yet what Bucket is recovering — that letter — is a document that will bring down Lady Dedlock.
