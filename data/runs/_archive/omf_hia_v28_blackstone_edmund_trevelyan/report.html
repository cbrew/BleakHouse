James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd agree with Edmund on the psychological force — and I'd add that the manner in which Dickens renders that moment is technically astonishing. We're inside a consciousness that has nearly ceased to exist, watching it reconstitute itself through sheer act of will. Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
Oliver is right about the rhythm — and I'd frame it in slightly different terms, if I may. What Dickens is doing in that moment is essentially Johnsonian — the assertion of the will against dissolution. Johnson believed that the great enemy of the moral life was a certain slackness — the failure to summon one's energies at the decisive moment. Here, that summoning is literal — it is survival itself. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And yet — and this is where the social context matters enormously — the money he's fleeing is also the money he eventually accepts. The dust heaps — the great mounds of London refuse from which old Harmon's fortune was built — are not merely a colourful backdrop. By the 1860s, the trade in metropolitan dust — ash, bone, cinders, street sweepings — was a genuinely substantial industry. There were men who made fortunes from it — and fortunes made from refuse carry a particular kind of moral taint, then as now. The novel insists that money, regardless of its origin, deforms every relationship it enters — and I think that insistence is grounded in what Dickens actually saw around him.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And yet before we leave this opening, I want to register one more passage — because it shows the novel's capacity for something altogether darker than satire. Bradley Headstone creeping up the staircase toward Eugene Wrayburn's chambers — it is one of the most purely threatening passages in the book. Dickens describes 'the haggard head' — not the man, but the head — floating up the dark staircase. And then the narrator delivers a line of almost horrible intimacy: 'If Lightwood could have seen the face which kept him awake, staring and listening in the darkness outside the door as he spoke of it, he might have been less disposed to sleep, through the remainder of the night.' Edmund Leigh (literary_critic)
One of the things that strikes me most forcibly about this novel is how Dickens plants, at its very root, a paradox about wealth and moral incorruptibility. Old John Harmon — the dead Jailer of Harmony Jail, as Dickens calls him — despised humanity, mistrusted everyone, and yet was compelled to acknowledge that some people simply could not be bought. Dickens gives us the psychology of it with great precision. He tells us that the old miser had perceived — "the powerlessness of all his wealth to buy them if he had addressed himself to the attempt." The wealth is powerless — not the miser, mark you, but the wealth itself. Dickens is telling us, at the outset, that integrity is not a commodity — and that a man who has made money his god can nonetheless, in extremis, recognise that fact.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Edmund is right to start there, because that dead man's will is the engine that drives everything that follows. And from a legal standpoint, what is remarkable is the sheer power the testator retains from beyond the grave. The Boffins are named as trustees; Bella is effectively conscripted as a potential heiress on terms she never agreed to; and John Harmon Junior is made to inherit a fortune tied to a marriage he had no hand in arranging. That is the lived reality of Victorian testamentary law — the dead hand reaching forward through time, shaping the choices of the living. And Dickens understands the cruelty of it perfectly well, even as he also, as Edmund says, grants the old man a kind of grudging moral lucidity.

I want to resist the temptation to call it merely absurd, though. Because Harmon himself, in that same extended confession, tells us precisely what he was afraid of. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The dilemma is real, I grant that — but there is something we should not elide, which is the extraordinary power differential at work here. Harmon chooses to test Bella — but Bella has no idea she is being tested.
