Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me first, every single time, is the audacity of the comedy. Eliot is writing a tragic novel — one of the great tragic novels in the language — and she opens it with a character who is, frankly, hilarious. Gwendolen Harleth is not merely vain — she is vain with a kind of operatic grandeur that Eliot clearly adores even as she anatomises it. There's a passage early on where Gwendolen is fantasising about Grandcourt — laying out this extraordinary little scenario in her head. And Eliot gives us the full spectacle of it — listen to this: 'My arrow will pierce him before he has time for thought. He will declare himself my slave—I shall send him round the world to bring me back the wedding ring of a happy woman.' The wedding ring of a happy woman — she hasn't even spoken to him properly, and she's already dispatching him on a quest with a deliberately impossible object. That is not vanity — that is a novelist's gift for comic invention, lodged inside a character who doesn't know she has it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver puts his finger on something important, though I should want to resist the word 'hilarious' — or at least to complicate it. The comedy of Gwendolen is always doing moral work. What Eliot is showing us, with that fantasy, is a young woman who has never been seriously checked — never had her will genuinely thwarted. The humour is affectionate, yes — but it is also diagnostic. And the novel's great moral drama depends on our understanding that this egoism, however entertaining, has a cost — ultimately, a devastating one. What I find equally telling is the later scene — during the quadrille with Grandcourt — where Eliot describes Gwendolen's pleasure. 'It began to appear probable that she would have it in her power to reject him, whence there was a pleasure in reckoning up the advantages which would make her rejection splendid.' The pleasure is not in possessing him — it is in rejecting him splendidly. That is a finely observed truth about a certain kind of vanity — the ego that feeds not on triumph but on the imagined spectacle of triumph.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't dispute the moral reading — but I think we have to ask what conditions produce this particular form of vanity. Gwendolen is not simply an egoist by nature. She is a woman of intelligence and real gifts — gifts for performance, for social mastery — who has been given absolutely no serious channel for those gifts. Marriage is the only arena available to her, so she brings to marriage the intensity and strategic brilliance that in another life might have gone elsewhere entirely. What's striking is the contrast with Catherine Arrowpoint — who is wealthy, independent, and who makes a choice that costs her dearly in social terms. When Catherine is challenged about marrying Klesmer — a musician, a man of no social standing by the world's reckoning — she says something remarkable. 'He is of a caste to which I look up — a caste above mine.' She inverts the entire social logic of the world these characters inhabit. Artistic achievement — genuine creative labour — stands above inherited money and social position. That is not sentimental — that is a direct challenge to the class order, put in the mouth of a woman who has everything to lose by saying it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Precisely — and it is why I think any reading that reduces Gwendolen purely to a social symptom misses the deeper point. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And Eliot finds the same structure of self-performance even in the most tragic characters — that's what makes her so extraordinary. There's that shattering scene with Deronda's mother — a woman who spent nine years as, she says, a queen. "I made believe that I preferred being the wife of a Russian noble to being the greatest lyric actress of Europe; I made believe — I acted that part." 'I acted that part' — she can't even describe her own capitulation without reaching for the performer's vocabulary. It is one of the most devastating self-revelations in all of Victorian fiction. And underneath the performance, the real sentence — 'It was because I felt my greatness sinking away from me.' That is not comedy any more — that is Eliot going somewhere very cold and very honest.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'd like to begin at the archery ground, because I think Eliot gives us the whole of Gwendolen's psychology in miniature there. She is performing unawareness of Grandcourt with an absolute, consuming awareness of him. Eliot puts it with characteristic precision — listen to this. "And all the while the certainty that he was there made a distinct thread in her consciousness." A thread — not a flood, not an obsession, but a thread. It runs through everything she does without ever quite becoming visible. And what does Gwendolen do with that thread? She wins the gold star. She hits three in succession. The consciousness of being watched does not paralyse her — it galvanises her. That is both her gift and, ultimately, her great vulnerability.
