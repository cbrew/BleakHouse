Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is marvellous on the music of it, and I don't dispute that for a moment. But I'd gently resist the suggestion that the fog is *doing* something beyond being fog — magnificently, terrifyingly, fog. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The passage about old Harmon's fortune is one of the most precise economic descriptions in the entire Victorian novel — and it's disguised as comedy, which is exactly how it gets past you. Mortimer tells us how the Dust Contractor grew rich — and then gives us this inventory, and you have to listen to what's in it. "Coal-dust, vegetable-dust, bone-dust, crockery dust, rough dust and sifted dust,—all manner of Dust." Bone-dust. That's not waste — that's the residue of living things, processed and sold. This is a man who has made a fortune from what the city discards. And the repetition of the word 'Dust' — seven times in five lines — is Dickens hammering the point home with a comic mallet. But the point is this: the wealth that drives every plot mechanism in this novel — Bella's education, the Boffins' philanthropy, Rokesmith's disguise — all of it sits on a mountain of sorted rubbish. That is not a symbol. That is a fact about Victorian capital.
