Welcome to New Grub Street Unpacked — a close reading of George Gissing's great novel of literary ambition, poverty, and the marketplace of words. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The first thing you notice — reading aloud or reading to yourself — is that Gissing's London is almost physically present on the page. He doesn't describe poverty the way a social reformer would, with statistics and horror. He describes it the way a great novelist does — with texture, with detail, with a kind of terrible specificity. There's a passage Daniel is going to talk about in a moment — a penny reading-room at the top of a building — that is one of the most evocative settings in Victorian fiction. Gissing takes you up those stairs, floor by floor, and by the time you reach the writing room at the top, you feel the whole weight of literary aspiration trapped under a sloping blue ceiling. That's what great novelists do — they make the physical world carry the emotional argument.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think Oliver is quite right about the texture — and I'd add that Gissing's great subject is not poverty as such, but the predicament of the educated man confronting poverty. That is a far more particular and interesting subject, and one that requires a novelist of considerable psychological insight. Johnson knew something of this. The original Grub Street was populated not by the ignorant but by the overqualified — men of talent who found no market for what they could actually do. Gissing inherits that tradition and gives it a Victorian form — the three-volume novel, the circulating library, the magazine market. What interests me most is whether his characters have the moral resources to meet that challenge — and in many cases, I'm afraid, they do not.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to pick up on that reading-room passage, because I think it repays very close attention. Gissing takes us up through this building floor by floor — newspapers, magazines, chess, refreshments — and each floor is a rung on a social ladder that goes nowhere. And then you arrive at the writing room, and listen to how he describes it: "The walls of this last retreat were of blue plaster and sloped inwards from the floor; along them stood school desks with benches, and in one place was suspended a ragged and dirty card announcing that paper and envelopes could be purchased downstairs." A retreat — he calls it a retreat, which is already rich — but the walls slope inward. This is a room that is literally contracting around the people who aspire to write in it. The decorations are ink blotches and scribbling — the residue of all the failed work that has passed through before. Edmund says the question is whether Gissing's characters have the moral resources to meet their situation — but I'd say Gissing is asking a prior question: whether the situation permits moral resources to operate at all.

Reardon is one of those characters who makes you want to grab the author by the lapels and say: why are you doing this to him? Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me is how carefully Gissing distinguishes between defeat inflicted from without and defeat accepted from within. Reardon is not simply a victim of a cruel market — he is a man whose character has a particular fragility. There is a passage from chapter four that I keep returning to, where Reardon articulates his own artistic conscience. "There's the shrinking from conscious insincerity of workmanship — which most of the writers nowadays seem never to feel." That is a man with a genuine moral imagination about his craft. And that very conscience — that refusal to say 'it's good enough for the market' — is precisely what the market punishes him for. Tragedy, in the classical sense, requires a man of stature brought low by a flaw inseparable from his virtue.

I don't dispute the indictment, but I notice Gissing also shows us exactly how Reardon's own history has unmade him. The Market Realities: Milvain's Gambit (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been watching Reardon collapse under the weight of his own artistic conscience — unable to bend, unable to sell. Now I want to turn to the man who is, in almost every respect, Reardon's photographic negative: Jasper Milvain.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Jasper Milvain is one of the great comic-serious creations in Victorian fiction — and I say that with complete conviction. He is utterly shameless, but Gissing never quite lets you hate him, because he is so refreshingly honest about his own shamelessness. Right at the start, he sits down with his sisters and delivers what I can only call his manifesto — with tremendous brio. "Literature nowadays is a trade. Putting aside men of genius, who may succeed by mere cosmic force, your successful man of letters is your skilful tradesman." The rhythm of that is extraordinary — the little parenthesis about men of genius, airily dismissed, and then the verdict landing like a hammer. Gissing gives Jasper all the best tunes, and he knows it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one must be careful not to mistake fluency for wisdom — which is precisely the mistake Jasper himself makes about Jasper. What strikes me in that opening declaration is how completely Milvain identifies his own convenience with historical necessity. He tells his sisters, in that same passage, that Reardon "sells a manuscript as if he lived in Sam Johnson's Grub Street" — as though the comparison settles the matter entirely.
