Opening: Three Women at the Crossroads (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to The Odd Women Unpacked — a close, argued, thoroughly enjoyed reading of George Gissing's 1893 novel. I'm your host, and over this series we'll be living inside one of the most searching novels ever written about women, work, and what it means to be free. Gissing's title is a joke with a bitter edge — the 'odd women' are the surplus women of Victorian England, statistically left over, unmarried, economically precarious. But Gissing does something extraordinary with that premise — he turns surplus into argument, and argument into drama. Three brilliant people are joining me today, and I want to introduce them properly, because each of them brings a completely different ear to this novel. Oliver Trevelyan has recorded more classic novels for audio than perhaps anyone alive — he comes to Gissing as a performer who hears the prose the way a musician hears a score. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don whose literary judgements carry the weight of a very long, very well-read life — he will tell us what the novel is actually about, beneath all our clever theories. And Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads novels as X-rays of power — he'll show us the skeleton beneath Gissing's Victorian flesh. Let's begin where Gissing begins — with a family portrait that is really an economic sentence of death.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me first — always, every time I return to this novel — is the opening movement's terrible efficiency. Gissing gives us Dr. Madden and his six daughters in the first chapter, and within a few pages that father is dead and the daughters are simply — adrift. There's no melodrama about it, which is the masterstroke. Gissing simply notes that the daughters have been educated as if husbands were guaranteed, and then quietly removes that guarantee. It's like watching a conjuror remove the tablecloth — except all the china smashes. And the daughters we follow — Monica, Virginia, Alice — are not stupid women. That's what makes it so painful to watch them flail within systems entirely designed for their failure.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right about the efficiency, though I'd describe what Gissing achieves rather differently. The Madden sisters are not victims of a system, primarily — they are inheritors of a particular kind of moral failure. Their father is a decent man, affectionate, but he has been culpably imprudent. He has raised daughters without preparing them for the reality of their position. Now — Gissing does not sentimentalise this. He is too good a novelist for that. But I think it matters enormously that the novel's catastrophe begins in parental failure — in a failure of love to become wisdom. That is a very different diagnosis from Oliver's, and from Daniel's, I suspect.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I'd say Dr. Madden's failure and the system's failure are the same failure — the system produces men like Dr. Madden. He is not an aberration — he is the norm. Middle-class Victorian men routinely failed to secure their daughters' futures because the ideology of femininity actively discouraged women from learning how to secure it themselves. What Gissing maps in those opening chapters is the material consequence of that ideology — these women have no trade, no capital, no recourse. Virginia descends into secret drinking. Alice is worn down to a kind of exhausted resignation. Monica — the youngest, the most vital — makes the worst calculation available to her, and marries Widdowson. These are not personal failures — they are economic destinies, wearing the costume of personal choice.
Host (host)
And set against the Madden sisters — almost as a deliberate structural contrast — we have Rhoda Nunn and Mary Barfoot. These are women who have, in their different ways, refused the available destinies. Oliver — how do you read that contrast in the novel's architecture?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
It's the novel's great structural gamble, and Gissing wins it, I think, though it took me a second reading to feel entirely sure. Mary Barfoot and Rhoda run a typewriting school — training women for the new clerical economy. And from one angle that sounds very prosaic — almost satirically mundane as a vehicle for feminist argument. But Gissing makes it feel like a revolutionary cell operating inside a hostile society. These women are actually doing something — and that 'actually' is doing enormous weight in the novel. Against the drift and dissolution of the Maddens, Barfoot and Nunn represent a kind of willed, precarious, magnificent self-invention.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'll allow that Mary Barfoot is a genuinely admirable creation — Gissing gives her a warmth and a pragmatic intelligence that is very attractive. She believes in her cause, but she is also a woman of the world — she understands that causes without economics are merely sermons. Rhoda Nunn is a rather more complicated proposition for me. Her rigidity — her insistence on an almost programmatic independence — strikes me as a different kind of imprudence from Dr. Madden's, but imprudence nonetheless. Dr. Johnson would have recognised her type immediately — the person so committed to a principle that they are incapable of being surprised by human nature. And of course, Gissing — very shrewdly — arranges for Everard Barfoot to walk through her door and complicate all that.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What I find most remarkable about this novel's opening is the precision of Gissing's social geometry. He places these two worlds — the Maddens' slow-motion catastrophe and Barfoot and Nunn's embattled project — at opposite ends of the same social stratum. They are not class enemies. They are women of the same respectable poverty, facing the same foreclosed horizon — but responding to it with utterly different wills. And that is Gissing's argument in miniature — not that individual will is irrelevant, but that individual will is where ideology does its most intimate damage. To break with the available script, as Rhoda has done, costs something — Gissing is not sentimental about that cost.
Host (host)
And that cost — what it actually means to live Rhoda Nunn's kind of independence — is exactly where we're headed next. Because Rhoda is not just a model of the emancipated woman — she is also a woman in the grip of something she hasn't fully reckoned with. After the break, we'll look at Rhoda Nunn's uncompromising stand — and ask whether what looks like principle might sometimes be armour.

The Weight of Independence: Rhoda Nunn's Uncompromising Stand (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been thinking about three women standing at crossroads — each one facing a different version of the same impossible question. Now I want to put one figure at the very centre of our lens: Rhoda Nunn, the woman who seems to have answered that question — and answered it with something close to ferocity.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Rhoda Nunn is one of those characters you meet and immediately want to perform — not because she's theatrical, but because she's so completely herself. Every line she speaks has this bracing quality — like cold water thrown in your face and you're grateful for it. Gissing gives her this magnificent set piece early on where she declares, in effect, that marriage is the enemy of women's development — not as a polemic, but as a simple statement of observable fact. And what makes it extraordinary on the page — and even more so aloud — is how utterly calm she is. There's no shrillness, no martyrdom — just this clear-eyed certainty that the world has arranged itself absurdly, and she has decided not to participate in the absurdity.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think Oliver is right about the calm — and that calm is precisely what troubles me about her, in the most productive sense. Rhoda is not a satirical target for Gissing — he clearly admires her. And yet he is honest enough — or perhaps cruel enough — to show us the cost. Her great speech to Mary Barfoot about the 'odd women' — those surplus women whom society has no designated role for — is stirring, genuinely stirring. But Gissing places it in a life that is, for much of the novel, conspicuously unlived — in the domestic and emotional sense. Whether one calls that sacrifice or impoverishment depends rather on one's values, I suppose.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd push back on 'unlived' — because that framing smuggles in the assumption that a life oriented around independence and work is somehow less than a life oriented around a husband. What Gissing is actually mapping, with great precision, is the material conditions that make Rhoda's position both necessary and radical. The typewriting school that she and Mary Barfoot run is not a hobby — it is an intervention in the labour market on behalf of women who would otherwise have no economic existence. Rhoda's personal stance and the school's institutional purpose are the same argument made at two different scales. When she tells Everard Barfoot that she wants no part of the conventional marriage arrangement, she is not being personally eccentric — she is being structurally consistent.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
But there is an Everard Barfoot problem, isn't there — and it's delicious. Gissing has the audacity to give Rhoda — the most principled woman in the novel — the most compelling man. Everard is clever, he is charming, he is genuinely interested in ideas — and he proceeds to test Rhoda in the most systematic way imaginable. He even proposes what he calls a 'free union' — marriage in spirit but not in law — as if removing the legal chain would make the existential one vanish. And you can feel Rhoda — feel her, on the page — genuinely wavering. Gissing does not let her off easily, which is what makes her final refusal so hard-won.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That wavering is the most humanly truthful thing in the novel, I would argue. Gissing understands — as all the great novelists understand — that conviction is not the same as immunity to feeling. What is tested in Rhoda is not her intelligence — no one doubts her intelligence — but her will. And there is a passage, when Rhoda reflects on what she calls the 'dread of an unoccupied future,' that I find genuinely affecting. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And then there is the scene in the Lakeland countryside — which Gissing, bless him, uses with no subtlety whatsoever and every possible effectiveness. The landscape is doing precisely what Victorian landscapes do — loosening the stays of civilisation, inviting the characters to be something other than their social roles. And Rhoda, out there in the open air, is closer to yielding than at any other point in the novel. It's gloriously uncomfortable to read — because you want her to yield, and simultaneously you know it would be a kind of defeat. Gissing engineers that ambivalence with the skill of a novelist who is himself entirely unresolved on the question.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to say something in Everard's partial defence, if I may. He is not, I think, a villain — he is a man who has constructed a philosophy of personal freedom and is genuinely surprised to find it colliding with someone who has constructed a more rigorous one. The novel's real sting comes when Rhoda discovers — or believes she discovers — his entanglement with Agnes Brissenden. Because that is where the theoretical becomes personal — and personal wounds have a way of hardening positions that might otherwise have softened. What Rhoda ends the novel with is not, I would argue, serene independence — it is independence purchased at the price of a very particular grief.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't dispute the grief — but I think we should be careful not to read the grief as Gissing's verdict on her choices. Look at what surrounds her at the novel's close: Monica dead, Widdowson broken, the Madden sisters crushed one by one by economic dependence. Every woman in this novel who chose the conventional path has paid a price that dwarfs Rhoda's. And the school goes on — the work continues — young women are being trained for economic life. Rhoda's grief is real, but it is also, structurally, the price the novel asks her to pay to prove she is human rather than merely symbolic.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That is very well put — 'human rather than merely symbolic' — because Gissing really does refuse to make her a saint. He gives her vanity, he gives her occasional cruelty — particularly in how she handles Monica — and he gives her this absolutely remorseless capacity to judge. There's a moment where she reflects on Monica's situation with something that is not quite sympathy — it's closer to impatience. As if Monica has wasted a resource — herself — that Rhoda would have deployed more efficiently. It is almost cold — and yet you understand it perfectly.
Host (host)
And that cold understanding — and what it costs the one who holds it — is something Gissing returns to again and again in this novel. Which brings us to Monica herself — the woman who made the opposite choice, and what that choice actually looked like from the inside.

A Loveless Bargain: Monica's Surrender and Survival (close_reading)

Host (host)
We've been looking at Rhoda Nunn's fierce refusal to compromise — her almost militant independence. But Gissing places another woman at the centre of this novel whose path runs in almost the opposite direction. Monica Madden — the youngest of the Madden sisters — chooses marriage, and Gissing gives us no illusions about what that choice costs her.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Monica is one of Gissing's most quietly devastating creations, and I think she's sometimes underestimated because she isn't combative. She doesn't argue with the world — she tries to survive it. She's trapped behind a draper's counter when we first meet her — exhausted, underpaid, her health already fraying. And Gissing is unflinching about what the draper's trade does to young women's bodies and spirits. When Widdowson comes along with his respectability and his income, the reader understands perfectly why Monica says yes. Gissing makes sure we understand — and he makes sure we understand the tragedy of that understanding.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think that's right, but I'd resist framing it purely as victimhood. Monica has genuine romantic feeling — or at least the longing for it — and she brings a great deal of her own trouble upon herself. There is a passage early in her courtship where Gissing tells us quite plainly that she does not love Widdowson. She reflects that his manner of looking at her gave her a sense of satisfaction — the satisfaction, one feels, of being wanted rather than of wanting. Gissing is not sentimental about this distinction. Monica chooses comfort and escape — which is entirely human — but she chooses it with her eyes open, and that matters morally.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Eyes open — yes, but open to what choices, exactly? Monica's alternatives are the draper's counter, or dependence on her sisters, or the slow grind of genteel poverty. Gissing is extraordinarily precise about the economic arithmetic of her situation. When she accepts Widdowson, she is not making a romantic error — she is executing a rational calculation with the limited variables available to her. To call that a moral failing is, I'd suggest, to mistake the symptom for the disease.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I wouldn't call it a failing — I'd call it a compromise, which is a different thing. And Gissing is, at his best, a novelist of compromises and their consequences. What strikes me about Monica's marriage is not the wedding but what comes after — the creeping realisation that she has exchanged one cage for another. Widdowson's house in Herne Hill is comfortable enough, but Gissing renders it with a kind of suffocating stillness. Monica is not beaten or abused — she is simply watched, and controlled, and gradually extinguished.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That word — extinguished — is exactly right, and I think Gissing earns it through accumulation. There's a marvellous, terrible scene where Monica simply wants to go out — to walk, to visit Mildred Vesper, to exist in the world. And Widdowson's response is this extraordinary performance of wounded propriety. He doesn't forbid her exactly — he suffers. He makes his suffering so visible, so heavy in the room, that Monica's freedom becomes an act of cruelty in his framing. Gissing understands that domestic tyranny rarely announces itself with a locked door — it announces itself with a long, reproachful silence.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And the ideology doing the work there is Widdowson's conception of what a wife is for. He has a genuinely medieval notion — dressed up in Victorian respectability — that a wife is property to be protected, which is to say possessed. There's a moment — and it's one of the most chilling in the novel — where Widdowson tells Monica something to the effect that a wife's place is her home, and that going out into the world is a kind of contamination. Gissing gives Widdowson just enough self-awareness to make him pitiful rather than purely monstrous — which is the more disturbing choice. Because a monster can be expelled from the story. A man who genuinely believes he loves his wife while destroying her — that's the novel's real horror.
Host (host)
And that's where Everard Barfoot enters the picture — because Monica's friendship with Barfoot becomes the fulcrum on which Widdowson's jealousy tips into genuine persecution. Oliver, how do you read Gissing's handling of that relationship — is Monica reaching for escape, or for something she never had?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Both, I think — and Gissing is wise enough not to separate them. Monica's attraction to Barfoot is partly intellectual — he talks to her as though her opinions matter, which Widdowson essentially never does. But it's also the simple, terrible pull of being seen as a person rather than an appendage. What I find so painful in the novel is that Gissing gives Monica a genuine moment of awakening — she glimpses a different kind of life — and then closes the door on it absolutely. Her death in childbirth at the novel's end isn't melodrama. It's the logic of the system played out to its conclusion.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to push back on that slightly — not to exonerate the system, but to restore Monica's agency in the tragedy. Gissing gives Monica opportunities — through Mary Barfoot's circle, through Rhoda's example — that she does not take. She visits the typewriting school, she meets women who have carved out independence — and she retreats. There is a timidity in Monica — a longing for the conventional shelter of marriage — that Gissing renders without contempt but also without false sympathy. Her tragedy is not simply what is done to her — it is also the choices that close around her, some of which she closes herself.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't deny Monica's interiority — but I'd say we have to ask what 'agency' means for a woman with no financial independence and no professional training. Rhoda Nunn can make her austere choices precisely because Mary Barfoot has created an institution that gives her economic ground to stand on. Monica has no such ground. The retreat Edmund describes isn't timidity in any simple sense — it's the rational response to having nothing to retreat to. Gissing himself understood this. He wrote Monica with a specificity and a tenderness that suggests he knew exactly what the novel was accusing.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And the baby — that detail is so precisely Gissing. Monica gives birth to a child she will never raise, in a marriage that was always a kind of slow erasure. And Widdowson survives — to grieve, to repent, probably to marry again. Gissing gives the system its victory, and he gives Monica her death, and he doesn't flinch from either. That's what separates him from a sentimentalist — he won't rescue his characters to spare the reader's feelings.
Host (host)
Monica's story ends in that terrible economy of loss — and Widdowson's jealousy, as you've all touched on, is at the heart of it. Which brings us to the man himself — because Gissing lavishes a kind of dark comic attention on Widdowson's possessiveness that deserves its own reckoning.

The Comic Misery of Widdowson's Possessiveness (discussion)

Host (host)
We've been tracing the slow suffocation of Monica's marriage — the way her surrender was never quite complete, never quite peace. Which brings us, rather naturally, to the man doing the suffocating — Edmund Widdowson, one of literature's great comic monsters. Except, of course, he's not quite a monster — and that's precisely what makes him so uncomfortable to read.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Widdowson is a gift — an absolutely spectacular gift to any reader who enjoys watching a man destroy himself with his own anxiety. He has retired on a modest income, he has no occupation, no real purpose — and then he acquires Monica, and she becomes the occupation. He follows her through the streets of London. He watches the windows of her friends' houses. He times her return home to the minute. And the extraordinary thing is that Gissing renders all of this with something very close to comedy — a comedy of absolute dread. There's a passage where Widdowson essentially admits that his idea of a wife is a creature who exists entirely within the domestic sphere — who has no life that is not sanctioned by her husband. He says something to the effect that a wife's place is her home, that women who go about independently are not behaving as women should. And Gissing lets him say it with complete conviction — there's no authorial sneer. Widdowson believes every word.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'd want to resist the word 'comedy' slightly — or at least to complicate it. Widdowson is not a fool. He is a man of limited imagination who has made a catastrophic mistake — which is not quite the same thing. He married a young woman thirty years his junior because he wanted warmth and companionship — perfectly human desires. What he could not foresee — what his nature made it impossible for him to foresee — was that those desires would curdle into tyranny. Gissing is actually quite generous to him in certain moments — Widdowson is capable of self-reproach, even tenderness. He knows, in his clearer moments, that he is behaving unreasonably. He simply cannot stop. That is not a comic figure — that is a tragic one. A man imprisoned by the very temperament he cannot shed.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund's point about self-reproach is well-taken — but I think we need to be careful not to let Widdowson's occasional tenderness obscure what he actually represents. His possessiveness is not simply a personality defect. It is the logical extension of a property relation. He has purchased Monica's time, her body, her social presence — in the most literal sense available to Victorian law. And when he follows her through the streets, when he interrogates her about every hour of her day — he is simply enforcing the terms of the contract. What makes Gissing remarkable is that he shows us a man who genuinely believes he is acting out of love — while systematically destroying the person he claims to love. That gap between self-image and material effect — that is the ideological core of the novel's critique of bourgeois marriage.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel's right about the contract, and Edmund's right about the tragedy — but I'd argue Gissing holds all three registers simultaneously, which is the real trick. Think about the surveillance episodes — Widdowson literally lurking outside the house in Chelsea where Monica visits Bevis. He is skulking in the fog, watching a front door, a middle-aged man of independent means reduced to the condition of a private detective. There is something almost Dickensian in that image — farcical and pitiable at once. And yet the consequences for Monica are entirely real — every evening he interrogates her, every discrepancy in her account becomes a wound he presses. Gissing won't let the comedy release the pressure. He keeps them both — the absurdity and the cruelty — in the same frame.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
There's a scene I return to often — where Widdowson presents Monica with a programme of self-improvement. He wants her to read serious books, to cultivate her mind — according to his prescription, naturally. It is, in its way, a portrait of benevolent despotism — he genuinely believes he is improving her. And Monica — bless her — endures it with a patience that Gissing describes as increasingly mechanical. Her inner life has simply retreated beyond his reach. She answers his questions; she reads his books; she gives him nothing of herself. It is a masterclass in the psychology of a trapped person — survival through a kind of interior emigration.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That phrase — interior emigration — is exactly right. And it points to something Gissing understands with unusual clarity. Monica's resistance is necessarily invisible — because visible resistance would be punished. She has no legal recourse, no economic independence, no support network outside the women she met before her marriage. Her connection to Rhoda Nunn and the world of Wimpole Street represents the only imaginable alternative — and Widdowson, with his instinctive radar for threat, resents that connection bitterly. He cannot name what he fears from those women — but he fears it. Women who have chosen not to be owned.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And there's the extraordinary irony — his jealousy of Bevis, which is at least partly warranted, is almost a distraction from the deeper threat. Bevis is a romantic escapade — a fantasy. Rhoda Nunn and Mary Barfoot are a philosophy. Widdowson can follow Monica to a house in Chelsea. He cannot follow her into a changed mind. And that, I think, is where Gissing's comedy tips into something genuinely painful — the moment you realise that Widdowson has already lost, and doesn't know it yet.
Host (host)
Widdowson lost, and the institution of marriage itself in crisis — which leads us directly to our next question. Because Gissing doesn't only diagnose the problem — he's testing whether any of his characters can actually escape it. What happens when the women of Wimpole Street try to build something different — and what are the limits of what reform can actually achieve?

Social Entrapment and the Limits of Reform (deep_dive)

Host (host)
Widdowson's jealousy gave us one face of entrapment — a man who mistakes ownership for love. But Gissing is doing something much larger in this novel — he's mapping a whole society in which escape routes for women are systematically closed off. Let's turn to that broader question: social entrapment, and whether the novel believes reform is actually possible.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The novel is, at its structural core, an anatomy of blocked exits. Gissing positions Rhoda Nunn and Mary Barfoot's typewriting school as the reform proposition — the thing the novel holds up and tests. And what does it actually produce? Monica Madden walks through that door, acquires a little economic foothold, and then the gravitational pull of marriage drags her straight back into the old system. The school is not a solution — it's a temporary reprieve before the walls close in.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I would put it rather differently, and I think with greater fidelity to what the novel actually shows us. Monica's return to the old system, as Daniel calls it, is not the failure of a social structure — it is the failure of Monica's own character under pressure. She is romantically susceptible, she is impulsive, and she makes a choice — marriage to Widdowson — that her own intelligence ought to have warned her against. Gissing is not indifferent to social conditions, I grant you that. But he is first and foremost a novelist of moral psychology — of what people do with whatever freedom they do possess.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Edmund, I love that reading, but I think the novel rather cheats Monica out of the freedom you're attributing to her. Think about where we first meet her — behind the counter of a draper's shop, exhausted, underpaid, surrounded by other young women in identical servitude. Gissing gives us that world with extraordinary precision — the pettiness of the rules, the surveillance of the shop floor, the sheer grind of it. And then Widdowson appears, and he is — whatever else you say about him — a way out of that shop. Her choice, Edmund, is less a moral failure than a calculation made under conditions of near-desperation.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that's the mechanism Gissing is so precise about — marriage as a labour market for women who have no other viable labour market. Mary Barfoot says it almost in those terms at one point, in her lectures to the students at the school. The first thing you notice, reading this novel aloud — and I've spent considerable time in its company — is that the narrator almost never raises his voice. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one must be careful not to read every instance of narrative restraint as ideological accusation. There is a long tradition in English prose — going back at least to Fielding — of the narrator who maintains composure precisely because the subject matter is distressing. What Gissing is doing, I'd suggest, is closer to a kind of stoic witness. He sees clearly; he records faithfully; he does not editorialise, because to editorialise would be to reduce the dignity of what he's observing. That said, Oliver is right that the irony is present — it operates through juxtaposition rather than explicit comment. When Rhoda Nunn speaks with such vigour about the emancipated woman, and we are shown Monica's quiet, crumbling hopes in the very next scene — that arrangement is the argument.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to take that a step further. Gissing's ironic distance isn't just a stylistic preference — it's a structural response to an impossible position. He is a man writing about women's oppression with genuine sympathy, but also with the consciousness that he is an outsider to that experience. The distance is partly ethical — he won't presume to speak from inside a suffering he hasn't lived. The Tyranny of Circumstance: Synthesis and Reckoning (closing)

Host (host)
We've been talking about how Gissing's narrative voice holds its characters at a certain ironic distance — observing, recording, never quite sentimentalising. That distance, I think, brings us to the hardest question the novel poses: who is actually responsible for the suffering we witness? Is it individual weakness, is it society, is it simply the brute weight of circumstance — the tyranny, as Gissing might say, of the way things are?

I think Gissing is absolutely clear on this, even if he refuses to be polemical about it. The Madden sisters at the beginning of the novel — they are not destroyed by moral failure. They are destroyed by the death of their father, a man who never thought to educate them for independence, who assumed the world would always provide a husband. That structural vulnerability — that's not personal weakness, that is a system designed to keep women dependent. Gissing shows us the mechanism with almost clinical precision.
