Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
The opening of Bleak House is one of those rare cases where you can feel a novelist solving a problem in real time — on the page, in front of you. The problem is: how do you open a novel about an institution so vast and so suffocating that it has consumed years, fortunes, and entire families? Dickens' answer is atmospheric annihilation. He doesn't introduce a character, or a scene, or even a plot. He introduces a condition — fog — and then lets it eat everything. He writes — and I find this almost unbearably good — "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping." Verified (100%) That repetition — "fog up," "fog down" — it's not descriptive writing, it's incantatory writing. Dickens is performing the fog, not just reporting it. And then — this is the genius of it — the fog closes in on the Lord Chancellor sitting in Lincoln's Inn Hall. The natural world and the legal world turn out to be the same fog.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Eleanor is identifying as a structural and poetic achievement is also a piece of ruthlessly accurate social observation. The Court of Chancery, by 1852, was a system so tangled in its own procedures that cases had genuinely been running for decades without resolution. The case of Jennens v. Jennens — which Dickens drew on for Jarndyce and Jarndyce — had been in Chancery for over a century. So when Dickens writes about fog, he is choosing the precise metaphor — one that obscures, disorients, and kills by slow suffocation. James Blackstone (social_historian)
The dual narration also has a specific social logic that I think is worth naming. The third-person narrator sees everything from above — institutions, systems, the sweep of historical injustice. Esther sees everything from below — from inside the human costs of those systems. She is an illegitimate woman with no legal standing, no inheritance, no place in the world that the law recognises. And Dickens gives this woman — this precise social nobody — one of the two controlling narrative voices of his longest and most complex novel. That is a structural argument, not merely a stylistic one.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me every time I return to Richard's opening chapters is how thoroughly Dickens flatters us into liking him. He arrives at Bleak House with Ada Clare, and the two of them are so luminously young, so genuinely appealing, that we're almost seduced alongside Esther. Dickens gives Richard wit, charm, physical energy — all the signals we read as promise. And then — very quietly, in almost throwaway asides — he plants the seeds of what will destroy him. There's that crucial early moment when Richard can't settle on a profession — law, medicine, the army — and he flits between them with almost comic speed. Esther and Jarndyce see it clearly as a warning sign. But Dickens has written Richard's restlessness with such infectious energy that the reader keeps giving him the benefit of the doubt. That gap between what we see and what we allow ourselves to believe — that's the trap Dickens sets for the reader.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And I think you need that seduction, Eleanor, because otherwise the Chancery machinery looks too nakedly brutal from the outset. What Dickens understood — and what I find remarkable given the limits of public knowledge at the time — is how the real court of Chancery operated as a slow-consumption machine. Cases in Chancery in the 1840s and 1850s could run for literally decades, accumulating costs that swallowed estates whole. The heirs of the original litigants would inherit the suits along with the debts. That moment has a real-world counterpart, and it still makes me angry. The double narration is doing something essential here, and I don't want us to leave without naming it.
