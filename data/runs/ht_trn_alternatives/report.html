Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
He does, and Dickens frames it with extraordinary economy. Stephen has come to ask his employer — the blusterous Bounderby — whether the law can release him from a wife who has become a torment. And Dickens gives him just one sentence of direct speech, but it carries the weight of an entire ruined life. 'I ha' coom to ask yo, sir, how I am to be ridded o' this woman.' The dialect is doing enormous work there — it places Stephen socially, morally, geographically, all at once. And Mrs. Sparsit's response — a 'gentle ejaculation, as having received a moral shock' — is one of Dickens's most perfectly aimed satirical pins. The woman who traffics in propriety is shocked — not by Stephen's suffering, but by its utterance.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And yet that dignity exists within a framework of absolute legal helplessness. Stephen cannot divorce his wife — divorce in mid-nineteenth-century England was a privilege of property, accessible to almost no working man. So what Dickens shows us, with that single question to Bounderby, is a man running up against the machinery of a system built without him in mind. And the consequences accumulate into something almost unbearable. Dickens describes Stephen as 'bound hand and foot, to a dead woman, and tormented by a demon in her shape.' That's not metaphor for its own sake — it's a precise description of what a legal institution does to a man without recourse.

The passage that haunts me — the one I return to whenever I think about this novel — is Louisa at the door of Stone Lodge, watching the fires of Coketown. It's late, her father has just left, and she stands there in the dark, staring at that lurid industrial glow. And Dickens gives us this extraordinary image of what she's doing in her mind. "It seemed as if, first in her own fire within the house, and then in the fiery haze without, she tried to discover what kind of woof Old Time, that greatest and longest-established Spinner of all, would weave from the threads he had already spun into a woman." And then — and this is the line that kills me — "But his factory is a secret place, his work is noiseless, and his Hands are mutes." The factory metaphor is doing something breathtaking there — Time itself becomes an industrial operation, grinding out human lives with the same indifference as the Coketown mills. And Louisa can't read the pattern being woven from her own life.

I don't dispute the ideological observation, but I think we risk reducing what is, at its core, a scene of profound moral failure on the part of a father.
