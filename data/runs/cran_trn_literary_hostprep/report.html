Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and the passage is short enough that we really should just hear it, because reading it is the argument. 'I have been so much startled—no, I've not been at all startled—don't mind me, my dear Miss Jessie—I've been very much surprised—in fact, I've had a caller, whom you knew once, my dear Miss Jessie.' What Gaskell has done there is remarkable — she has written the self-censorship in real time. Miss Jenkyns begins to feel something, catches herself feeling it, and immediately issues a correction — not to Miss Jessie, but to herself, out loud, in front of witnesses. The em dashes are doing enormous structural work here — each one is a small social flinch, a place where propriety reasserts itself over feeling. And Gaskell lets the syntax enact exactly what Cranford as a community enacts constantly: the management of emotion through form. The sentence doesn't just describe flustered dignity — it performs it.

Both of those readings are acute — I don't want to be the one who marches in and flattens the comedy.
