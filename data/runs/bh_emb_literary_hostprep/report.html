Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What you should listen for is the rhythm — because Dickens does something very unusual here. He doesn't begin with a character, or a scene, or even a plot — he begins with a word, repeated, until it becomes almost physical. Listen to what happens when you just let it wash over you. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." You feel it before you understand it. That anaphora — 'fog up,' 'fog down,' 'fog on,' 'fog in' — it's almost Biblical in its accumulation. By the time you reach the Greenwich pensioners and the shivering apprentice boy, you're already inside the fog yourself. Dickens has made you breathe it in — and I think that's completely deliberate.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And notice that the sentence structure itself enacts the fog — it doesn't stop, it just keeps spreading. There's no full stop until Dickens has covered the entire geography of the Thames estuary — Greenwich, Essex, Kent, the ships, the barges. The syntax is doing the fog's work. And what I find extraordinary is how it moves — it travels socially as well as geographically. From the open river to the 'ancient Greenwich pensioners,' to the 'wrathful skipper,' to his 'shivering little 'prentice boy' — it descends through the social hierarchy. The fog does not discriminate — but Dickens notices exactly who it hits hardest.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Both of which are true — and I don't want to be the one who drags everyone out of the music. But I think we need to notice where the fog is thickest. Dickens is quite precise about this. "The raw afternoon is rawest, and the dense fog is densest, and the muddy streets are muddiest near that leaden-headed old obstruction, appropriate ornament for the threshold of a leaden-headed old corporation, Temple Bar." He's not describing weather — he's pointing a finger. 'Leaden-headed' — used twice, so you can't miss it — is a direct charge of mental torpor against a legal institution. Temple Bar was the old gateway to the City of London — it had literally been an obstruction for centuries. Dickens knew his readers would recognise the joke — and the indictment behind it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
An audit in the strict sense — a reckoning of what an institution has produced, line by line. Dickens runs through every category of human wreckage Chancery has generated with the precision of a clerk totalling a ledger. And what he produces is devastating, because the categories are so specific. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." 'Every shire.' 'Every madhouse.' 'Every churchyard.' The repetition of 'every' is not poetic excess — it's a claim about total systemic reach. And this was not fiction — the Court of Chancery had cases in the 1840s and 1850s that had been running for decades, consuming entire estates. Dickens had done the research, and he was furious.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — because what Dickens is doing here is something quite radical for a Victorian novel. He's introducing a narrator who is simultaneously omniscient and obscured. The fog is the narrator's condition of knowing — or of not quite being able to know. Look at that phrase — 'the Lord High Chancellor looks into the lantern that has no light in it.' "The attendant wigs are all stuck in a fog-bank." Dickens has placed the seat of legal authority inside a condition of epistemic blindness. The court cannot see clearly — and the narrative voice inherits that condition. We will spend the entire novel trying to see through fog — and Dickens built that in from sentence one.
