Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The thing about Eliot is that the comedy and the compassion are the same move — she doesn't separate them. Most novelists give you the joke or the sympathy — Eliot gives you both simultaneously, and it's a more demanding ask of the reader. The Casaubon passage in chapter eleven is a perfect example — the narrator steps forward, almost clears her throat, and says: no, wait, let's look at this properly. She builds this gorgeous absurdist series — Mrs. Cadwallader's contempt, Sir James's opinion of his rival's legs — and then she lands on something quite astonishing. "Mr. Casaubon, too, was the centre of his own world; if he was liable to think that others were providentially made for him, and especially to consider them in the light of their fitness for the author of a 'Key to all Mythologies,' this trait is not quite alien to us, and, like the other mendicant hopes of mortals, claims some of our pity." That phrase — mendicant hopes of mortals — is doing the work of a whole philosophy. He's begging, essentially — begging the universe to confirm that he matters — and Eliot says: yes, we do that too, and it is pitiable, and it deserves pity. That's not heavy going — that is a radical act of generosity toward an objectively difficult man, delivered with wit.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I love the performance — Oliver always makes me want to agree with him — but I think we need to look at what that invitation to pity is actually doing. The narrator protests — and that word protest is interesting, she uses it herself — against hasty judgment of Casaubon. And who is she defending him against? Mrs. Cadwallader, Sir James, Celia — the very people who can see the social transaction clearly. Celia thinks he looks wrong for Dorothea — and she's right, not because of his personal appearance, but because of what he is as a social actor. By asking us to go inside Casaubon's consciousness — with what fading of hopes, what fixity of self-delusion — the narrator redirects our attention from the damage he does to Dorothea toward his inner suffering. The sympathy is real — I don't deny that — but it's also, structurally, a mystification.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel, I must resist the word mystification — which implies a kind of deliberate concealment where I see something quite the opposite. What Eliot is doing in that passage is exercising — insisting upon — the fundamental moral discipline of imaginative sympathy. The novel has spent ten chapters allowing various characters to diminish Casaubon — and Eliot says: not so fast. To understand a person from inside their own consciousness is not to excuse them — it is to take their humanity seriously. Dr. Johnson said something to the effect that we rarely think of others' feelings with the same vividness we apply to our own — and Eliot's project is precisely to repair that deficiency.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I do — and I think the passage repays reading very carefully, because it is one of the most precisely observed sentences about a certain kind of moral vanity I have ever encountered. Eliot writes — and I shall read it slowly, because the rhythm matters — "She felt some disappointment, of which she was yet ashamed, that there was nothing for her to do in Lowick; and in the next few minutes her mind had glanced over the possibility, which she would have preferred, of finding that her home would be in a parish which had a larger share of the world's misery, so that she might have had more active duties in it." She is ashamed of her disappointment — which means she knows the disappointment is not entirely virtuous. She wants a parish with more misery — not for the suffering people's sake, but so that she might have more active duties. That is not oppression. That is the deepest variety of egoism — the egoism that clothes itself entirely in the language of selflessness.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund is right that there's egoism in it — I wouldn't deny that. But ask yourself: why does a young woman of genuine intelligence, genuine energy, need to justify her desire to act by attaching it to someone else's suffering? Because there is no legitimate channel for that energy — not for a woman of her class and moment. It's a genuine tension, and I think it's more productive to say that than to paper over it. I want to say something about Dorothea and Lydgate being held apart — because I flagged this before we started and I still think it matters.
