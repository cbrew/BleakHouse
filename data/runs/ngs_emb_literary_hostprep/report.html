James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd call it the most damning thing he says about himself — and I mean that structurally, not morally. Jasper isn't wrong about the market — in 1891 the three-volume novel was collapsing, the circulating libraries were losing their grip, and the mass-market periodical press was absolutely ravenous for copy. He's describing iron reality. But notice what he also says — listen to the full shape of his argument. 'We are not geniuses, and if we sit down in a spirit of long-eared gravity we shall produce only commonplace stuff.' He's already foreclosed the alternative — dismissed integrity before he's considered it. That's not honesty — that's rationalisation dressed in the clothes of honesty.

And that's exactly where the formal question bites hardest. Because in the passage where Reardon speaks about the weight dragging men down, the syntax does something completely different from Jasper's syntax. 'The world has no pity on a man who can't do or produce something it thinks worth money.' That sentence isn't performing — it's collapsing.
