Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Right — and I want to make the argument in the text itself, because it only works if you hear it. Listen to this opening — the very first sentence. "A wide plain, where the broadening Floss hurries on between its green banks to the sea, and the loving tide, rushing to meet it, checks its passage with an impetuous embrace." Now — no character, no plot, no named person. Just water meeting water in what she calls an impetuous embrace — and you're already emotionally involved. That's not description — that's seduction by syntax. And then she does something even more audacious — she drops the narrative mask entirely and says this is a memory. She writes — and this is the line that gets me every time — "How lovely the little river is, with its dark changing wavelets! It seems to me like a living companion while I wander along the bank, and listen to its low, placid voice, as to the voice of one who is deaf and loving." Deaf and loving. Two words that shouldn't work together — and they devastate. Because unconditional love that cannot hear you back — that is the entire emotional logic of the novel in eight syllables.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That reading is beautiful — and I mean that sincerely. But I want to ask what else that opening is doing — what it's asking us not to see. Because those black ships on the Floss — laden with fir-planks and oil-bearing seed and coal — that is an industrial economy. Eliot gives us St Ogg's as a picture — red roofs, purple water — and the commerce is tucked inside the picture like cargo in a hold. It's all there — the ships, the seed, the coal — but rendered so beautifully that the question of who owns it never quite forms. That's what I'd call ideological work done at the level of the sentence.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
If I may — I think both of you are circling something rather more fundamental. It isn't primarily about what the landscape hides, nor about the scaffolding of a tragedy. Eliot is making a philosophical claim about how a soul gets formed — how the self acquires the capacity for feeling at all. Later in those early pages, she makes it explicit. "These familiar flowers, these well-remembered bird-notes, this sky, with its fitful brightness, these furrowed and grassy fields, each with a sort of personality given to it by the capricious hedgerows — such things as these are the mother-tongue of our imagination, the language that is laden with all the subtle, inextricable associations the fleeting hours of our childhood left behind them." The mother-tongue of our imagination. That is Eliot's thesis — not about class, not about tragedy — but about the deep structure of moral being. If you cannot feel the hedgerows — if you are uprooted from the language your imagination first learned to speak — you cannot love properly.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I want to push back on that — gently but seriously. That mother-tongue passage is gorgeous — but notice what it naturalizes. It was a clear frosty January day on which Mr Tulliver first came downstairs. The bright sun on the chestnut boughs and the roofs opposite his window had made him impatiently declare that he would be caged up no longer; he thought everywhere would be more cheery under this sunshine than his bedroom; for he knew nothing of the bareness below, which made the flood of sunshine importunate, as if it had an unfeeling pleasure in showing the empty places, and the marks where well-known objects once had been. The impression on his mind that it was but yesterday when he received the letter from Mr Gore was so continually implied in his talk, and the attempts to convey to him the idea that many weeks had passed and much had happened since then had been so soon swept away by recurrent forgetfulness, that even Mr Turnbull had begun to despair of preparing him to meet the facts by previous knowledge. The full sense of the present could only be imparted gradually by new experience,—not by mere words, which must remain weaker than the impressions left by the old experience. This resolution to come downstairs was heard with trembling by the wife and children. Mrs Tulliver said Tom must not go to St Ogg’s at the usual hour, he must wait and see his father downstairs; and Tom complied, though with an intense inward shrinking from the painful scene. The hearts of all three had been more deeply dejected than ever during the last few days. For Guest & Co. had not bought the mill; both mill and land had been knocked down to Wakem, who had been over the premises, and had laid before Mr Deane and Mr Glegg, in Mrs Tulliver’s presence, his willingness to employ Mr Tulliver, in case of his recovery, as a manager of the business. This proposition had occasioned much family debating. Uncles and aunts were almost unanimously of opinion that such an offer ought not to be rejected when there was nothing in the way but a feeling in Mr Tulliver’s mind, which, as neither aunts nor uncles shared it, was regarded as entirely unreasonable and childish,—indeed, as a transferring toward Wakem of that indignation and hatred which Mr Tulliver ought properly to have directed against himself for his general quarrelsomeness, and his special exhibition of it in going to law. Here was an opportunity for Mr Tulliver to provide for his wife and daughter without any assistance from his wife’s relations, and without that too evident descent into pauperism which makes it annoying to respectable people to meet the degraded member of the family by the wayside. Mr Tulliver, Mrs Glegg considered, must be made to feel, when he came to his right mind, that he could never humble himself enough; for that had come which she had always foreseen would come of his insolence in time past “to them as were the best friends he’d got to look to.” Mr Glegg and Mr Deane were less stern in their views, but they both of them thought Tulliver had done enough harm by his hot-tempered crotchets and ought to put them out of the question when a livelihood was offered him; Wakem showed a right feeling about the matter,— he had no grudge against Tulliver.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Yes — and the text supports it, quite precisely. Look at what Eliot gives us when Tulliver comes downstairs — he expects sunshine to be cheering, he knows nothing of the bareness below. And then this — listen to the language: "the flood of sunshine importunate, as if it had an unfeeling pleasure in showing the empty places, and the marks where well-known objects once had been." Importunate sunshine — nature itself performing the cruelty of exposure. But who stripped the house? Not the weather — creditors, lawyers, the machinery of debt. And notice what follows: Wakem buys the mill and offers Tulliver employment as its manager — in his own mill, for his enemy. The aunts and uncles think this is entirely reasonable — and Eliot renders that reasonableness without irony, almost. That almost is where the novel lives — in the gap between what the respectable world calls reasonable and what it actually costs a human being.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What that scene reveals, for me, is the shape of a man's character in extremity. Tulliver's hot-tempered crotchets — as the aunts call them — are not merely unfortunate personal habits. They are the expression of a man who has never submitted his will to anything outside himself. The catastrophe is the moment that tests whether there is anything more durable in him than pride. Eliot is not writing about debt law — she is writing about the discovery of what one actually is when the world strips everything else away.

But amidst all this new passionate tumult there were the old voices making themselves heard with rising power, till, from time to time, the tumult seemed quelled. Was that existence which tempted her the full existence she dreamed? Where, then, would be all the memories of early striving; all the deep pity for another’s pain, which had been nurtured in her through years of affection and hardship; all the divine presentiment of something higher than mere personal enjoyment, which had made the sacredness of life? She might as well hope to enjoy walking by maiming her feet, as hope to enjoy an existence in which she set out by maiming the faith and sympathy that were the best organs of her soul. And then, if pain were so hard to her , what was it to others? “Ah, God! preserve me from inflicting—give me strength to bear it.” How had she sunk into this struggle with a temptation that she would once have thought herself as secure from as from deliberate crime?
