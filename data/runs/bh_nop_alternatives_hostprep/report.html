Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver reads it beautifully — and he's right that the rhythm is extraordinary. I wouldn't disagree with a syllable of that. But I think there's something he's perhaps leaving aside, which is the moral and spiritual weight of that fog. Dickens is writing in a tradition that goes back well before the Victorian novel — back to Milton, frankly. The fog is confusion. It is the condition of fallen human beings who cannot see clearly. He writes that the fog is "most thick" at the heart of the Court of Chancery — where the Lord High Chancellor sits. The institution of the law is not merely corrupt — it is blinded. And the people inside it cannot help but share that blindness. That's not a political diagnosis. That's a Christian and tragic view of what happens when human beings organise themselves without humility. Oliver's staging metaphor is apt — but the smoke in Dickens's theatre is also incense burning in the wrong direction.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'm going to push back on both of you — warmly, but firmly. When Edmund says the fog represents human smallness and spiritual confusion, and when Oliver says it's theatre — both readings evacuate it of a specific social content. Dickens is not describing universal human confusion. He is describing a specific city, a specific court, a specific class of victims. The fog doesn't fall equally on everyone. Listen to where it accumulates in that first chapter — it's in the law courts, in the tenements, in the rookeries. He describes Tom-all-Alone's — the ruined slum at the heart of the novel — as a place of pestilence and darkness. And that darkness is not metaphysical. It is material. The people who can afford to be somewhere else are somewhere else. The people who cannot — Jo the crossing sweeper, the brickmakers' wives — they live inside the fog permanently. Calling it 'atmosphere' is the most dangerous thing you can do to a radical novel — it turns a diagnosis into a mood.
