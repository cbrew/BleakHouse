### Deep Dive: Richard's Slow Ruin in Chancery (deep_dive)

[Host]

  Richard Carstone: ward of Chancery, cousin of Ada, and one of the great slow catastrophes in English fiction.  (@0.98x *Richard Carstone,catastrophes*)
  Daniel, I want to start with something you said before we came on air — that Richard doesn't just fall into Chancery, he gets marketed to.  (*marketed*)
  That the system identifies exactly the kind of person it can use. Can you walk us through that?  (<1500ms> @0.98x *identifies,use*)

[Daniel Rosen]

  Chancery is not a passive institution that Richard stumbles into. It is an active system with a logic of perpetuation.  (@0.99x *active,perpetuation*)
  And that logic requires a particular kind of subject — someone young, someone without capital, someone whose hope can be harvested rather than fulfilled.  (@0.97x *harvested,hope*)
  Richard arrives as a ward — meaning the court already has legal custody of his future before he makes a single decision.  (@0.99x *custody,future*)
  The very structure of wardship means his identity is legally bound to the outcome of Jarndyce and Jarndyce.  (@0.97x *wardship,bound*)
  So when people say Richard chose this — I want to ask: chose it from what position, with what information, inside what structure?  (@1.01x *chose,structure*)
  The system produces his naivety. It needs him credulous. A sceptical Richard Carstone is no use to Chancery at all.  (<1500ms> @0.99x *produces,credulous*)

[Edmund Leigh]
  I'm afraid that argument, however elegantly constructed, proves too much.  (+220ms @0.94x *proves too much*)

[Host]

### Close Reading: Jo and the Machine of Social Indifference (close_reading)

[Host]
  We've been watching Richard Carstone consumed by Chancery — a man with agency, with choices, destroyed incrementally by an institution.  (+600ms @0.98x *agency,choices,institution*)
  Now I want to move to a figure who has none of that — who never had it — Jo, the crossing-sweeper.  (@0.98x *none,Jo*)
  Daniel, you said something before we recorded that I haven't been able to shake: that Jo doesn't die despite the system, he dies because it's working.  (@0.98x *working*)
  Can you walk us into that — pick the passage you'd use as exhibit A for indifference as infrastructure?  (<800ms> @0.98x *exhibit A,infrastructure*)

[Daniel Rosen]
  The passage I keep returning to is in chapter sixteen — Jo is on the streets, and the refrain is relentless: 'move on.'  (+200ms @0.99x *relentless,move on*)
  Dickens gives us the full machinery of it — the police, the beadle, the law — all saying the same two words.  (@0.99x *machinery*)
  And the devastating thing Dickens does is ask: move on to where?  (@0.97x *where*)
  He writes — and I want to get this exactly right — something to the effect that Jo moves on through the streets of London with no fixed abode, no knowledge, no connexion, and no place to go.  (@0.97x [setup])
  > 'He is not one of Mrs. Pardiggle's Tockahoopo Indians; he is not one of Mrs. Jellyby's lambs... he is the ordinary home-made article.'  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *ordinary,home-made*)
  That phrase — 'home-made article' — is not sentiment. It is an indictment.  ([commentary] *indictment*)
  Jo is produced right here, by this city, by this economy, by these institutions — and then he is told there is no room for what has been produced.  (<800ms> @0.99x *produced,no room*)
  That is not cruelty. That is design.  (<1500ms> @0.95x *design*)

[Edmund Leigh]
  Daniel, I won't pretend that reading is without force — the 'move on' passages have tremendous cumulative power.  (+220ms @0.94x *cumulative power*)
  But I think you are doing what the sentimentalist and the ideologue share in common: you are removing the human agent from the picture.  (@0.94x *human agent*)
  Because Dickens does not give us an abstraction called 'the system.' He gives us Bucket. He gives us Chadband. He gives us Skimpole.  (@0.94x *Bucket,Chadband,Skimpole*)
  He gives us, most devastatingly, Harold Skimpole — who is the very image of the comfortable man who refuses moral engagement by pretending to be a child.  (@0.93x *Skimpole,moral engagement*)
  When Jo dies, Dickens turns on his reader directly — he does not say 'the system has failed.' He says, in effect: this is upon you.  (@0.94x *upon you*)
  The famous passage — 'Dead, your Majesty. Dead, my lords and gentlemen' — that is not a charge against capitalism. It is a charge against conscience.  (@0.93x [setup] *conscience*)
  > 'Dead, your Majesty. Dead, my lords and gentlemen. Dead, Right Reverends and Wrong Reverends of every order.'  (+150ms @0.92x [reading] *Dead,Wrong Reverends*)
  That is Dickens putting individuals in the dock — specific, named, titled individuals who chose not to see.  (<1500ms> @0.95x [commentary] *chose*)

[Daniel Rosen]
  Edmund, I love you for that quote — and it is magnificent rhetoric — but notice what it does.  (+240ms @0.99x *notice*)
  It addresses lords and reverends and majesty — it addresses the powerful. And it tells them to feel shame.  (@0.99x *shame*)
  But Jo has been moved on by a police constable, not by a lord. By a beadle. By Mrs. Pardiggle who is busy saving savages she'll never meet.  (@1.01x *constable,beadle,Pardiggle*)
  The cruelty that kills Jo is entirely ordinary — it requires no malice, no broken conscience, just people doing their jobs.  (@0.99x *ordinary,jobs*)
  When you frame it as a question of repentance, you actually let the structure off the hook — because structures don't repent.  (<1500ms> *repent*)

[Oliver Trevelyan]
  Can I — I want to do something perhaps slightly reckless here, which is to actually read some Jo, because I think you're both in danger of making him into a symbol before you've heard him.  (+260ms @1.02x *read,symbol*)
  There's a moment — it's the inquest scene, chapter eleven — where Jo is asked by the Coroner if he can say anything, and he says he 'can't exactly say' what Nemo was.  ([setup])
  > 'He wos wery good to me, he wos,' Jo says. And then he's dismissed. That's all he gets.  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *wery good*)
