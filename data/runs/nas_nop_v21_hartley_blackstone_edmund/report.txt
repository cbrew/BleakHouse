### The Strike: Thornton's Reckoning and the Cost of Progress (discussion)

[Host]
  Welcome back to North and South Unpacked.  (+500ms @0.98x *North and South Unpacked*)
  We've spent the last stretch tracing the architecture of Margaret Hale's moral education — her encounters with the mill, the workers, the Higgins family.  (@0.98x *moral education*)
  Now we turn to what is, I'd argue, the novel's most politically charged episode: the strike.  (@0.97x *strike*)
  The Milton workers walk out, Thornton holds firm, Irish hands are brought in, and Margaret steps into the mill yard — with consequences no one could have predicted.  (@0.98x *Margaret,mill yard*)
  Eleanor, you've written about how novelists use scenes of collective action — what does Gaskell do with this one?  (<800ms> @0.98x *collective action*)

[Eleanor Hartley]
  What strikes me first — if you'll forgive the pun — is how Gaskell choreographs the crowd.  (+220ms *choreographs,crowd*)
  Victorian novelists were terrified of collective bodies — they tend to render crowds as bestial, undifferentiated.  (@1.01x *bestial,undifferentiated*)
  But Gaskell does something much more sophisticated: the mob outside Thornton's mill retains individual faces — we know Boucher is in there, broken and desperate.  (*Boucher,individual*)
  The violence has a human origin, not just a social one.  (@0.98x *human origin*)
  And then there's the sentence that's always haunted me from that chapter — Margaret telling Thornton, and I believe this is very close to the text:  (@0.97x [setup] *Margaret,Thornton*)
  > 'If you have any courage or noble quality in you, go out and face them like a man.'  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *courage,face,man*)
  It's an astonishing speech act — Margaret is daring him to meet his workers as a human being, not as an economic authority.  (<800ms> [commentary] *human being,economic authority*)
  And the grammar is imperative — she's giving an order to a man who owns a mill.  (<1500ms> @1.02x *imperative,order*)

[James Blackstone]
  I want to hold that word 'man' for a moment, because historically it carries enormous freight.  (+220ms @0.96x *man,freight*)
  The 1840s and 1850s saw an extraordinary number of industrial disputes in the cotton trade — the Preston lock-out of 1853 is usually cited as Gaskell's model.  (@0.96x *Preston,lock-out*)
  What made those disputes so bitter was the absolute refusal of masters to recognise that workers had any right to a voice in setting wages.  (@0.95x *voice,wages*)
  Thornton is scrupulously placed within that ideology — he believes in the market as the only legitimate arbiter.  (@0.96x *market,arbiter*)
  And yet Gaskell gives him a line that cuts against his own position — something like his admission to Margaret that he cannot explain his actions purely by self-interest.  (@0.96x *self-interest,admission*)
  That crack in the ideology is precisely where the novel does its most interesting historical work.  (<1500ms> @0.97x *crack,historical work*)

[Edmund Leigh]
  James is quite right about the historical scaffolding, and I wouldn't quarrel with any of it.  (+240ms @0.94x)
  But I find myself more interested in what the strike does to Thornton's soul than to his ledger.  (@0.93x *soul,ledger*)
  He goes out to face that crowd — at Margaret's insistence, remember — and he is stoned.  (@0.94x *stoned,Margaret's insistence*)
  Margaret herself is struck by a stone meant for him.  (@0.93x *struck,stone*)
  Now, the crowd reads this as courtship — they hoot and jeer at Thornton for having a woman defend him.  (@0.94x *courtship,defend*)
  And Thornton himself, in that dreadful misreading that drives the novel's middle section, takes it to mean the same thing.  (@0.93x *misreading,middle section*)
  What Gaskell is doing is the oldest thing in English fiction — testing character under pressure.  (@0.94x *testing character,pressure*)
  How does a proud man behave when he discovers that his courage required a woman's dare?  (<1500ms> @0.93x *proud,dare*)

[Eleanor Hartley]
  Edmund, yes — and Gaskell layers the irony beautifully, because Margaret acted from pity and contempt, not from love.  (+220ms @1.01x *pity,contempt*)
  She's mortified afterwards — the narrator gives us her horror at being seen to shelter him.  (*mortified,horror*)
  The architecture of the scene is almost symmetrical: Thornton descends to face the crowd, Margaret descends further still to face the crowd for him.  (@0.99x *symmetrical,descends*)
  But neither of them understands the other's motive, and that mutual opacity is the engine of everything that follows.  (<800ms> *mutual opacity,engine*)

[Edmund Leigh]
