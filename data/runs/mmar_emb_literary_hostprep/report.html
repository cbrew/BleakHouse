James Blackstone (social_historian)
Oliphant is clearly a superb technician — I don't dispute that for a moment. My question isn't about the author's awareness — it's about what the text invites the reader to inhabit. And what it invites us to inhabit is Lucilla's perspective — fully, seductively, without a competing voice. The 'master-hand' — notice it's not 'a Lucilla' or even 'a woman.' It's a hand. A hand is the instrument of labour. The hand belongs to whoever can afford to exercise it. Lucilla can afford to be a master-hand because her father is a wealthy doctor and she has no need to work for money. I'm not saying that diminishes her — I'm saying the novel never quite makes us feel the weight of that foundation, because the narrator is too delighted by the edifice.

That oxymoron — 'sublime despondency' — is doing so much structural work I almost can't bear it. 'Sublime' is the register of Burke, of Kant — overwhelming, transcendent, beyond ordinary human scale.
