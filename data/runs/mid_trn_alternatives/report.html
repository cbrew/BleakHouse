Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Delighted to be here — and Middlemarch is, frankly, the novel I would take to a desert island if I weren't afraid I'd never want to leave it. But let's start where Eliot puts us — with Dorothea, riding back toward Lowick, sinking into silence. Now, that silence is a novelist's masterstroke — because what follows it is one of the funniest and most sympathetic sentences in the book. Eliot writes — and I want to read this slowly because every clause earns its keep — "She felt some disappointment, of which she was yet ashamed, that there was nothing for her to do in Lowick; and in the next few minutes her mind had glanced over the possibility, which she would have preferred, of finding that her home would be in a parish which had a larger share of the world's misery, so that she might have had more active duties in it." She is disappointed — ashamed of being disappointed — and then immediately wishes she lived somewhere more miserable. That is not satire — or not only satire — that is a novelist who loves her heroine and sees her completely.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me in that passage is the precision of the moral diagnosis. Dorothea's error is not that she wishes to do good — that impulse is genuine and admirable. The error is that she cannot separate the wish to serve from the wish to be seen — even by herself — as someone who serves grandly. Eliot notes she "made a picture of more complete devotion" — not devotion itself, but a picture of it. That single word — picture — is the whole of Eliot's indictment, and it is delivered without a raised eyebrow. This is what I mean when I say the moral imagination is at work — not argument, not programme, just the exact word in the exact place.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't want to quarrel with either of those readings — but I think we should notice what Eliot is also doing structurally here. Dorothea has enormous energy, real intelligence, and a genuine appetite to change material conditions for people in poverty. And the novel's opening movement is about the systematic redirection of all that capacity — toward a husband, toward a scholar's footnotes, toward pictures of devotion rather than devotion's object. The shame Eliot identifies isn't just personal vanity — it's what a society produces when it tells a young woman that wanting to act on the world is itself embarrassing. The passage is tender and satirical at once — but the target of the satire isn't only Dorothea.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And then Eliot turns the camera — which is exactly the right word — onto Casaubon himself. The next chapter contains what I think is one of the most extraordinary pieces of narrative rhetoric in the Victorian novel. Eliot has spent chapters letting everyone — Mrs. Cadwallader, Sir James, Celia, Mr. Brooke — take their shots at this dry, pedantic clergyman. And then she simply stops and says: but wait. She writes — and this is Eliot at her most quietly devastating — "Mr. Casaubon, too, was the centre of his own world; if he was liable to think that others were providentially made for him, and especially to consider them in the light of their fitness for the author of a 'Key to all Mythologies,' this trait is not quite alien to us, and, like the other mendicant hopes of mortals, claims some of our pity." Mendicant hopes of mortals — meaning we are all beggars for recognition, every one of us. Casaubon's vanity is not monstrous — it is ordinary, and that is far more unsettling.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That passage is Eliot at her most Johnsonian, I think. Johnson too had that capacity to arrive at sympathy through clear-eyed judgment rather than despite it. And notice how the narrator earns the right to demand our sympathy for Casaubon — by first conceding everything that is unattractive about him. She writes: "even Milton, looking for his portrait in a spoon, must submit to have the facial angle of a bumpkin." No reflected image — however small the mirror — does justice to the inner life. That is the epistemological ground of the entire novel — and Eliot states it here, in a subordinate clause, almost as an aside.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd add one thing: the narrator's defence of Casaubon isn't politically neutral. She asks us to consider, as she puts it, "what is the report of his own consciousness about his doings or capacity" — to attend to his inner pressure, his fading hopes. That is a radical demand in a culture that equates visible achievement with worth. And Lydgate, later in the novel, arrives at exactly the same precipice — his pride stripped away by financial pressure until he must consider begging from his uncle. Eliot keeps returning to that image — the gifted man, the ambitious person, reduced to calculating the contents of other people's pockets. It is the novel's cruelest joke: aspiration and humiliation share the same address.

What was he to do? He saw even more keenly than Rosamond did the dreariness of taking her into the small house in Bride Street, where she would have scanty furniture around her and discontent within: a life of privation and life with Rosamond were two images which had become more and more irreconcilable ever since the threat of privation had disclosed itself. But even if his resolves had forced the two images into combination, the useful preliminaries to that hard change were not visibly within reach. And though he had not given the promise which his wife had asked for, he did not go again to Trumbull. He even began to think of taking a rapid journey to the North and seeing Sir Godwin. He had once believed that nothing would urge him into making an application for money to his uncle, but he had not then known the full pressure of alternatives yet more disagreeable. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And Eliot gives that humiliation its full weight — "he had not then known the full pressure of alternatives yet more disagreeable." That phrase — full pressure — is a physical sensation rendered in abstract language. Lydgate doesn't just face a hard choice — he is being crushed by the weight of it. The same man who began the novel with grand ambitions about medical reform ends up staring at a journey north to beg money from an uncle he has always disdained. The architecture of that fall is precise and merciless — and Eliot earns every inch of it.

Host (host)
We've been talking about the weight of expectation that hangs over Middlemarch's central figures. But expectation doesn't float in the air — it runs through institutions, through money, through the machinery of provincial society. Let's look at how Eliot builds that machinery — how the social world of Middlemarch actually works on its inhabitants.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The mechanism Eliot is most precise about is inheritance — the way anticipated money shapes behaviour before a single coin changes hands. Fred Vincy is the novel's clearest case study. His whole social position — the way Middlemarch views him, the way his father tolerates his failures — rests on what old Featherstone is expected to leave him. Eliot puts it with devastating precision — she sets up the quote like a theorem. "tacit expectations of what would be done for him by uncle Featherstone determined the angle at which most people viewed Fred Vincy in Middlemarch." The angle — not the opinion, not the judgment — the angle. That geometric metaphor tells you everything: the social gaze is already distorted by money that doesn't yet exist.

And what's wonderful is that Eliot doesn't let Fred off the hook by making him a villain. He's a victim of the system — and simultaneously its most enthusiastic collaborator. That detail about the twenty pounds he keeps back as — and this is her phrase — "a sort of seed-corn, which, planted by judgment, and watered by luck, might yield more than threefold." He's going to gamble it, of course. But the agricultural language — seed-corn, planted, watered — dresses up recklessness as patient husbandry. Eliot is laughing at him, but with such affection — it's irresistible.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think the comedy is inseparable from the moral seriousness, which is precisely Eliot's strength. Fred is not a bad young man — he has a conscience, which is why he agonises over the question of lying to his father. He prides himself — and this is rather touching — on keeping clear of lies. But his moral machinery is overwhelmed by his appetites and his circumstances in precisely equal measure. That is the condition Eliot is always examining — not wickedness, but weakness of will in a world that rewards the wrong things.

Her flame quickly burned up that light fuel; and, fed from within, soared after some illimitable satisfaction, some object which would never justify weariness, which would reconcile self-despair with the rapturous consciousness of life beyond self.
