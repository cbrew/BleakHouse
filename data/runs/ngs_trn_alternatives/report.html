The Grub Street Trap: Setting the Scene (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to New Grub Street Unpacked, the show that takes George Gissing's great novel about literary ambition and failure and asks what it still has to say to us. New Grub Street, published in 1891, is a novel about writers — writers who succeed, writers who are crushed, and the grinding commercial machinery that determines which is which. It's a novel about money and art and what happens when they collide — and it is, in my view, one of the most painfully honest books in the English language. Joining me today to unpack it are three readers who bring very different things to this table. Oliver Trevelyan is an actor and writer who has voiced more classic novel audiobooks than perhaps anyone alive — he comes to Gissing as a performer who hears the prose from the inside. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don whose lifelong conviction is that great novels are above all stories about individual character tested by circumstance — about moral fibre, not social programme. And Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads every novel as an anatomy of class power — and who I suspect will find Gissing exceptionally well-stocked material. Let's begin with the world Gissing puts us in — this London literary world of the 1880s, the world of Grub Street. Oliver, you've lived inside a great many novels — how does this one feel different from the first page?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The first thing you notice is the cold. Not cold as in emotional distance — though that's there too — but the physical, domestic cold of people who cannot afford to be warm. Gissing is extraordinarily precise about the texture of poverty — the specific indignity of it. And yet the novel has this extraordinary comedic energy running beneath the tragedy — these wonderfully drawn grotesques, these magnificent operators like Jasper Milvain. Gissing is ferocious about the literary marketplace, but he's also enormously funny about it — and that combination is what makes the novel a pleasure rather than a punishment. What strikes me about Reardon and Biffen — the two writers at the heart of the novel — is that Gissing gives them a tenderness and a dignity that the world they inhabit simply refuses to acknowledge.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right about the texture — Gissing is a novelist of surfaces in the very best sense. But I would resist the temptation to read this as primarily a novel about a system — an economic mechanism grinding down its victims. What Gissing is actually exploring — with great psychological subtlety — is why certain men fail and others succeed, and whether the difference lies in will or in circumstance. Reardon is a fascinating study in a particular kind of moral paralysis — a man of genuine gifts who is undone by his own temperament as much as by his poverty. Gissing doesn't let his characters off the hook simply because they are poor — and that, I think, is the mark of a genuinely serious novelist.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund raises a real question — about will and circumstance — and I want to press on it, because I think the novel does too. There's a passage where Reardon himself makes this diagnosis about London — about what the city actually does to men who come to it with ambitions. "London is only a huge shop, with an hotel on the upper storeys." That is Reardon speaking — a man who has been broken by the place — and the bitterness of it is earned. He goes on to say that he thought, as so many men do, that London was still the one centre of intellectual life — and that this belief was the delusion that ruined him. Now Edmund might say that is a failure of individual judgment — and it is, in part. But Gissing is showing us that the delusion is structural — it is what the literary marketplace requires of ambitious men in order to feed on them.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And the novel gives us another portrait alongside Reardon's self-diagnosis — Harold Biffen, who suffers just as completely but chooses a different kind of integrity. There's a moment that I find almost unbearable in its quiet dignity — Biffen describing a consumptive student who came to him for Latin lessons. "After a lesson or two I told him his cough was too bad, and he had no right to study until he got into better health; that was better, I think, than saying plainly he had no chance on earth." And then Biffen adds — and this is the line that breaks you — "the food I bought with his money was choking me." Here is a man who is himself on the edge of starvation, refusing payment out of a scruple so fine that most people in comfort would never even feel it. That is not merely character — that is the novel showing us what the system costs, in human terms, when the people it grinds are people of genuine moral beauty.

Reardon's Unraveling: Ambition and Failure (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been mapping the pressures that Grub Street exerts on everyone who enters it. Now let's put the novel's central figure under the glass — Edwin Reardon, and the question of whether his ruin is tragedy or self-inflicted defeat.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me first, every time I come back to Reardon, is the sheer physical comedy of his situation — which Gissing absolutely refuses to let be merely comic. Take the writing of that final volume. "The last volume was written in fourteen days. In this achievement Reardon rose almost to heroic pitch, for he had much to contend with beyond the mere labour of composition." And then Gissing proceeds to list what he had to contend with — lumbago, headaches, sore throat, the pawnbroker. It reads almost like a comic inventory — except it absolutely isn't, because every item on the list is a small humiliation. And then he finishes, writes 'The End,' and simply — lies back and lets time pass in blankness for a quarter of an hour.

Oliver's right that there's something almost farcical about it — but I want to hold onto what that list actually represents. Reardon is pawning his watch and selling his books not to live — but to buy himself the time to produce a commodity that may not sell. And the books he sells — Gissing is very specific about what they are. "Of course he had chosen from among the most valuable he possessed; such a multitude must else have been sold to make this sum of two pounds ten. Books are cheap, you know." That sardonic aside — 'Books are cheap, you know' — is Gissing speaking directly to the market that has destroyed his character. The intellectual labour that produced those books, the years of a poor student's accumulation — rendered worthless by the logic of the trade.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I want to enter a mild dissent here, because I think Gissing is more interested in character than Daniel allows. Consider how Reardon and Biffen first meet — and what that meeting reveals about the man. Biffen walks into a circulating library and asks for Reardon's books. "A voice near at hand asked the attendant if he had anything 'by Edwin Reardon.' The novelist turned in astonishment; that any casual mortal should inquire for his books seemed incredible." 'Any casual mortal' — that is not the self-pity of a victim. That is a man who has made a private peace with his own obscurity, and can still find it faintly absurd. The question the novel poses is not only what the market does to Reardon — it is what Reardon does, or fails to do, in response.

Host (host)
We've been sitting with Reardon's paralysis — his inability to write, to earn, to hold his marriage together. Now I want to turn to the figure who seems to move through that same world with almost obscene ease: Jasper Milvain.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Milvain is one of the great comic monsters of Victorian fiction, and I mean that as the highest possible compliment. He's not a villain who dissembles — he never pretends to be anything other than what he is. He announces his cynicism with the cheerfulness of a man reading out a train timetable. And Gissing gives him a perfect comic foil in Whelpdale — the man who fails at everything and then makes a living advising others how to succeed. Milvain can barely contain himself when he reports it. "A man who can't get anyone to publish his own books makes a living by telling other people how to write!" The exclamation mark is doing enormous work there — that's a man who recognises a kindred spirit.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That chill is structural, not merely moral. What Milvain understands — and what Reardon cannot bring himself to accept — is that the literary market doesn't reward talent. It rewards the appearance of productivity, the performance of relevance, the management of connections. Whelpdale's advisory scheme isn't a joke — it's a logical extension of how the whole system operates. And Whelpdale later gives us the most nakedly honest account of what that market actually wants. "What they want is the lightest and frothiest of chit-chatty information — bits of stories, bits of description, bits of scandal, bits of jokes, bits of statistics, bits of foolery." That isn't satire — that's a business plan that would feel quite at home today.
