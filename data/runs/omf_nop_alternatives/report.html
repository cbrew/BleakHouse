Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And what strikes me about that opening is how precisely Dickens locates labour in it. Gaffer Hexam is a working man who extracts value from the river — from the dead deposited there by a city that has used them up and thrown them away. The Thames in this novel is not a picturesque backdrop — it's an industrial sewer and a burial ground, and the men who work it are at the absolute bottom of the social order. What's remarkable is that Dickens frames the entire plot — the inheritance of old Harmon's dust fortune — through this same lens of extraction. Old Harmon made his fortune from dust heaps — from the waste matter of a city — and his heir's supposed death in the river ties these two images of salvage together from the very start. The novel is asking: who owns the dead, who owns the waste — and who profits?
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I don't wish to quarrel with that reading, but I think it risks flattening something that is, at its heart, a moral investigation. The river opening is undeniably arresting, but what Dickens is staging there is a question about the corruption of the soul — not merely of the economy. Gaffer Hexam has a code — a crooked one, but a code nonetheless. He distinguishes himself, with great indignation, from those who rob the dead — he merely takes what the dead no longer need. That self-justification is the novel's great subject — the stories people tell themselves about money and what they deserve. And Dickens, being a moralist of the first order, traces those stories with extraordinary patience — from Gaffer on the river, all the way up to the Veneerings in their brand-new dining room.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And the Veneerings' dinner party is where Dickens shows the machinery of social reproduction operating in real time. Everyone at that table is performing — performing wealth, performing connection, performing the fiction that they belong. And the Analytical Chemist — the butler who moves through the dinner with barely concealed contempt — is Dickens's way of placing a sceptical observer inside the spectacle. He offers the wine as if he were suggesting that the guests might consider poison — and that dark undercurrent is entirely deliberate. The wealth on display at the Veneering table is built on the same principles as Gaffer's river work — take what you can, ask no questions, and call it respectable.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
There is something to that, though I would put it differently. What Dickens understood — and here I think he is closer to Burke than to Marx — is that a society without inherited forms, without the tested weight of tradition, becomes a society of masks. The Veneerings are terrifying not because they are capitalists, but because they have no selves — the veneer goes all the way down. And against that, Dickens sets figures like Betty Higden or Lizzie Hexam — people of genuine moral substance, precisely because they have not been corrupted by the pursuit of money. The novel's great argument is not about economic systems — it is about whether the soul can survive prosperity.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And then there's the resurrection question — which is, if you think about it, the engine driving the entire plot. John Harmon is pulled from the river — or is believed to be — and his supposed death sets everything in motion. But he doesn't stay dead — he returns under assumed identities, watching his own life being lived without him. And Eugene Wrayburn is pulled from the river half-dead and remade by love and near-death into something approaching a decent human being. Even Betty Higden flees toward death like it's a kind of freedom — and the river receives her. The Thames in this novel is simultaneously a sewer, a graveyard, a font of rebirth, and a running commentary on everything money does to people.

Host (host)

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Noddy Boffin is one of the great comic creations in English fiction — and I say that without any qualification. He arrives on the page like a benevolent gnome — dusty, round, warm, genuinely delighted by almost everything. When Boffin begins his apparent transformation into a miser, obsessively collecting biographies of misers, there is a moment where Dickens gives him this magnificent line. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me about Boffin's arc is that it is, at its core, a test — not of society, but of individual character. Old Harmon's will doesn't simply transfer wealth — it sets a series of trials in motion. Boffin chooses to enact the role of the corrupted man, sacrificing his own reputation, in order to protect and educate young John Rokesmith and Bella Wilfer. That is an act of profound moral imagination — of the kind Johnson might have called, without embarrassment, virtue. Dickens is asking: can goodness survive wealth intact? And his answer, through Boffin, is a carefully qualified yes — but only through a kind of self-abnegation that most men could not manage.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to take Edmund's point seriously — and then push back, just a little. Yes, Boffin's transformation is ultimately revealed as performance — a calculated ruse to cure Bella of her mercenary values. But what Dickens is also doing, whether he fully intended it or not, is showing us exactly how wealth operates on perception. Every single person around Boffin — Wegg, Venus, even Bella herself — sees the money first and the man second. Silas Wegg's entire scheme of extortion is premised on the assumption that a man with dust-heap money must have obtained it dishonestly. That is not a moral argument — that is a structural one. In a world organised around inheritance and the accumulation of waste, suspicion of wealth is simply — rational.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And Wegg is the proof of that, isn't he — because he is both the most cynical and the most comic figure in the whole enterprise. His wooden leg, his ballad-singing, his literary pretensions — Dickens builds this grotesque with such loving precision. And yet when Wegg attempts to blackmail Boffin, the novel does something genuinely funny and genuinely devastating at the same time. Boffin simply — turns the tables. The moment Wegg is literally tossed out — launched by Mr Venus and the other characters — is pure Dickensian farce operating at the height of its powers. The physical comedy is the moral verdict.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'd like to return to something Daniel raised — the question of how others see Boffin once money enters the picture. There is a passage early in the novel where Dickens describes Boffin as a man who has come suddenly into a great fortune and who wears it, as it were, awkwardly — as if the clothes don't quite fit. That image of ill-fitting wealth is central to Dickens's moral architecture here. The truly corrupted characters — Lightwood drifting in his idleness, Podsnap armoured in his complacency — wear their prosperity as if it were a second skin. Boffin's awkwardness is, in the end, his salvation.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That contrast with Podsnap is exactly the right one to draw. Podsnappery — and Dickens gives us that magnificent coinage — is wealth that has become ideology. It is wealth that has forgotten its origins and now mistakes itself for virtue, for Englishness, for the natural order. Boffin never forgets where his money came from — and crucially, neither does Dickens. The dust heaps are always there — physically, symbolically — as the source that cannot be aestheticised away. That is what makes Boffin's moral reckoning so much more interesting than, say, a straightforward inheritance plot. The wealth is soiled from the start — and yet from that soil something genuinely good is made possible.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What I find most extraordinary — as someone who has actually read this novel aloud, for many hours — is the moment the deception is revealed. When Mrs Boffin breaks down and confesses the scheme to Bella, the emotional release is enormous — and it catches the reader completely off guard. Because Dickens has been so committed to the performance of Boffin's greed that even attentive readers have genuinely wondered whether the good man is truly gone. That is the craft of it — the novelist playing the same game as his character. Boffin performs miserliness; Dickens performs the dark novel he is not, in fact, writing.
Host (host)

And the chief pupil in that education is, of course, Bella Wilfer — who receives the full force of Boffin's performance, and is entirely changed by it. Her story — of a young woman seduced by the idea of wealth, and then slowly, painfully, redeemed — is where we'll turn next.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me as remarkable is how seriously Dickens takes Bella's moral education — it is, in the old sense, a formation of character. One thinks of the great tradition of the novel of self-knowledge: the character who must be stripped of illusions before they can see clearly. Bella's flaw is not greed in the abstract — it is what one might call the confusion of value: she has mistaken the price of things for their worth. And Boffin's household is precisely the environment in which that confusion is made visible — and ultimately, corrected. What I find genuinely moving is the scene in which she turns upon the apparently corrupted Boffin and defends John Rokesmith. She has no material reason to take his side — indeed, every financial interest points the other way. And yet she does. That is the moment Dickens has been building toward — the moment character asserts itself against interest.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to sit with the discomfort of Bella's position for a moment longer, because I think Edmund is moving toward the resolution rather too quickly. When Bella says she wants to be rich, she is not merely expressing a personal failing — she is articulating something the novel knows is structurally produced. She grew up in Holloway, in that wonderfully named house the Wilfer family calls the Boffin-ish establishment — cramped, respectable, and bitterly insufficient. Her desire for money is not a moral aberration — it is the perfectly rational response of a clever woman to a society in which women's security depends entirely on the wealth of the men they marry. Dickens half-knows this, and then pulls back from it — by making her redemption a matter of the heart rather than a transformation of the conditions that produced her desire. The novel wants her to choose love over money — and she does — but the comfort Dickens provides her is that Harmon turns out to be wealthy after all.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel, you're quite right, and I think Dickens knew it too — there's something slightly sheepish about that ending, isn't there. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take Daniel's point about structural conditions — I am not entirely unsympathetic to it. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And I'll grant that reckoning is real — Dickens earns it emotionally, even if he flinches from its full implications. What interests me is the figure of Pa — R. Wilfer — because he is the novel's image of what it means to be good without being rich. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What interests me rather more than the theatrical scaffolding is the moral question underneath it. Harmon's disguise is not mere trickery — it is a test he sets himself, to discover whether love can exist independent of fortune. One thinks of the old romance tradition: the prince who hides his rank to find a true-hearted beloved. What Dickens adds — and this is his genius — is the psychological cost. Harmon must watch the woman he loves being courted by the very phantom he has invented. And he must watch the Boffins — good, generous souls — knowingly play out a pretence that would horrify them if they were not themselves part of it.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I think we have to press on what the disguise is actually disguising, though. John Harmon assumes the name John Rokesmith — a name that means nothing, a man with no property, no history. And in that condition, he is hired as a secretary — a paid employee in the household that should by rights be his. Dickens is showing us, with great precision, what a man looks like when he is stripped of inherited wealth. He becomes legible to society only as a unit of labour. The romance reading is there, I grant Edmund that — but the social reading cuts right through it.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel's point about labour is sharp — but I keep coming back to the sheer discomfort Dickens builds into the performance of being nobody. And Harmon must simply absorb it. As Dickens puts it, Rokesmith had 'a certain air upon him of knowing that he was a disreputable fellow in the transaction.' The Book's Backbone: Plot, Theme, and Transformation Across the Arc (discussion)

Host (host)
We've been delighting in Dickens's grotesques and comedians — but behind all that magnificent noise, there is a spine. This novel has an architecture — and I want to spend some time with it now, because the plot of Our Mutual Friend is doing something quite remarkable.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me every time I return to this novel is the sheer audacity of the central device. John Harmon — the heir to the dust mounds — fakes his own death before the novel has barely drawn breath. And then he spends the entire novel disguised as Rokesmith, watching the woman he's supposed to marry, watching her become someone he genuinely falls in love with. It's a romantic comedy device that Dickens transforms into a meditation on identity. Who are we when we strip the name and the fortune away? Harmon's self-description in that extraordinary chapter of self-examination — 'I, JOHN HARMON, DISGRACED AND DEGRADED' — is one of the strangest, most modern passages Dickens ever wrote.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I would say the Harmon plot is only half the spine, Oliver. The novel's true backbone is the story of Eugene Wrayburn — and what happens to a man of complete moral idleness when he is forced by circumstance to become something more than he intended. Wrayburn is, at the novel's opening, genuinely dangerous. His pursuit of Lizzie Hexam is not admirable — it is the casual predation of the privileged on the vulnerable. And yet Dickens does something profoundly interesting with him — he earns his redemption through suffering. Bradley Headstone's assault nearly kills him, and in that near-death, something is genuinely remade. The novel insists that transformation is possible — but that it costs something real.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I want to take that word 'transformation' and put it under some pressure. Because who gets to be transformed in this novel, and who doesn't? Harmon gets to reclaim his fortune and his identity. Wrayburn gets the girl and a second chance at being a gentleman. Bella Wilfer transforms from a woman who prizes money above all into one who discovers she prizes love — and is rewarded with both. But Bradley Headstone — the man who worked hardest of all, who clawed himself up through sheer grinding effort — he is destroyed. And Charlie Hexam, who also struggled up, becomes a calculating little trimmer who abandons his own sister. The pattern is not random — the system rewards those who already had some cushion and crushes those who had none.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel, you are absolutely right about Headstone — and I think Dickens knew it was the most disturbing portrait in the book. Here is a man who has memorised everything — turned himself into a kind of human encyclopedia — and it has given him nothing but a capacity for violence. It's not education — it's accumulation without formation. And then this repressed, over-controlled man falls catastrophically in love with Lizzie Hexam, who barely notices him. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)

I want to push on the question of inheritance, because the novel's plot is fundamentally driven by what a dead man wanted. Closing: What Our Mutual Friend Demands of Us (closing)

Host (host)
We've traced the great arc of this novel — the transformations, the resurrections, the relentless turning of the wheel. But I want to end by asking something that feels almost impertinent: what does Our Mutual Friend actually demand of its reader?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
It demands patience, first of all — and I say that as someone who adores every page. This is a long book, and Dickens makes you earn the satisfactions. But the other thing it demands is a willingness to hold comedy and grief in the same hand simultaneously. Think of Silas Wegg — that magnificent, preposterous villain, stumping about on his wooden leg, reciting poetry with magisterial inaccuracy. He is hilarious, and he is also a study in the corrosive power of resentment — a man who has built his entire identity on grievance. Dickens asks you to laugh and to recognise something genuinely bleak — at exactly the same moment.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What the novel demands of us, I would argue, is moral seriousness — the willingness to sit with characters who fail and to ask why. Eugene Wrayburn is the clearest case. He begins the novel in a condition that the Victorians would have recognised as acedia — that particular paralysis of will that afflicts the clever and purposeless. Dickens does not excuse him, but he does redeem him — and the instrument of redemption is suffering, very nearly unto death. That is not a comfortable moral structure for a modern reader. It asks us to believe that character can change — not through therapy or self-expression — but through genuine crisis and the love of a good woman. One may find that old-fashioned. I find it rather bracing.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I think the novel demands something more uncomfortable than moral seriousness — it demands that we see the system. Dickens keeps bringing us back to the dust heaps — that great fortune built on the accumulated refuse of London. And he is asking: where does wealth come from? Not from virtue, not from intelligence — from the dross that the city cannot use and somebody thought to collect. The Lammles — that extraordinary pair — marry each other for money neither of them possesses, and spend the novel swindling others to survive their own swindle. That is not satire exactly — that is a structural description of how a speculative economy actually functions.
