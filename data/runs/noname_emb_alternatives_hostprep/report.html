Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Thank you — and I think it's worth pausing on why Collins places this meditation precisely where he does: at the hinge of catastrophe, when everything the family has depended upon has been stripped away. He is asking — with genuine philosophical seriousness — whether the social surface of a person is in fact the whole person. And his answer is a very old one. The passage asks — and I will read it, because Collins deserves to be heard in his own words — "Are there, infinitely varying with each individual, inbred forces of Good and Evil in all of us, deep down below the reach of mortal encouragement and mortal repression — hidden Good and hidden Evil, both alike at the mercy of the liberating opportunity and the sufficient temptation?" That phrase — 'the sufficient temptation' — is doing an enormous amount of work. Collins is not saying Magdalen is simply wicked — he is saying that the conditions which reveal what we already are have now arrived. Augustine would recognise this entirely.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I admire the elegance of that, and I admire Augustine — but I want to press on exactly the word you just used: 'conditions.' Because the conditions Collins is describing aren't metaphysical — they're legal. The Vanstone sisters lose their name, their inheritance, their social standing — not because of anything in their character, but because of a statute. And then Collins turns to Miss Garth to speculate about latent evil — and look at what she actually says about Magdalen. "Was the life of the younger doomed to be the battle-field of mortal conflict with the roused forces of Evil in herself?" 'Doomed.' The law has already rendered its verdict, and the novel's moral language endorses it. The 'hidden evil' that Collins invokes as metaphysics is, I would argue, a bourgeois alibi — it relocates into Magdalen's soul a dispossession that was performed by Parliament and enforced by lawyers.

They're not identical — but they're closer than Edmund would like. Oliver is right that the performance is honest — what Magdalen does with disguise and deception is not corruption, it's adaptation. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I find myself in the unusual position of agreeing with both of you partially and resisting you both entirely. Collins does know what he's doing — and he tells us so, at the very outset of the novel. He is not winking at Miss Garth. He is not secretly a Marxist. He is a novelist with a declared purpose. He writes — and this is from his own preface — "Here is one more book that depicts the struggle of a human creature, under those opposing influences of Good and Evil, which we have all felt, which we have all known." 'We have all felt, which we have all known' — Collins is making a universal claim, not a sociological one. The question of whether we excuse Magdalen because the law was unjust is beside the point to him. The point is whether she can be made pathetic — sympathetic even in her error — and he believes she can.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That's exactly the point at which the novel becomes most interesting — and most revealing. Collins declares a moral framework — Good and Evil, the soul's struggle — and then writes a character whose intelligence and tenacity the reader is compelled to admire. The framework and the fiction are in permanent contradiction. And I think what produces that contradiction is precisely the legal machinery — the inheritance law, the marriage settlement, the entail — all of which the novel depicts with forensic accuracy. The machinery generates the moral crisis. Collins calls it the soul's battle — but the battlefield was built by lawyers.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The passage has undeniable force. But I think both of you are, in rather different ways, missing its most important register. Collins is doing something quite old-fashioned here — almost Johnsonian, in fact. He is making a moral claim, not an economic one. The monarch Money is not capitalism in the abstract. He is pride. The Writing on the Wall is a warning addressed to individual souls — to the men who make the system — not to an impersonal mechanism. That, I think, is where Daniel and I will always part ways. He sees a machine. Collins sees men, and their choices, and their consciences.
