Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Better. Unambiguously better — and the novel knows it. Carlingford has had its drawing rooms organised, its Wednesday evenings, its social fabric held together by one woman's extraordinary will and intelligence. The alloy is real. Lucilla's vanity, her occasional ruthlessness, her management of the people around her — all real. But Oliphant's point — her great moral point — is that the alloy is what makes the gold possible in fallen human nature. To demand purity of motive from anyone who acts in the world is to demand inaction. And Oliphant refuses that bargain.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I agree Carlingford is better — I just want to ask, better for whom? The novel tracks what Lucilla achieves and what it costs her. It is far less interested in what it costs the people who don't have her talent or her position. That is not a flaw in Oliphant — it is a limit. A historically specific limit. She sees what her society allows her to see. And the legacy of Lucilla's world is that it trains us to ask the question in terms of individual genius rather than collective arrangement. That is Oliphant's genuine radicalism and her genuine limitation — often in the same sentence.
