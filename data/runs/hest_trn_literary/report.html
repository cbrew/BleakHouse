What strikes me first, every time I return to this novel, is how wide its tonal range is. James Blackstone (social_historian)
What Eleanor is identifying in the prose is, I think, inseparable from the social reality Oliphant is working with. That "world of latent possibilities" is exactly what the novel will spend the next four hundred pages systematically closing down. Hester is a young woman of intelligence and energy living in a society that has very precise ideas about what she may and may not do with those qualities. The morning freshness is real — but Oliphant knows, and the reader slowly comes to know, that it cannot last. By the time we reach the novel's crisis, that pastoral tenderness has curdled into something almost unbearable. A Woman of Business: Catherine's Paradox (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been circling the closed door of Heriot Row — that sense of Hester excluded, watching from outside. But the woman who holds that door shut is herself a fascinating paradox — Catherine Vernon, banker, matriarch, and deeply self-deceived lover. Let's look hard at Catherine — at what she sees, what she refuses to see, and what the novel does with that tension.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Catherine Vernon is one of the genuinely unusual figures in Victorian fiction — a woman who runs a bank, commands a community, and is answered with deference by men. And yet she inhabits that authority in a way that is structurally precarious from the outset. The bank is hers by succession, not by any formal legal entitlement that Victorian law would straightforwardly protect. So when she extends trust to Edward, she is, in historical terms, doing something women in her position actually did — delegating executive function to a male proxy. The tragedy is that the system she relies on — patriarchal succession dressed up as family loyalty — is precisely what destroys her.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about Catherine is how Oliphant uses irony as architecture — the novel is built on Catherine's misreadings. There's that wonderful moment when Catherine amuses herself thinking about the impossibility of Hester marrying Harry. The narrator gives us this line — "She to fall to the lot of Harry! This was so strangely funny, so paradoxical, so out of character, that it amused Catherine altogether beyond description, yet gave her a strange blow." That phrase — "yet gave her a strange blow" — is doing so much work. Catherine laughs, but something in her flinches — and Oliphant doesn't explain why. She leaves it for us to understand: Catherine's pride in Hester's spirit is inseparable from her possessiveness of it.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
What the Edward plot ultimately reveals is that Catherine's power was never as absolute as it appeared. She ran the bank with intelligence and genuine authority — but she ran it through a man, and that man had his own agenda. The legal and financial structure of Victorian England ensured that women like Catherine were always ultimately vulnerable in precisely the space they thought they owned. And Hester ends up carrying the moral weight of that structural failure — which is not incidental, it's the novel's central argument.
