Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Right — and it is a very funny joke, and that's precisely what makes it so lethal. What Dickens does in that passage is he borrows the language of inventory — he treats a human being with the same syntax he'd use for furniture, and he never drops the metaphor, he just extends it further and further. Listen to how Dickens introduces him: "Being first cousin to Lord Snigsworth, he was in frequent requisition, and at many houses might be said to represent the dining-table in its normal state." 'In frequent requisition' — that's commercial language, procurement language. Twemlow isn't invited to dinner; he's requisitioned, like a piece of equipment. And the sentence does that to him — the grammar performs the dehumanisation before we've even registered it consciously. That, to me, is money as narrator — it's infiltrated the sentence structure itself.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor's right about the syntax, and I want to add a layer of historical sharpness to it. 'In frequent requisition' isn't just metaphor — it describes the actual social economy of the Victorian dinner party with precision. Twemlow's value is his connection to Lord Snigsworth — he's a unit of aristocratic legitimacy that the Veneerings can rent by invitation. New money buys access to old title through the medium of dinner — it was quite literally how it worked. What's brilliant — and what Dickens understands that a sociologist might miss — is the cruelty buried in the comedy. The further Twemlow is pulled out, the nearer he gets to the sideboard — the more socially useful he is, the more marginalised he becomes. That is a precise diagnosis of how the Victorian class system worked — and it's lodged inside a joke about furniture.

What gets me every time is that we laugh — and we should feel slightly uncomfortable about that. Because who are we laughing with, exactly? James Blackstone (social_historian)
That is exactly my argument — and I hold it with some affection and some frustration. Dickens sees the machine with absolute clarity. He understands that the Veneerings' world is built on a fiction — that their 'newness' is purchased, that the table exists to launder social aspiration through aristocratic proximity. It's tender — genuinely tender — and also a little dangerous as ideology. Because it tells the poor that their poverty is a spiritual asset — which is a very convenient thing to tell people if you are not, yourself, poor.

Yes — and I think it's the most politically charged passage in the opening movement of the novel. I'd argue it's neither — or rather, both, but not in the way the question implies.
