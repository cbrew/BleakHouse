Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'll confess I don't quite wince — though Oliver seems to expect it of me. What I resist is the idea that comedy and moral seriousness are in competition here. Eliot is laughing at Lydgate, certainly. But she is laughing at him the way one laughs at a friend who is walking towards a cliff. The comedy is in the gap between his self-estimation and the reality Eliot can already see bearing down on him. She tells us plainly: 'character too is a process and an unfolding. The man was still in the making.' The novel we're about to read is precisely that unfolding — and the laughter is the price of admission to something much graver.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Both of those readings are true and they're also incomplete. What Eliot is cataloguing when she talks about Lydgate's 'spots of commonness' isn't simply pride — it is the specific texture of class affect. His prejudices about furniture, about women, about 'being known without his telling that he was better born than other country surgeons' — those aren't moral failings that happen to have a social dimension. They are the markers by which a particular class reproduces its sense of its own superiority. The comedy Oliver rightly identifies is Eliot showing us the machinery of that reproduction — and she is cold about it even when she is tender.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
'Scandalous' was perhaps too strong — though I'll stand by the spirit of it. What Eliot establishes in that extraordinary Prelude is that the condition of Dorothea's greatness is precisely that it cannot find an adequate object. She gives us the flame image, and I want us to sit with it for a moment. 'Her flame quickly burned up that light fuel; and, fed from within, soared after some illimitable satisfaction, some object which would never justify weariness, which would reconcile self-despair with the rapturous consciousness of life beyond self.' That is not a complaint about society's constraints. That is a description of a soul whose nature is to exceed any available container. The drama is in the soul itself — Middlemarch is merely the arena where it becomes legible.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I think that's a beautiful reading that happens to be ideologically convenient. If the soul simply exceeds any container, then no particular container is to blame — which neatly removes the provincial bourgeoisie from indictment. The passage is doing something almost shocking if you slow down to read it carefully. Eliot tells us that her plain dress gives her 'the impressiveness of a fine quotation from the Bible — in a paragraph of today's newspaper.' That simile is not an admiring one. It is a description of how distinction functions in a marketplace of social signs. The Bible quotation stands out because everything around it is dross — and Dorothea's spiritual beauty works precisely the same way in the provincial context. Her asceticism is legible as distinction precisely because the other women around her are busy with gimp and artificial protrusions of drapery. It is spiritual capital operating as social capital — and Eliot sees both simultaneously.
