The Vanstone Sisters: A Family Shattered (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to No Name Unpacked — a close, unhurried look at one of the most audacious novels of the Victorian era. No Name, published by Wilkie Collins in 1862, is a novel about two sisters who lose everything — not to death or disaster, but to a legal technicality. Their parents were not married when they were born, and in the eyes of Victorian law, that made Magdalen and Norah Vanstone nobodies — no name, no inheritance, no standing. What Collins does with that premise is extraordinary — one sister accepts her fate, the other declares war on it. To explore this novel in all its richness, I'm joined today by three exceptional readers, each bringing a quite different lens to the text. Dr. Eleanor Hartley is a novelist and creative writing teacher whose obsession is the architecture of fiction — how great writers build their effects sentence by sentence. She's going to show us how Collins earns every twist. Professor James Blackstone is a legal and social historian who knows exactly what words like 'illegitimacy' and 'intestacy' meant in practice in 1862 — and what they still mean today. He brings the world the novel was written into, and written against. And Ms. Caroline Woodcourt has been reading this novel since she was a teenager and hasn't quite stopped since. She reads with her whole heart — and she has very strong opinions about which characters deserve better. Let's begin at the beginning — the shattering of the Vanstone family, and what it does to the two sisters at the centre of this novel.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I return to those opening chapters is how thoroughly Collins makes you love the Vanstone family before he destroys it. Combe-Raven is this warm, ordered, sunlit world — horses, music, domestic happiness. And Magdalen especially — she just leaps off the page from her very first scene. Collins describes her as possessing a natural grace that no artifice could have taught her — there's this sense of a personality that is simply too large for its circumstances. And then the catastrophe comes in two waves — the parents' deaths, and then the revelation about their marriage. By the time the lawyer Pendril arrives to explain what has happened, you feel the ground drop away. Not because it's melodramatic — Collins is almost brutally quiet about it — but because you already care so completely.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That quietness is everything, isn't it — and it's such a deliberate craft choice. Collins structures the first act almost like a comedy — the amateur theatricals, Magdalen's performance, the lively household. He's building a genre expectation: domestic novel, romance, perhaps a heroine who makes a good match. And then he pulls it apart — with a carriage accident and a clause in the law of marriage. What's brilliant is that the theatrical scenes aren't just atmosphere — they're character revelation. Magdalen's ability to inhabit a role completely, to become someone else, is established there as a talent. It becomes her weapon for the rest of the novel. Collins is doing this thing where the backstory isn't backstory — every scene in that first section is loading the gun for what follows.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the gun, as it were, is the Marriage Act — the legal mechanism that makes the whole catastrophe possible. Andrew Vanstone, we learn, had been previously married — a disastrous first marriage he could not legally dissolve. His second marriage to the girls' mother, the one they all believed to be valid, was void from the start. Under English law in 1862, the children of such a union had no legal standing whatsoever — they were, in the precise legal term, filius nullius: the child of nobody. And here's where Collins gets the law exactly right, which is unusual for a novelist of the period. Andrew Vanstone, in his hurry to legitimize his union, made a will — but it was drawn up before the retrospective marriage ceremony and then he died before he could update it. The estate therefore passes intestate to his nearest male heir — his monstrous brother Michael. The cruelty isn't accidental or conspiratorial — it's structural. The law doesn't need a villain to grind people up.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That line of James's — the law doesn't need a villain — gets at something I find so moving about Norah's response to it. Norah accepts the verdict. Not passively — she's not a passive character — but she looks at the situation and concludes that fighting it would require her to become something she doesn't want to be. Collins describes her grief as having a certain dignity — a composure that comes not from coldness but from a kind of moral decision. And the contrast with Magdalen is heartbreaking in the best way. Magdalen's grief cracks open into fury — and out of that fury comes the entire plot of the novel.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And what Collins is doing structurally there is giving us two equally valid responses to injustice, without adjudicating between them. That's extraordinarily sophisticated for 1862 — or for any era, frankly. The novel never quite says Magdalen is wrong to fight, nor does it say Norah is passive for accepting. Miss Garth, their governess, is the character who most explicitly mourns the loss of the old Magdalen — the girl before the catastrophe. But even she can't quite condemn what Magdalen becomes, because the reader understands completely why she becomes it. Collins plants us so deep inside Magdalen's perspective that her logic becomes our logic — that's the novel's great seduction.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to come back to the figure of Michael Vanstone for a moment — because he's doing important work in the novel beyond being the obstacle. He represents a particular Victorian type: the man who hides behind legality as a substitute for morality. He isn't legally obliged to recognise his brother's daughters — and that fact is, in his view, entirely sufficient. The moral question of what his brother clearly intended for those girls simply doesn't arise for him. Collins is skewering something real about how Victorian respectability worked — the law as moral alibi.
Host (host)
The law as moral alibi — that phrase is going to stay with me. And it brings us neatly to where we're heading next — because the legal structures that shatter the Vanstone family weren't incidental to Victorian society, they were load-bearing. In our next segment, we're going to look hard at what illegitimacy actually meant in 1862 — and at what it means that Collins chose to put two women at the centre of a story about fighting a system designed to erase them.

Bastards, Heiresses, and the Law: Social Structures on Trial (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been talking about the Vanstone family and the catastrophic rupture at the novel's heart. Now I want to push into the machinery behind that rupture — the law, the name, the social logic that turns two daughters into legal nonentities overnight.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The situation Collins dramatises is quite precisely calibrated to the law as it stood in the 1840s. Andrew Vanstone married his first wife in Canada, and that marriage was never formally dissolved — or at least, the dissolution was never properly registered. His subsequent marriage to the mother of Norah and Magdalen was therefore void under English law. The daughters are legally illegitimate — and in the 1840s, illegitimacy carried specific, brutal consequences. They cannot inherit intestate. They have, in the cold phrase of the law, no name and no kin. Collins didn't choose his title carelessly.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And what strikes me as a novelist is how Collins makes the legal machinery almost a character in itself. The title announces the theme structurally before the story even begins — No Name — and then the plot proceeds to dramatise exactly what that absence means. There's a scene early in the novel — the reading of the will, or rather the crushing moment when the solicitor explains there is no valid will that can protect them. The money passes to Michael Vanstone, Andrew's legitimate estranged brother — a man the girls have never met. Collins is staging the cruelty of a system that rewards legal status over lived relationship, over love, over moral claim. The law doesn't care that these women were raised as daughters. It cares only about the paperwork.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What I find so devastating as a reader is the speed of it. One moment Magdalen and Norah are comfortable young women with prospects, with a name, with a place in the world. Then their father dies, and their mother dies within hours, and the law descends on them like a trap. Collins has Norah's reaction be one of grief and quiet resignation — she accepts it, or tries to. But Magdalen's reaction is fury. And her fury is completely rational. She is not raging against bad luck. She is raging against a system that has decided she doesn't exist.
Host (host)
James, can you give us a sense of how unusual or how common this situation actually was in Victorian England?
James Blackstone (social_historian)
It was more common than respectable Victorian society wished to acknowledge. The complications of colonial marriages — marriages contracted abroad under different legal systems — created genuine uncertainty about validity under English law. And there was a class of case — the bigamy case, the invalid first marriage — that periodically surfaced to ruin families. What Collins does with characteristic precision is use a situation where there is no villain in the ordinary sense — Andrew Vanstone may have been negligent, but he is not malevolent. The cruelty is structural. The law itself is the antagonist. And that is, if anything, more disturbing than a wicked individual — because you can't appeal to a system's conscience.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That point about the absence of a conventional villain is crucial to what Collins is doing architecturally. Think about how he then provides two figures who fill the villain-shaped hole — Michael Vanstone first, and then his son Noel. Michael Vanstone refuses to make any provision for the girls. He has the law absolutely on his side, and he knows it. He is the law's human face — correct, cold, impregnable. When he dies — and Collins has him die before Magdalen can reach him, which is a gorgeous piece of plotting — the problem doesn't die with him. It transfers to Noel, who is feebler but in some ways harder to manipulate because he's surrounded by that extraordinary creation, Mrs. Lecount.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Mrs. Lecount is one of the great supporting characters in Victorian fiction, I'll fight anyone on that. She's Noel Vanstone's housekeeper — this Swiss widow with glacial intelligence and absolute loyalty to her employer's interests. What makes her fascinating is that she's not wrong about Magdalen. She sees through her almost immediately. Collins gives us this wonderful antagonism — Magdalen's brilliance of deception against Lecount's brilliance of detection. And there's something darkly funny about the fact that two women are effectively fighting over a weak, vain little man, neither of whom actually wants him for himself. One wants his name on a marriage certificate. The other wants to preserve her position in his household.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that observation about the marriage certificate is precisely where Collins is sharpest about the law's relationship to women. The only legal instrument available to Magdalen is marriage itself. She cannot sue. She cannot inherit directly. She cannot enter a contract in her own right. Her entire strategy — this audacious, elaborate strategy she constructs with Captain Wragge — is aimed at using the one mechanism the law offers a woman: becoming a wife. And the bitter irony Collins builds into the novel is that marriage under the coverture laws of the time would erase Magdalen's legal identity just as surely as illegitimacy already had. She is trying to use a trap to escape a trap.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That's the sentence that unlocks the whole novel for me — she's trying to use a trap to escape a trap. And Collins signals this right from the moment Magdalen forms her plan. There's that remarkable scene where she announces her intention to Captain Wragge — this cheerful professional fraud who becomes her unlikely accomplice. Wragge is delighted. He sees an interesting game and a potentially profitable one. But Magdalen is not playing a game. She is deadly serious, and that discrepancy in register is one of Collins's most effective tonal devices throughout the book. The comedy and the tragedy live right next to each other, and neither one cancels the other out.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
I want to go back to the name, because I think we haven't quite exhausted what Collins is doing with it. Magdalen's entire theatrical project — she disguises herself, takes on aliases, performs different identities — is this extended meditation on what a name is and what it does. She had a name — Vanstone — that was taken from her by the law. So she becomes someone who multiplies names, who collects them, who treats identity itself as a costume. There's something both powerful and heartbreaking in that — the girl who has no name learns to wear all names. But Collins never lets us forget that underneath all the costumes, she wants the one specific name that was hers by right.
Host (host)
That image of wearing all names as a costume leads us, I think, very naturally into the territory we want to explore next. Because deception in this novel isn't just a plot mechanism — it seems to be where Magdalen's whole character lives.

Magdalen's Mask: Deception as Character (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been talking about how the law strips Magdalen and Norah of their identity — their very names, in a sense. Which makes it all the more striking that Magdalen's response is to multiply her identities — to become, deliberately, someone else entirely. This segment is about deception — about what it means that Collins makes his heroine a consummate actress and con artist. Eleanor, I want to start with you, because the theatrical dimension here feels like a craft obsession for Collins as much as a plot device.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It absolutely is — and what's so thrilling is that Collins never lets the acting be merely costume. Magdalen doesn't just put on a disguise; she inhabits a character so completely that the reader starts to wonder which version of her is the real one. Think about the private theatricals early in the novel — before any of the catastrophe has happened. Collins introduces Magdalen's talent almost casually, as a drawing-room accomplishment. But the structural implication is enormous: he is planting the instrument she will later use for survival. The girl who plays a role for entertainment becomes the woman who plays a role for her life.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And what I find almost unbearable, reading it, is that she's so good at it. There's a moment when she transforms herself into Miss Garth's manner and voice, and it's played partly for comedy — but there's something vertiginous underneath. Because you realize this young woman can erase herself at will. Collins has a line about her performance — he says something to the effect that she had the rare and enviable gift of telling truth by means of fiction. And that phrase just — it stops you. Because that is precisely what a novelist does.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and I think Collins is quite deliberately collapsing the distance between his heroine and himself. The novelist and the con artist share a toolkit: invention, sustained performance, the management of other people's credulity. When Magdalen studies a character she intends to impersonate, Collins describes the process with the vocabulary of authorship — she observes, selects, constructs. She doesn't simply mimic; she writes a person.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to ground this slightly, because I think the historical stakes of her deception are easy to underestimate. A woman assuming a false identity in the 1840s and 50s — the period the novel is set — is not merely committing a social impropriety. Obtaining money or property by false pretences was a criminal offence. When Magdalen marries Noel Vanstone under a false name, she is not simply playing a role — she is, by the strict letter of the law, potentially voiding the marriage and exposing herself to prosecution. Collins knew this, and he uses the legal jeopardy to tighten the dramatic tension without ever quite spelling it out. It's a very precise kind of craftiness — on Collins's part as much as Magdalen's.
Host (host)
The marriage to Noel Vanstone is extraordinary to read — it's one of the most cold-blooded courtships in Victorian fiction. Caroline, you've written about what it costs Magdalen psychologically to sustain that performance.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
It costs her everything, and Collins doesn't flinch from that. There are passages in the later 'Scenes' where we see Magdalen alone — after a day of performing sweetness and compliance for Noel Vanstone — and she is barely holding herself together. Collins gives her these moments of private self-reckoning that feel almost confessional. She asks herself, in effect, whether the woman doing these things is still Magdalen Vanstone — or whether she has performed herself out of existence. That is a genuinely modern question — about identity, about the self under sustained social pressure — dressed in Victorian clothes.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And there's a letter she writes to her sister Norah — I believe it falls somewhere in the third or fourth 'Scene' — that reads almost like a will. She is accounting for herself, settling her moral debts, as though she doesn't expect to survive what she's about to do. For a social historian, that document is fascinating — it shows a woman who has entirely internalized the moral framework that condemns her, even as she defies it. She knows what she is doing is wrong by the standards of her world. She does it anyway.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Which is what makes her so unusual among Victorian heroines — and so dangerous, I think, to some of Collins's original readers. Most fallen or transgressive women in the period are either punished conclusively or repent so thoroughly that the transgression is cancelled. Magdalen does neither, cleanly. She collapses, she suffers — but she is not destroyed, and her recovery is almost unsettlingly matter-of-fact. Collins gives her a good marriage at the end, and I've always felt that this is less a concession to convention than a quietly radical act — he refuses to punish her. He just — lets her live.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And the name — the title, No Name. The whole novel is haunted by that irony: the woman with no legal name becomes a woman of many names. She is Miss Garth, she is Mrs. Noel Vanstone, she is the parlour maid — each name a mask, each mask a kind of power. But underneath all of them is this furious, grieving, brilliant girl who simply will not accept what the law has decided she is.
Host (host)
And she doesn't operate alone in all of this — which brings us, naturally, to the man who is in many ways her mirror and her enabler. Captain Wragge: swindler, self-mythologist, and one of the most purely enjoyable characters in Victorian fiction.

Wragge's Comedy of Cons (discussion)

Host (host)
We've been talking about Magdalen's masks — the extraordinary discipline it takes for her to sustain a false identity over months. But Magdalen doesn't arrive at that discipline alone — she has a teacher, and a very peculiar one at that. Captain Wragge — con man, genealogist, self-described moral agriculturalist — is one of the strangest comic creations in Victorian fiction. So let's talk about what Wragge actually is in this novel, and why Collins gave him so much of the best material.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Wragge is one of those characters who seems to have arrived from a different, brighter novel and simply refused to leave. He's a rogue, genuinely, and Collins never soft-pedals that — he scrounges, he swindles, he exploits Magdalen's desperation. And yet Collins gives him this extraordinary forensic self-awareness, this almost philosophical relationship to his own dishonesty. He classifies himself — he has a taxonomy of his own moral failures. He describes his career as one of 'moral agriculture' — and there is something genuinely delightful about the precision of that euphemism. He's not a criminal in his own eyes — he's a farmer of opportunity, tilling whatever social soil presents itself. That self-mythologising is what makes him so funny and so unsettling in equal measure.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me historically is how precisely Collins has drawn a recognisable social type. The mid-Victorian period was full of men like Wragge — educated enough to understand the machinery of respectability, but without the capital to sustain it honestly. The fake genealogy business is particularly sharp satire — there was a thriving trade in exactly that. Newly wealthy families wanted heraldic legitimacy, and men like Wragge were perfectly positioned to supply it — for a fee. Collins is making a pointed argument: the respectable classes were themselves complicit in the frauds being practiced upon them. They wanted the fiction of ancient lineage badly enough to pay for it — and Wragge simply meets that demand. The irony being, of course, that Magdalen's entirely legitimate grievance — her actual disinheritance — gets no such market.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Can I come back to the comedy for a moment, because I think we risk making Wragge sound merely interesting when he's actually genuinely hilarious? His letters — the letters he writes to Mrs. Wragge — have this deadpan authority that never breaks, no matter how absurd the situation. He instructs her on how to dress, what to say, what expressions to wear — as though she were a puppet he's rehearsing for a play. And Mrs. Wragge's responses — or rather her complete failure to follow any of his instructions — are some of the funniest moments in the book. She puts the wrong cap on, she forgets her cue, she wanders into scenes she wasn't supposed to enter. And yet she's also — I find her genuinely touching. She's the one person in this entire ensemble who has absolutely no guile — who cannot deceive if she tried. In a novel saturated with performance, she's the only authentic thing — and that's both funny and rather sad.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That's beautifully put — and it points to something important about how Collins uses the Wragges structurally. He places them in a comedic subplot that runs parallel to Magdalen's increasingly desperate main plot. When you're with the Wragges, there's a lightness — you can breathe. And then Collins cuts back to Magdalen's actual situation and the contrast is almost vertiginous. It's the same tonal control Dickens uses — but Collins is more disciplined about it, more surgical. Wragge's comedy never tips over into farce because it's always anchored to something real — a genuine insight into how deception actually functions in society.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And there's a real intelligence in his tuition of Magdalen — the scenes where he trains her are remarkable. He doesn't teach her to lie in some crude sense — he teaches her to inhabit a persona completely, to think from inside a false identity. Which is, incidentally, what actors learn — and what certain legal professionals learn too, though we phrase it more decorously. He tells her at one point that discovery comes not from saying the wrong thing, but from a single unguarded moment of feeling — of being yourself. That's quite a sophisticated theory of performance — and it happens to be exactly right. The danger for Magdalen isn't incompetence — it's authenticity breaking through at the wrong moment.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And that's what makes their partnership so peculiar and so moving, actually. Wragge genuinely admires Magdalen — you can feel it in the way Collins writes his observations of her. He describes her talent for performance with what I can only call professional respect — she's a better natural actor than anyone he's ever trained. There's a line where he notes, with something like wonder, that she can cry real tears on command — not theatrical weeping, but actual grief, summoned and deployed. And the reader feels that wonder too — because we've been watching Magdalen do exactly that across the whole novel. She isn't pretending to feel — she feels, and then she weaponises it.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That distinction is crucial — and it's what separates Magdalen from a conventional villainess. A villainess doesn't feel — she performs feeling as a weapon and nothing else. Magdalen's tragedy — and it is a tragedy of sorts — is that she genuinely grieves what she's lost, while simultaneously using that grief as a tool. Wragge sees this clearly and it makes him an unexpectedly perceptive reader of her character. He's the only character in the novel who fully understands what she's doing and doesn't judge her for it. That's not nothing — in a novel where almost everyone is passing judgment on Magdalen, Wragge's amoral acceptance is almost a form of kindness.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Though I'd add one note of historical caution to that warmth. Wragge does leave, ultimately — and on his own terms, once the scheme becomes legally risky for him. His loyalty is real but it has a price — and the price is always his own safety first. Collins is careful not to fully rehabilitate him — which is the right instinct, because a rehabilitated Wragge would be a lesser character. He ends the novel prosperous, married to a reformed Mrs. Wragge, running what I believe is described as a patent medicine business. Which is, frankly, a perfect final joke — the confidence man finds his metier in the most respectable of disreputable Victorian trades.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
The patent medicine ending is perfect — Collins couldn't resist it. And it has this bittersweet quality because by the time Wragge exits, we've genuinely come to like him. Not despite his roguery but through it — he's honest about his dishonesty in a way most of the novel's 'respectable' characters never are. There's something almost Falstaffian about him — the likeable scoundrel who tells you truths that the virtuous characters cannot. He sees the comedy in the world, and he invites us to see it with him.
Host (host)
The confidence man who tells the truth — that's a thread worth pulling on. Wragge's comedy has done something important for this novel — it's shown us the machinery of property and inheritance from the outside, from the angle of someone who exploits its fictions. Which brings us, naturally, to the question of what that machinery actually costs — and who it destroys. Coming up: Noel Vanstone, his father's will, and the extraordinary legal trap that Magdalen walks into with her eyes open.

Noel's Inheritance: Plot, Property, and Powerlessness (close_reading)

Host (host)
We've been laughing — and wincing — at Captain Wragge's elaborate performances of respectability. But behind all that comedy lies a very serious question: what exactly is Magdalen trying to win back, and at what cost? That brings us to the heart of this segment — Noel Vanstone, the inheritance, and the peculiar powerlessness at the centre of Collins's plot.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Collins does with Noel Vanstone is genuinely audacious, structurally speaking. He gives us the nominal antagonist — the man who holds Magdalen's rightful fortune — and makes him almost pitiably small. Noel is vain, hypochondriacal, desperately susceptible to flattery — he's essentially a locked box with no key of his own. And Collins is quite explicit about this — the real power behind Noel sits entirely with Mrs Lecount, his housekeeper. So the target of Magdalen's scheme is, in a sense, not Noel at all — it's Lecount. Collins splits the antagonism across two figures, which is a far richer construction than a single villain would be.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And the legal architecture underpinning all of this is quite precisely observed by Collins. Andrew Vanstone's fortune was his to dispose of entirely as he wished — and when he dies intestate, or rather without a proper will acknowledging the girls, English law simply cuts them out. Magdalen and Norah are not legally his daughters because their parents' marriage was retrospectively discovered to be invalid. That's not Collins inventing a gothic peculiarity — that was a genuine and brutal feature of English law at the time. Illegitimacy carried consequences that were total and irrevocable: disinheritance, social stigma, no recourse whatsoever. What Collins understood — and what makes the novel so pointed — is that the law wasn't merely indifferent to Magdalen's suffering; it was the instrument of it. She has no name in the eyes of the law, quite literally.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That phrase — 'no name' — is where the novel's title does its real work, isn't it. And it haunts Magdalen from the very first revelation of her parents' situation. But what I keep returning to — what I find almost unbearably moving — is the specific texture of her powerlessness. Collins gives us this passage where she contemplates what she's reduced to — and the word he uses is 'nobody.' 'I am Nobody's child. The law gives me no name and the law gives me no right.' Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That refusal is, structurally, the entire engine of the novel. Collins sets up an almost impossible narrative problem: how do you give your protagonist agency when the law and society have conspired to deny her every legitimate claim? His answer is — you make her a performer. You make her willing to become anyone, to wear any face that serves the purpose. And crucially, Collins doesn't sanitise what that costs her. There's a moment — I think it's one of the most honest lines in the book — where she says something to the effect that in disguising herself so completely, she has lost track of who she actually is. That's Collins being psychologically acute in a way that anticipates far later fiction.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to stay with Noel for a moment, because I think he's historically interesting beyond his function as an obstacle. He inherited everything from his father Michael Vanstone — who was himself a figure of some venality, a man who chose to take the money rather than acknowledge any moral obligation to the girls. Noel simply continues that refusal. He wraps himself in legality and calls it principle. Collins is quite unsparing here — Noel's meanness with money is a kind of moral signature. There's that beautifully dry moment when we're told that Noel's idea of generosity is to be grateful without inconveniencing himself financially in any way. Collins uses financial pettiness as a window into moral bankruptcy. It's one of his most economical satirical strokes.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And Mrs Lecount sees through Magdalen in a way that Noel simply cannot — partly because Lecount is paying attention, and partly because she has her own reasons to guard what she's built. What I find fascinating is that Lecount is, in her own way, also a woman without security. She's a dependent too — her position, her livelihood, everything she has is tied to Noel's survival and favour. Collins sets up a kind of mirroring between the two women that's never made explicit but is there on every page they share. Both are intelligent, both are resourceful, both are operating within systems that deny them real power. The difference is that Lecount has chosen to work within the system, and Magdalen has chosen — magnificently, terrifyingly — to attack it.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That opposition is also a formal one — Collins structures many of their scenes as a kind of chess match. Each move Magdalen makes is anticipated, countered, or deflected by Lecount — and vice versa. It creates this extraordinary narrative tension where you're rooting for Magdalen while simultaneously dreading what victory will require of her. Collins understood something very important: the most gripping plots are the ones where the reader cannot be entirely comfortable with the protagonist's methods. We want her to win.
