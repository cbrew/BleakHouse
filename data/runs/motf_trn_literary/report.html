James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me first, coming to this novel as a historian, is how precisely Eliot understands the mechanics of ordinary ruin. She is not interested in melodrama — in scheming villains and spectacular crimes. She's interested in the far more common and far more dangerous engine of destruction: thoughtlessness. There's a passage early in the novel where the narrator makes this explicit, and it is one of the most scalpel-sharp pieces of moral writing I know. "It is easy enough to spoil the lives of our neighbours without taking so much trouble; we can do it by lazy acquiescence and lazy omission, by trivial falsities for which we hardly know a reason, by small frauds neutralised by small extravagances, by maladroit flatteries, and clumsily improvised insinuations." Every word in that catalogue earns its place. "Maladroit flatteries" — not calculated flattery, maladroit flattery, the kind we offer because it's easier than honesty. Eliot is indicting a whole social texture, not individual bad actors.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And crucially, that devastation has a specific social cause that Eliot never lets us forget. Mr Tulliver, when under the influence of a strong feeling, had a promptitude in action that may seem inconsistent with that painful sense of the complicated, puzzling nature of human affairs under which his more dispassionate deliberations were conducted; but it is really not improbable that there was a direct relation between these apparently contradictory phenomena, since I have observed that for getting a strong impression that a skein is tangled there is nothing like snatching hastily at a single thread. It was owing to this promptitude that Mr Tulliver was on horseback soon after dinner the next day (he was not dyspeptic) on his way to Basset to see his sister Moss and her husband. For having made up his mind irrevocably that he would pay Mrs Glegg her loan of five hundred pounds, it naturally occurred to him that he had a promissory note for three hundred pounds lent to his brother-in-law Moss; and if the said brother-in-law could manage to pay in the money within a given time, it would go far to lessen the fallacious air of inconvenience which Mr Tulliver’s spirited step might have worn in the eyes of weak people who require to know precisely how a thing is to be done before they are strongly confident that it will be easy.

What undoes me every time — every single re-reading — is the moment when Tulliver turns his horse back. He's just made this grand, impulsive resolution to call in Moss's debt and restore his honour. And then he stops. In the lane. And turns his head from side to side in a melancholy way. And what comes out of him — this big, blundering, proud man — is just this. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And then the novel shows us exactly what that exposure means — through Tom. The passage about Tom's mind is one of the most controlled pieces of diagnostic prose in the book. Eliot is building a taxonomy of a certain kind of moral character — not wicked, but dangerously narrow. She writes — and this is worth taking slowly — "Let a prejudice be bequeathed, carried in the air, adopted by hearsay, caught in through the eye,—however it may come, these minds will give it a habitation." "It is something to assert strongly and bravely, something to fill up the void of spontaneous ideas, something to impose on others with the authority of conscious right."
