Steering notes

Push hard for the passages to be read aloud — Trevelyan especially will want to perform the fog sequence and the Megalosaurus line, and hearing the prose will ground the argument. Trevelyan is the bridge — he believes in both the comedy and the critique — so use him to keep the conversation from becoming binary. If things get too abstract, pull it back to a specific word: *defiled*, *blighted*, *waddling*. Make them argue about the word choice.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The joke is the serious work — that's precisely the point, and Dickens knew it from the first sentence. When you stand at a lectern and you read that opening aloud, something extraordinary happens. The prose is completely deadpan — and I mean clinically, mercilessly deadpan. Listen to how it sets up the line. "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." That word — wonderful — is everything. Not terrifying, not impossible — wonderful. Dickens is telling you: this world has already slipped back into primordial chaos, and we're all too muddy and bad-tempered to even notice. The comedy and the horror are the same gesture — and that's the whole novel in a sentence.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'll grant the comic genius — one would be a fool not to. But I think Oliver's framing rather flatters our theoretical instincts at the expense of the simpler and deeper truth. Dickens opens with chaos — primordial, pre-legal chaos — because he wants to locate his Court of Chancery inside a moral condition, not merely a political one. The fog comes before the institutions. What Dickens gives us is a condition of consciousness — the experience of not being able to see clearly — and then he shows us what men have built inside that darkness.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, that's an elegant formulation, and I think it's precisely backwards. The fog doesn't precede the institutions — the institutions produce the fog. Dickens is not describing a timeless condition of human blindness. He's mapping something very precise — look at the specific route the fog travels. "Fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." Defiled — that word is doing enormous work. The fog is not some timeless miasma — it is specifically contaminated by contact with commercial infrastructure. Shipping, tiers, waterside pollutions — Dickens is naming the nodes of a system.

Edmund, I admire the argument — and I don't think it's entirely wrong — but it asks us to split the experience from what produces it. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Dickens unquestionably believes in the individual — that is what separates him from a pamphleteer. The fog is the world's condition — but a character's soul is not the world. There will be characters in this novel who maintain clarity of purpose, who give themselves to others without calculation — and Dickens presents that as genuinely possible, genuinely redemptive.
