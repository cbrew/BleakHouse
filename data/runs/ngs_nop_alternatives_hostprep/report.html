The New Grub Street: Ambition, Poverty, and the Literary Marketplace (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to New Grub Street Unpacked, a close reading of George Gissing's extraordinary 1891 novel about writing, money, and what the literary marketplace does to the people inside it. Gissing wrote this book in extraordinary circumstances — impoverished, obscure, furiously productive — and he made a novel that still feels like an open wound. I'm joined today by three people who've been arguing about it, in some cases for decades, and whose arguments I promise are going to collide rather spectacularly. Oliver Trevelyan has narrated more classic novels for audio than almost anyone alive, and he comes to Gissing as a performer — hearing the rhythms, the comic timing, the grotesque music of the prose. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don who reads novels as stories about individual character tested by circumstance — and has very little patience for what he calls sociological mood lighting. And Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads every institution in a novel — including the publishing industry — as a mechanism for protecting the powerful and exhausting everyone else. Oliver, I want to start with you, because something you said when we spoke has been rattling around in my head ever since. You called New Grub Street a comedy. Not a tragedy — a comedy. Look at Jasper Milvain in the very first pages of the novel — he sits at breakfast and announces, cheerfully, that he intends to succeed by writing whatever the market wants, morality be damned. Gissing gives him such perfect, breezy self-awareness that it tips over into something genuinely comic — this man has looked into the abyss and found it rather convenient. And then there's Whelpdale — oh, Whelpdale is pure Dickensian grotesque — failing upward through sheer undentable optimism, pitching magazines with titles like Chit-Chat to an audience who want everything in snippets. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
It's a fair challenge and I won't sidestep it. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)

That is a devastating self-indictment, and Gissing means it seriously. What distinguishes Reardon from, say, a Milvain — who is also without literary conscience — is that Reardon knows. He is always watching himself fail, recording the failure with the precision of the very intelligence that can't generate the work. For me that self-knowledge is not exculpatory — it is the tragedy.
