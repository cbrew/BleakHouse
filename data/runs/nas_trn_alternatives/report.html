Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd start with Margaret Hale — because she arrives in Milton as an outsider, and that outsider status lets Gaskell anatomise the place with extraordinary precision. And what Margaret sees, almost immediately, is a society that produces great men from poverty — but then asks nothing of those men in return. There's a passage early in chapter eleven that stops me every time I read it. Margaret is reflecting on her first real meeting with Thornton, and she separates the man into two halves — the boastful industrialist and the honest former shop-boy. She says: "Everything else revolted me, from its hardness; but he spoke about himself so simply — with so little of the pretence that makes the vulgarity of shop-people, and with such tender respect for his mother." That word 'revolted' is doing serious work. Thornton's pride in Milton, his contempt for the improvident worker — Margaret calls it hardness. And then she asks the question that the whole novel circles back to: what duty does a man who clawed his way up owe to those still at the bottom?

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think Daniel is right that the passage is striking — though I would weight its significance rather differently. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I respect that reading — and Gaskell certainly frames it in those terms. But I'd argue the moral framing is itself ideological — it places the burden on individual conscience and lets the structure walk free. And then there's Nicholas Higgins — who has no patience at all for the language of conscience and duty when it comes from above. Higgins is, I think, the novel's most honest voice — because he refuses abstraction. He tells Margaret directly: "I believe what I see, and no more. That's what I believe, young woman. I don't believe all I hear — no! not by a big deal." That is not the voice of a man who can be pacified by a master's good intentions. He is empirical, suspicious, and — crucially — tender about his daughter in the very same breath.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And yet those affections are tested — violently — by the conditions the system creates. The riot scene in chapter twenty-two is where everything crystallises. Boucher — one of the most desperate workers, a man ground down past the point of reason — is in that crowd. And what stops him — what stops the whole mob — is not Thornton's authority. It is the sight of Margaret's face — wounded, still, weeping. "That pale, upturned face, with closed eyes, still and sad as marble, though the tears welled out of the long entanglement of eyelashes, and dropped down; and, heavier, slower plash than even tears, came the drip of blood from her wound." 'The long entanglement of eyelashes' — that is not social critique, perhaps. But the fact that it is a woman's body, bleeding, that disarms the violence of hungry men — that tells you something about who bears the cost of this conflict.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right that the comedy is carefully managed, but I think what Gaskell is establishing here goes rather deeper than social observation. Margaret is performing a duty — her head aches, she is in a strange city she rather dislikes, and yet she resolves to throw herself into the breach, as the narrator puts it, rather than allow her father's guest to feel neglected. That is a moral portrait, and a quite precise one. And then in the same chapter, she turns to look at Thornton properly — as if for the first time — and Gaskell gives us a description of extraordinary physical attentiveness. She writes of Thornton that "the straight brows fell low over the clear, deep-set, earnest eyes, which without being unpleasantly sharp, seemed intent enough to penetrate into the very heart and core of what he was looking at." That phrase — to penetrate into the very heart and core — is not merely a physical description. It is a characterological one — it tells us what kind of man Thornton is, and what kind of moral attention he is capable of.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't want to quarrel with either of those readings, but I think we need to hold two things in mind at once here. When Margaret looks at Thornton and sees in his face the lines of a man ready to do and dare everything — the novel's exact phrase — she is not simply admiring a striking appearance. She is encountering for the first time the human face of industrial capital — a man whose entire physiognomy has been shaped by the will to command and compete. And what is remarkable is that she is drawn to it — she likes the smile, the novel says — even before she has the vocabulary to understand what produced it. Margaret's education in this novel is the slow acquisition of precisely that vocabulary.

Margaret had remained in the same state; white, motionless, speechless, tearless. They had told her that her aunt Shaw was coming; but she had not expressed either surprise or pleasure, or dislike to the idea. Mr. Bell, whose appetite had returned, and who appreciated Dixon’s endeavours to gratify it, in vain urged upon her to taste some sweetbreads stewed with oysters; she shook her head with the same quiet obstinacy as on the previous day; and he was obliged to console himself for her rejection, by eating them all himself. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I won't quarrel with that reading of the sentence — it's beautiful. What I'd add is that even here, in the most intimate corner of the novel, the social world hasn't disappeared. Mrs. Shaw comes on a railway — the very instrument of the industrial transformation the novel has been anatomising. The comfort of family, when it finally arrives, arrives by train. That detail, in a novel this carefully constructed, is not accidental. Gaskell is showing us that the personal and the political do not exist in separate rooms. They share the same walls.
