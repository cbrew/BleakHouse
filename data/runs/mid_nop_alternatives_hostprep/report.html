Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right about the syntactic cunning, and I'd add something to it. The Prelude is also a piece of moral framing — Eliot is establishing what kind of heroism she intends to examine. Saint Theresa found her epic life — Eliot's phrase is 'a far-resonant action' — and her point is that some souls seek that scale of action and cannot find it. Now — I want to resist very firmly the notion that this is primarily about being a woman. Eliot writes of 'unhistoric acts' — deeds that shape the world invisibly, performed by souls whom the world never registers. That condition — of having a nature too large for the vessel that holds it — is not the exclusive property of women. It is the human condition, as Eliot understood it. Dorothea's tragedy is universal — that is precisely what gives it its force.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I expected Edmund to say that, and I want to take it seriously before I push back — because the universalist reading is seductive. It's meant to be. But look at what Eliot actually says about the 'medium' in which these later-born Theresas find themselves. She speaks of 'a social life which seemed to present itself as a labyrinth of petty constraints' — that word 'social' is not accidental. Eliot is naming a specific structure — the structure that produces Dorothea's situation — not a timeless metaphysical predicament. Edmund says the condition is universal. But Casaubon does not face that labyrinth. Lydgate nearly escapes it. Dorothea cannot. The asymmetry is the argument. And I'd call what the novel does with Dorothea's idealism something quite specific: it has been weaponized against her.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I'd be a little careful, though, about turning the narrator into a smirking ironist. Eliot's sympathy for Dorothea is entirely genuine — the famous Prelude makes that plain, the comparison to Saint Theresa who found no epic life to live. What Eliot is doing is more demanding than mockery. She is asking us to hold Dorothea's grandeur and Dorothea's error in the same hand simultaneously. And the error is real. Dorothea chooses Casaubon — no one compels her. She chooses him in the teeth of her sister Celia's very commonsense misgivings, in the teeth of Sir James Chettam's perfectly decent affection. That is not a trap sprung by the system. That is the pride of a young woman who believes her spiritual appetite places her beyond ordinary human judgement.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I'd push back on that — gently, because it's a sophisticated reading — but I think 'she chose' is doing an enormous amount of ideological work there. She cannot study at a university. What she can do is attach herself to a man whose work she believes is great — and pour her intelligence into serving that work. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Blame is the wrong word entirely, and I'd resist it. Eliot is doing something more interesting than distributing blame. But I don't think understanding and responsibility are opposites.
