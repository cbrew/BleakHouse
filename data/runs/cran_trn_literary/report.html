James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to hold on to the financial context here, because I think it deepens everything Caroline and Eleanor are saying. When Miss Matty tells Mary about the bank failure, Gaskell is not offering a vague misfortune — she is giving us the exact numbers. "If that bank goes wrong, I shall lose one hundred and forty-nine pounds thirteen shillings and fourpence a year; I shall only have thirteen pounds a year left." Thirteen pounds a year — that is not poverty in a literary sense; it is genuine destitution by the standards of the time. A working-class labourer might earn twelve to fifteen pounds a year — Miss Matty, a rector's daughter, is about to occupy the same economic floor. And the silence after she speaks — Mary says she squeezed her hand and didn't know what to say — that silence is socially legible. There are no words in Cranford's social grammar for a gentlewoman's absolute ruin.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Mary Smith is one of the most carefully positioned narrators in Victorian fiction, and I don't think she gets nearly enough credit for it. She's an outsider — from Drumble, from a different class register — which means she can observe Cranford's rituals with both warmth and a degree of comic detachment. But what's genuinely brilliant is how Gaskell uses her to manage the reader's emotions — allowing sentiment in through the door, then quietly closing it before it becomes mawkish. There's a passage where Mary admits her own imaginative investment is simply dismantled by reality. She writes that any sentimental romance which she might be inclined to build was quite done away with by his manner. She hands you the emotion she almost had, and then withdraws it — with perfect precision. That move — the offered feeling, then the gentle deflation — is Gaskell's signature, and Mary is the instrument through which it operates.
