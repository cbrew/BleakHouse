Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me first, every time I return to this novel, is the sheer sculptural completeness of that opening description. Dickens doesn't merely tell us that Gradgrind is a man committed to hard fact. He builds it into the man's very architecture — the square forehead, the square coat, the square shoulders. And then he arrives at something quite extraordinary. "his very neckcloth, trained to take him by the throat with an unaccommodating grasp, like a stubborn fact, as it was" The neckcloth has become the ideology. The man is literally throttled by his own convictions, and he doesn't notice — because to him, that is simply the natural order. That is what makes Dickens a moralist of the first order, whatever else one wishes to call him.

I want to hold onto something in that passage — because it isn't merely comic. Gradgrind is not some private eccentric with a peculiar philosophy. He runs a school. He shapes children. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel, I take your point — though I'd say Dickens's concern is less with capitalism as a system than with what happens to the individual soul within it. The passage about M'Choakumchild — Gradgrind's schoolmaster — is where the novel's true alarm sounds, to my ear. Dickens compares him to Morgiana filling jars with boiling oil — checking each vessel to see what it contains. And then he asks a question that is almost a prayer in its anguish. "dost thou think that thou wilt always kill outright the robber Fancy lurking within — or sometimes only maim him and distort him!" Notice that Dickens gives Fancy a capital letter — it is a living thing, a person almost, hiding inside the child. And then — just when you think the novel is closing the door — Dickens does something almost unprecedented. He steps out from behind the curtain entirely. He speaks to you directly, as himself, as the author. "Dear reader!" — two words, and suddenly the whole architecture of the novel shifts. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That direct address is where I think the novel becomes most honest about its own limits — and most radical precisely because of those limits. Dickens knows he cannot resolve structurally what is a structural problem. Stephen's muddle is not cured by one man's redemption. It requires collective action — different choices in every field of action. And what strikes me is the phrase "similar things" — not justice, not happiness, not revolution. Similar things. It is deliberately modest. He is asking only for the imagination to see that what happened here — in Coketown, to Sissy, to Stephen — could be otherwise.

And he ends not with a manifesto but with an image. "We shall sit with lighter bosoms on the hearth, to see the ashes of our fires turn gray and cold." A hearth. Ashes going cold. The natural end of things, faced without dread. That is Dickens's idea of the good life — not prosperity, not progress, not the triumphant march of improvement. Simply: the capacity to sit quietly at the end, with a lighter heart, among people you have not betrayed.
