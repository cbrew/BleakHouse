James Blackstone (social_historian)
Several real figures, and they're worth naming. James Kay-Shuttleworth, who architected the national school inspection system in the 1840s, was someone Dickens followed closely. Then there's Henry Cole, and the broader apparatus of the Department of Practical Art — which insisted that working-class children should learn only what was economically productive. And behind all of it, the long shadow of Benthamite utilitarianism — the idea that human beings are essentially units of productive capacity to be maximised. Dickens visited schools in Preston during the 1853 cotton workers' strike, which fed directly into the novel's setting of Coketown. What he saw there — the drilling of children in rote answers, the physical grimness of the factory schools — he rendered in Hard Times with only slight dramatisation. The caricature, as I say, is almost documentary. The real educators of Coketown-in-life were not significantly more humane than Gradgrind-in-fiction.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Caroline is naming emotionally, I keep wanting to locate in the prose itself — because Dickens earns that devastation technically. James Blackstone (social_historian)
