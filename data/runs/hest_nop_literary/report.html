Opening: The Weight of Inheritance (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to Hester Unpacked — a close, unhurried conversation about one of the nineteenth century's most unjustly overlooked novels. I'm your host, and today we're opening the pages of Hester, published in 1883 by Mrs Oliphant — a writer who was, in her lifetime, as celebrated as George Eliot, and is now, bafflingly, far less read. This is a novel about money, power, inheritance, and what happens when a brilliant young woman is born into a world that has already decided what she is worth. Joining me today are three people who know this novel deeply and care about it passionately — and who bring very different kinds of intelligence to bear on it. Dr Eleanor Hartley teaches creative writing and is a novelist herself — she's the person you want in the room when you're trying to understand how a great book actually works, sentence by sentence. Professor James Blackstone is a legal and social historian whose work on the institutions of Victorian England will give us the real-world stakes behind every scene in this novel. And Ms Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who first read Hester as a teenager and has returned to it many times — she brings the reader's eye, the reading experience itself. Let's begin at the beginning — with the weight of the novel's title, and what it means to be Hester Vernon in this world.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What strikes me every time I return to this novel is how Oliphant sets up the inheritance — not just of money, but of story. We begin not with Hester herself, but with Catherine Vernon — the woman who saved the family bank thirty years before the novel opens. Catherine stepped in when the men failed, used her own fortune to rescue Vernon's bank, and has been running it — and the family — ever since. And then Hester arrives — the daughter of the man who caused the crisis in the first place — and she is, from the very first page, inconvenient. There's a wonderful, painful irony that the two most capable people in the novel — Catherine and Hester — are locked in this inherited antagonism that neither of them entirely chose.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline is absolutely right about that structural irony, and I think it's worth pausing on how Oliphant engineers it at the level of the novel's opening chapters. She gives us this extended flashback — the history of Catherine's rescue of the bank — before we ever meet the young Hester. It's a bold structural choice because it means we arrive at Hester already filtered through Catherine's perspective, already standing in Catherine's shadow. Oliphant wants us to feel the weight of that history pressing down on a child who had nothing to do with creating it. And then there's the detail of the Vernonry — this little cluster of houses Catherine has built on her grounds for the impoverished Vernon relatives. It's a masterstroke — charity that is also surveillance, generosity that is also power.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The Vernonry is fascinating to me precisely because it maps so accurately onto a real social phenomenon of the period. The genteel poor — particularly women — in Victorian England occupied an extraordinarily precarious position. They were too well-born for ordinary work, but utterly without the capital that gave middle-class life its stability. What Catherine has done is essentially institutionalise that dependency — she has made herself the solution to a problem that the economy and the law created. And she is not cruel about it — that's Oliphant's real insight. Catherine is genuinely generous, and that generosity does not prevent her from being, structurally, a figure of domination.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That's the thing that gets me every time — Oliphant refuses to make Catherine a villain. She makes her something much harder to read: a woman who is admirable and stifling in almost equal measure. And Hester, for her part, is not simply a victim either — she is fiercely proud, sometimes maddening in her refusals. There's a moment early in the novel where young Hester insists on making herself visible to Catherine even when it would be easier and safer to efface herself. Oliphant describes it with this wonderful dry precision — Hester's refusal to be grateful on demand is almost a form of self-preservation.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And that pride is the engine of the whole novel, isn't it. Oliphant gives us a heroine whose defining quality — her fierce, uncompromising sense of herself — is exactly what makes her life so difficult. It would be easy to write a passive Hester who suffers beautifully. Instead we get a Hester who pushes back, who sees clearly, who judges — and who is often wrong in her judgments in ways that cost her. That tension between her intelligence and her blind spots is what makes her genuinely novelistic — genuinely alive on the page.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to add something about the title itself — the fact that the novel is simply called Hester, which was not an obvious choice. The subtitle Oliphant originally worked with gestured toward the family and the banking house — but stripping it down to one name is a statement. It says: this is about a person, not a property dispute. And yet the entire novel is, in a very real sense, a property dispute — Hester's claim on her own future against the institution that has absorbed her family.
Host (host)
That tension between the personal and the institutional is really where Oliphant lives as a novelist — and it brings us naturally to what we want to explore next. Because the banking house at the centre of this novel is not just a backdrop — it is an architecture of social constraint, with its own logic and its own cruelties. After the break, we'll look closely at how Oliphant builds that world — the Vernon bank, the relationships it governs, and the secrets it keeps.

The Banking House and Its Secrets: How Oliphant Constructs Social Constraint (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been talking about the weight of inheritance — what Catherine Vernon carries, what she's built, and what it costs her. But inheritance doesn't float free — it's housed somewhere, and in this novel, that somewhere is the banking house itself. Let's dig into how Oliphant uses that institution — the bank, the family firm, the house — to construct the social constraints that every character in Hester has to navigate.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first is that Oliphant never lets the bank be merely a backdrop. It's a structural principle — the way a cathedral organises the lives of a medieval town. Every relationship in Redborough is a tributary of that central financial fact: Vernon's Bank exists, and Catherine controls it. And the genius of the novel's architecture is that Oliphant gives us the bank not through ledgers or business meetings, but through domestic space. The drawing room at the Vernonry, Catherine's evening parties — these are where credit and obligation are actually transacted. The social form is the economic form. That's the compression Oliphant achieves.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Eleanor is right that the bank saturates the social atmosphere — and historically, that's completely accurate to how a private country bank operated in the mid-Victorian period. Vernon's Bank is what we'd call a family partnership bank — before the joint-stock reforms really took hold in the provinces. There were no shareholders to whom you were accountable in any formal sense. The bank's reputation was the family's reputation, completely inseparable. So when Oliphant has Catherine govern the bank by governing the dinner table, she's not being metaphorical — she's being precise. That's genuinely how confidence in a provincial bank was maintained: through visible social order, through the appearance of a family that was solid, harmonious, respectable. Which makes Catherine's management of the dependent Vernons not charity — it's risk management.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
And what's so devastating about that, as a reader, is what it does to the people inside that system. The Vernonry — that row of little houses Catherine has provided for the dependent family members — is one of the more quietly terrible images in Victorian fiction. Because it looks like generosity. It presents itself as generosity. But Oliphant shows us exactly what it costs the people who live there — the constant awareness of obligation, the surveillance, the performance of gratitude. Hester's mother Emma is almost a case study in what that kind of dependency does to a personality over decades. She's become deliberately incurious, a little vapid — not because she's stupid, but because it's safer not to think too hard about your situation.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That contrast between Emma and Hester is one of Oliphant's sharpest structural moves. Emma has accommodated herself entirely to the Vernonry world. Hester refuses to — and the refusal is almost involuntary, almost physiological. And Oliphant is so precise about how that refusal reads socially: as ingratitude, as pride, as something verging on bad manners. There's a moment early in the novel where Catherine observes Hester at one of those evening gatherings and thinks — something to the effect that this is a girl who does not know how to be grateful. And from Catherine's point of view, within her system of values, that's a genuine moral failing. The bank demands gratitude the way a creditor demands interest. It's the emotional tax on charity.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That phrase — the emotional tax on charity — is exactly it. And I'd push that further into the historical context, because in mid-Victorian England, the social mechanisms of obligation weren't merely cultural — they had real legal texture. A woman in Emma Vernon's position — widowed, dependent on a family connection for her housing and income — had almost no independent legal standing. The gratitude Oliphant describes isn't a social nicety — it's the performance required to retain what amounts to a precarious licence to exist in that space. And Catherine knows this. That's what makes her such a complex figure — she isn't villainous; she understands the power she holds, and she tries to wield it well. But the structure itself is merciless, regardless of the individual at its head.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Which is why the young men matter so much — Edward and Harry, the two nephews Catherine has brought into the bank. Because they represent what the institution looks like from the inside — from a position of apparent privilege within it. Edward is the dangerous one. He has the charm and the competence, but he also has this restless impatience with Catherine's authority. And Oliphant does something very clever with Edward — she makes us feel the pull of his energy even as we see how fundamentally untrustworthy he is. He's the person in the novel who could actually match Hester's intelligence — and he uses it for speculation and self-advancement rather than anything genuinely generative. There's a kind of tragedy there that Oliphant doesn't sentimentalise.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Edward is the novel's most technically demanding character, I think, from a craft perspective. Because Oliphant has to make him genuinely attractive — to Hester, to the reader — while seeding every scene with the evidence of his unreliability. The way he talks about the bank's possibilities, about the investments he wants to make — there's a real excitement in those passages, a visionary quality. And then you catch the note of arrogance underneath it, the willingness to risk other people's security for his own excitement. It's a portrait of a certain kind of financial personality that is absolutely still with us — the brilliant speculator who genuinely believes that he's creating value while he's actually creating exposure.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I would only add that what makes the banking crisis, when it comes, so resonant is that Oliphant understands how these things actually happened. Provincial banks failed not through grand villainy but through exactly this mechanism: a trusted insider, excess confidence, speculative positions that outran the deposit base. The Overend Gurney collapse of 1866 — the great Victorian banking disaster — was fresh in the cultural memory when Oliphant was writing this novel. She's drawing on a decade of public anxiety about exactly this kind of internal failure. And crucially — the people who suffer most are the ones with no buffer: the small depositors, the dependent families, people precisely like the Vernonry residents. The institution that housed them could also, at any moment, dissolve the housing.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That precariousness is something Oliphant builds into the very atmosphere of the novel — you feel it before any crisis arrives. There's something almost claustrophobic about Redborough — the way everyone knows everyone's business, the way the bank is visible from so many windows. And Hester feels that claustrophobia more than anyone, because she has the intelligence to understand the system but no sanctioned position within it. She can see exactly what Catherine has built, and she half-admires it — Oliphant is honest about that — and she also finds it suffocating. That ambivalence is one of the things that makes Hester such a modern-feeling novel. The protagonist doesn't simply reject the world she's given.
Host (host)
And that ambivalence — the way Hester sees clearly and is trapped precisely because she sees clearly — takes us naturally into what I think is the heart of the novel. In our next segment, we want to look at Hester's voice itself — her wit, her ambition, and what happens when a woman that articulate collides with a world that has no place for her.

Hester's Voice: Ambition, Wit, and the Limits of Belonging (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been talking about the banking house as a kind of architecture of constraint — the way Catherine Vernon's world is built to exclude as much as it includes. But there's someone inside that world who refuses, stubbornly and brilliantly, to be contained by it — and that's Hester herself. So let's talk about Hester's voice — her ambition, her wit, and the very particular loneliness of being the cleverest person in a room that won't acknowledge it.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What strikes me first about Hester as a character is how Oliphant constructs her intelligence — it's not ornamental, it's structural. Hester doesn't just have opinions; she has a way of perceiving the world that is genuinely analytical, and Oliphant lets us see it working in real time. There's that wonderful moment when Hester watches the young men around her — Roland Ashton, Edward — and she essentially dissects them with her eyes. She sees through their performances in a way none of them can see through hers. And Oliphant is quite clear-eyed about what this costs her — because a young woman who sees too clearly in that world is not admired, she is unsettling.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That word — unsettling — is exactly right, and I think Oliphant is quite deliberately making Hester uncomfortable to be around for the other characters. But what I love is that she's also uncomfortable to classify as a reader. She can be quite difficult — impatient with her mother, proud to the point of harshness — and Oliphant never smooths that over. There's that scene where Hester sits with the old people at the Vernonry and she is so visibly, painfully bored — and she knows it is uncharitable of her to be bored, and she is bored anyway. Oliphant gives us that interiority without apology — this is who Hester is, take her whole.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to add a dimension to that, because historically speaking, what Hester is experiencing is a very specific and well-documented form of social frustration. She has real financial intelligence — she understands money, she understands risk, she grasps the mechanics of the bank in ways that Edward, the heir apparent, arguably does not. And yet in the 1880s, for a woman of her class and situation, there is simply no legitimate channel for that capacity. The novel is painfully precise about this — Hester can see the problem in the bank long before the crisis arrives, and her insight counts for nothing. That is not a personal failure of the people around her — it is the systemic failure of the entire social arrangement.
Host (host)
And I think that's where the wit comes in — because Hester's irony feels like a coping mechanism as much as a personality trait. Eleanor, there's a quality to the way Hester speaks — to the way she talks to Roland especially — that feels almost like sparring.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and the Roland scenes are some of Oliphant's finest writing in the novel, precisely because of that quality. Roland is one of the few characters who actually meets Hester on her own intellectual level — he's quick, he's ironic himself, he doesn't condescend. And what's craftily done is that Oliphant uses him to show us what Hester might have been — what she could have become — if the world were arranged differently. Their conversations have a verbal energy that is genuinely pleasurable — there's almost a Shakespearean quality to the way they trade observations. But Oliphant is also quietly devastating — because Roland ultimately chooses safety, and Hester is left brilliant and unpartnered in any real sense.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That ending still makes me a little furious, if I'm honest. Oliphant ends the novel with Hester in a state of suspension — she has suitors, she has possibility, but nothing is resolved. And the narrator offers us that extraordinary closing note — asking whether a husband or independence or work will be her answer — and declining to say. Oliphant writes something to the effect that there are people who cannot be content, who require something more — and Hester is one of them. She doesn't wrap it up because there is no wrapping it up — for women like Hester, in that world, there never was.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
I read that ending quite specifically as Oliphant's refusal to lie to her reader. The marriage plot resolution — the tidy closure — would have been a falsification of what she'd spent three hundred pages demonstrating. Hester's problem is not that she hasn't found the right man — her problem is structural, as Eleanor said. And a structural problem cannot be solved by a proposal of marriage. The open ending is the only intellectually honest ending available to her.
Host (host)
And yet Hester also belongs — partially, uncomfortably — to the world she chafes against. There's her relationship with Catherine — this extraordinary antagonism that is also, at some level, a recognition.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That relationship is the great engine of the novel for me — more than any of the romantic plots. Oliphant sets up Catherine and Hester as structural mirrors — each is stubborn, each is financially capable, each is emotionally isolated in her own way. But because of the accident of generation and circumstance, one has all the power and the other has none — and they both know it and resent it. What's brilliant craft is how rarely they have a direct, warm exchange — and yet when they do, briefly, we feel the force of what could have been between them.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
There's a scene I come back to — where Catherine watches Hester and almost, almost acknowledges her quality. And you hold your breath — is she going to say it, is she going to offer her something real? And Catherine steps back from it — pride, or habit, or just the weight of her own long history. It's one of those moments where the novel is quietly heartbreaking without ever announcing itself as such.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
Catherine's tragedy, if we want to call it that, is that she built the bank, saved it, and yet the structures she operates within deny her the ability to choose a genuine successor. She can't simply name Hester — not formally, not institutionally — because the world doesn't provide that mechanism. So she defaults to Edward, who is nominally correct and practically disastrous. The irony is excruciating — and deliberate.
Host (host)
And that excruciating irony is threaded through Hester's voice itself — the wit that keeps her sane also keeps her at arm's length from the world. It's a mode of both connection and defence — and I think that ambivalence is exactly what makes Oliphant's narrative technique so interesting to examine. Which is precisely where we're heading next — how Oliphant deploys her narrator, her irony, her prose rhythm to hold all of this in balance.

The Art of Restraint: Oliphant's Narrative Technique in Hester (close_reading)

Host (host)
We've been talking about Hester's voice — her wit, her hunger, the way she's always pressing against the boundaries of what's permitted. But what I want to turn to now is the hand holding the pen — Oliphant's own narrative technique, and the extraordinary discipline it demands.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Restraint is exactly the right word, and it's harder to achieve than pyrotechnics. What Oliphant does throughout Hester is practice a kind of narrative tact — she knows exactly when not to tell us something. Think of the scenes between Hester and Edward Vernon in the later chapters — the emotional stakes are enormous, but Oliphant never melodramatises them. She renders the crisis almost entirely through dialogue and small physical notation — a hand held, a tone of voice, a silence. And the effect is more devastating than any purple passage could be.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
That restraint is something you feel as a reader — there's a kind of held breath to the prose. I keep coming back to old Mrs Vernon — Catherine — and the way Oliphant handles her interiority. We are told, very early on, that Catherine has ruled the Vernons Bank for decades, that she saved it from ruin through sheer force of will. But Oliphant doesn't glorify that. She keeps showing us the cost — the loneliness, the hardness that power requires — without ever making a speech about it. There's a passage where Catherine watches the young people dancing at one of the Merridew parties — she's outside the window of pleasure, really — and Oliphant just lets that image sit. No commentary, no authorial sigh. Just the picture.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me as a historian is how that restraint functions almost legally — as evidence rather than argument. Oliphant presents the material circumstances of her characters — the bank, the Vernons' social position, the question of who controls money — and trusts the reader to draw the conclusion. She doesn't tell us that women are excluded from financial power — she shows us Catherine Vernon, who grasped that power in exceptional circumstances, surrounded by men who take credit for her achievement. The Vernons — the cousins who live in the almshouses she built — are a masterclass in this technique. They flatter her, defer to her, and quietly resent her — and all of that is rendered in surface behaviour, in tone, in what is not said. It's social observation of a very high order.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
James, you've identified something I find technically fascinating — that the Vernoncourt almshouse community is essentially a micro-society with its own power dynamics. And Oliphant builds that society the way a dramatist builds a stage — through entrance and exit, through who speaks first, who defers. There's also something remarkable happening with free indirect discourse throughout the novel. Oliphant will slide, mid-paragraph, from the narrator's perspective into a character's consciousness — and you barely feel the join. The chapter where Catherine first begins to suspect Edward of disloyalty is extraordinary for this reason. We experience her suspicion as she experiences it — not announced, not explained, but felt as a growing unease in the very texture of the sentences. That is a Jamesian tool, deployed without James's apparatus of qualification.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
The moment you mention that chapter I think of how Oliphant describes Catherine's relationship to the bank itself — almost as a marriage. She gave everything to it — her youth, her social life, the possibility of a different kind of love — and in return it is hers. And then Edward comes along, this charming, irresolute young man, and starts treating what is hers as if it were his by inheritance. There's a line — and I'm fairly confident of this — where Oliphant describes Edward as having 'the confident ease of a man who has never been refused anything.' James Blackstone (social_historian)
That portrait of Edward is interesting to me because historically it's a very specific type. He is the heir-apparent who has absorbed the idea of entitlement without absorbing any of the discipline that built what he expects to inherit. Victorian banking families produced this figure with some regularity — men who understood credit as a social lubricant but had no feel for its arithmetic. She never lets us fully hate Edward, which is ultimately more damning than hatred would be.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That dual effect — sympathy and indictment — is one of the hardest things to pull off in fiction. And I think Oliphant achieves it through point-of-view management. She rotates her narrative perspective — we spend time inside Catherine's consciousness, inside Hester's, occasionally inside Edward's — and that rotation means we see each character as both subject and object. Edward seen from inside his own head has a kind of pathos — he is genuinely fond of Hester, genuinely uncertain of himself in ways he'd never admit. Their encounters have this extraordinary layering — respect beneath resentment, recognition beneath wariness — and all of it carried in the prose without being named.

Money, Risk, and the Flight from Responsibility (discussion)

Host (host)
We've been talking about how Oliphant withholds and reveals — the craft of what she chooses not to say. But there's another kind of withholding in this novel — the withholding of money, of risk, of moral courage. Let's talk about what the novel does with financial responsibility — and who, in Oliphant's world, gets to flee from it.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
The financial architecture of this novel is, frankly, one of its most underappreciated achievements. What Oliphant understood — with a precision that rivals any economic historian — is that the Victorian country bank was not merely a commercial institution. It was the locus of community trust, family reputation, and personal honour — all bundled together in a single balance sheet. When Vernon's bank nearly fails at the novel's opening, Oliphant is describing something that happened with devastating regularity in the 1820s and 1830s. Country banks were undercapitalised, over-extended, and entirely dependent on the confidence of depositors — confidence that could evaporate in an afternoon. And it is Catherine Vernon who steps in — out of family duty, or pride, or something more complex — and saves it.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And the novel never lets us forget that act of salvation — it becomes the moral weight that everything else is measured against. What strikes me structurally is the way Oliphant sets up this originary crisis — the bank failure that happened before the novel begins — and then lets it radiate forward through every subsequent relationship. Catherine's authority in Redborough is inseparable from that financial act. She didn't inherit power — she purchased it, or rather, she earned it through risk. And then Oliphant does this rather devastating thing — she surrounds Catherine with men who are constitutionally incapable of taking equivalent risks. Edward Vernon is the great case in point — charming, capable in the ordinary run of business, but fundamentally hollow when it comes to the moment of genuine financial courage.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
Edward is the character I find most painful to read — precisely because Oliphant gives him just enough charm that you want to excuse him. He has all the surface qualities of a capable man — he runs the bank competently enough in peacetime, so to speak. But when the crisis comes — the speculative scheme he's been drawn into, the real money at stake — he doesn't confess, he doesn't act, he runs. And what he runs toward is deeply revealing — he runs toward marriage, toward Emma Ashton, toward the comfortable domestic shelter that a wealthy wife provides. It's a kind of financial cowardice dressed up as romantic choice.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
That pattern — using marriage as a financial escape route — was entirely recognised in the period. Marriage settlements, a wife's separate property — these were real legal instruments, and men in financial difficulty absolutely used them. What's remarkable is that Oliphant makes Edward's manoeuvre legible to a reader without ever stating it baldly. She trusts her reader to understand the financial subtext of what looks like an emotional plot. And the contrast with Hester herself is pointed — because Hester, who has no money and no institutional power, understands risk in a way that Edward, who has both, conspicuously does not.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
That's the great irony at the heart of the novel, isn't it. Hester is denied access to any of the formal mechanisms of financial life — she can't run the bank, she can't make investments in her own name, she can't even be consulted in any official capacity. And yet she is the one character who grasps — intellectually, instinctively — what genuine financial courage looks like. Oliphant sets up this agonising structural joke: the person most capable of saving the bank is the one the institution will never allow near it. There's a moment — when Edward first draws Hester into his confidence about the speculative scheme — where Oliphant lets us see Hester's mind working at full stretch. She sees the risk clearly, she weighs it, and she's willing to take it — whereas Edward has already decided to flee and is merely recruiting her as cover.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
What undoes me about that section of the novel is Hester's excitement — because it's completely genuine. She isn't naive about the danger; she knows exactly what's at stake. But the prospect of actually doing something — of being consequential, of mattering in the financial life of Redborough — is intoxicating to her. And Oliphant renders that with such precision — the way intellect and longing and frustration all fuse in the same moment. There's a quality to Hester's engagement with the scheme that feels almost like falling in love — not with Edward, but with the idea of agency itself.
James Blackstone (social_historian)
And that reading helps explain why the novel's ending — and I'll be careful here, because it genuinely divides readers — is so charged. When the crisis finally breaks and Catherine's bank is once again threatened, it is Hester who acts — who goes to Catherine, who breaks the silence. And Edward, the man in the position of institutional authority, has fled — literally, physically removed himself from Redborough. The flight from responsibility couldn't be more literal, or more damning. Oliphant was writing this in the 1880s, but the figure of the man who gambles with other people's money and then disappears when it goes wrong — that figure has not become less relevant.
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
And Catherine's response to the crisis — the second crisis, the one she faces as an old woman — is what makes her such a rich creation. She has spent the entire novel as the figure of financial authority — almost mythologised, both by the other characters and by her own self-regard. And then Oliphant quietly dismantles that mythology — because it turns out that the very act of saving the bank all those years ago bred a kind of complacency. She trusted Edward because he was family, because the bank had always been family — which is exactly the thinking that nearly destroyed it in the first place. The novel has this beautiful, cruel circularity — history threatening to repeat, but with the roles redistributed in ways that expose everyone differently.
Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
There's something I want to say about the younger Vernons — Harry and Reginald and the rest of the tribe at the Vernonry. Because they represent a different kind of flight from responsibility — not the dramatic, catastrophic flight of Edward, but the quiet, daily refusal of the dependants. Catherine has housed them, supported them, given them an income — and they resent her for it with extraordinary energy.
