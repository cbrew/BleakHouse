Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The thing that strikes you immediately — and I mean the very first sentence — is that the river is already doing something surprising. It isn't a backdrop. It's an actor. George Eliot writes of the Floss hurrying toward the sea, and then the tide rushes back to meet it — and she calls it, and I love this, "an impetuous embrace." You're inside a love story before you've even been introduced to a single human being. But then the narrator does something even more astonishing — she steps into the scene herself and speaks of the Ripple, the little tributary, with what I can only call personal tenderness. "How lovely the little river is, with its dark changing wavelets! It seems to me like a living companion while I wander along the bank, and listen to its low, placid voice, as to the voice of one who is deaf and loving." "Deaf and loving" — that is one of the most quietly devastating phrases I have ever had to read aloud. It describes love that doesn't need a response, love that simply persists — and that, I'd argue, is the emotional key to the entire novel.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to fix on that phrase, and I want to add that the narrator's presence in that opening is doing something morally quite deliberate. This is not a camera panning across a countryside. This is a consciousness remembering. And what memory is doing here — what it always does in Eliot — is conferring value. These places matter because a particular self was formed within them. There is a passage later — the May Day meditation — where Eliot makes this absolutely explicit. "These familiar flowers, these well-remembered bird-notes, this sky, with its fitful brightness, these furrowed and grassy fields, each with a sort of personality given to it by the capricious hedgerows — such things as these are the mother-tongue of our imagination, the language that is laden with all the subtle, inextricable associations the fleeting hours of our childhood left behind them." "The mother-tongue of our imagination." That is a formulation of real philosophical weight. It means that without this early emotional grammar, all later experience is impoverished — you lack the very words with which to feel. And that, to my mind, is the tragic engine of the novel — Maggie Tulliver possesses this mother-tongue more richly than anyone around her, and the world cannot accommodate the depth of feeling it produces in her.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't want to diminish what either of you has said — that opening is genuinely extraordinary prose — but I think we need to hold it alongside something else the novel gives us almost immediately. Look at what else is on that river: black ships laden with fir-planks, sacks of oil-bearing seed, coal. St Ogg's is a commercial town. The Floss is a trade artery. The pastoral beauty exists inside an economy. And what that economy does — what we see with devastating clarity when Mr Tulliver comes downstairs — is reduce a family's entire world to a set of empty places. Eliot's description of that moment is one of the most quietly ferocious passages in the novel. The sunshine that floods the stripped house is described as — and I want everyone to hear this carefully — "the flood of sunshine importunate, as if it had an unfeeling pleasure in showing the empty places, and the marks where well-known objects once had been." "Unfeeling pleasure" — the light itself becomes an agent of humiliation. The law has acted. The creditors have taken what they came for. And the sunshine just keeps on shining.

It was a clear frosty January day on which Mr Tulliver first came downstairs. The bright sun on the chestnut boughs and the roofs opposite his window had made him impatiently declare that he would be caged up no longer; he thought everywhere would be more cheery under this sunshine than his bedroom; for he knew nothing of the bareness below, which made the flood of sunshine importunate, as if it had an unfeeling pleasure in showing the empty places, and the marks where well-known objects once had been. The impression on his mind that it was but yesterday when he received the letter from Mr Gore was so continually implied in his talk, and the attempts to convey to him the idea that many weeks had passed and much had happened since then had been so soon swept away by recurrent forgetfulness, that even Mr Turnbull had begun to despair of preparing him to meet the facts by previous knowledge. The full sense of the present could only be imparted gradually by new experience,—not by mere words, which must remain weaker than the impressions left by the old experience. This resolution to come downstairs was heard with trembling by the wife and children. Mrs Tulliver said Tom must not go to St Ogg’s at the usual hour, he must wait and see his father downstairs; and Tom complied, though with an intense inward shrinking from the painful scene. The hearts of all three had been more deeply dejected than ever during the last few days. For Guest & Co. had not bought the mill; both mill and land had been knocked down to Wakem, who had been over the premises, and had laid before Mr Deane and Mr Glegg, in Mrs Tulliver’s presence, his willingness to employ Mr Tulliver, in case of his recovery, as a manager of the business. This proposition had occasioned much family debating. Uncles and aunts were almost unanimously of opinion that such an offer ought not to be rejected when there was nothing in the way but a feeling in Mr Tulliver’s mind, which, as neither aunts nor uncles shared it, was regarded as entirely unreasonable and childish,—indeed, as a transferring toward Wakem of that indignation and hatred which Mr Tulliver ought properly to have directed against himself for his general quarrelsomeness, and his special exhibition of it in going to law. Here was an opportunity for Mr Tulliver to provide for his wife and daughter without any assistance from his wife’s relations, and without that too evident descent into pauperism which makes it annoying to respectable people to meet the degraded member of the family by the wayside. Mr Tulliver, Mrs Glegg considered, must be made to feel, when he came to his right mind, that he could never humble himself enough; for that had come which she had always foreseen would come of his insolence in time past “to them as were the best friends he’d got to look to.” Mr Glegg and Mr Deane were less stern in their views, but they both of them thought Tulliver had done enough harm by his hot-tempered crotchets and ought to put them out of the question when a livelihood was offered him; Wakem showed a right feeling about the matter,— he had no grudge against Tulliver.

Daniel, that passage always stops me cold when I read it — because Eliot is doing something technically very bold there. She's given moral consciousness to the light — and then immediately denied it. The light is importunate, but it doesn't know it. It can't feel. Which makes it worse, somehow, than cruelty. Cruelty has a mind. Indifference just keeps shining. And what strikes me about Tulliver coming downstairs is the heartbreak of the timing — he thinks it's yesterday. He has no idea. Eliot writes that the attempt to prepare him with words failed — because, as she puts it, words "must remain weaker than the impressions left by the old experience." That's the May Day passage meeting the January passage — childhood's mother-tongue confronting the wordless shock of adult catastrophe.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver has put his finger on something I think is central — the question of what a person can be prepared for, and by what means. The aunts and uncles in that chapter are not monsters — they are, in their own estimation, being reasonable. They want Tulliver to accept Wakem's offer and get on with it. And Eliot is entirely clear-eyed about the logic of their position — they think his pride is a self-indulgence that harms his wife and daughter. What the novel asks us to do — and this is where I part company from certain modern readings — is to hold both truths at once. Tulliver's pride is destructive. And it is also the last remnant of a dignity that the system has already taken almost everything else from.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't entirely disagree with Edmund on that — in fact I think that tension is precisely what makes Eliot's social vision so much sharper than a simple morality tale. But notice who benefits from the arrangement. Wakem buys the mill at auction — the mill that defined a family's identity for generations — and then offers to employ the man he's dispossessed as his own manager. And the respectable relatives pronounce this generous. Eliot even notes their concern about the "too evident descent into pauperism which makes it annoying to respectable people to meet the degraded member of the family by the wayside." "Annoying to respectable people" — the degradation of a human being is reframed as a social inconvenience. That is Eliot's anger showing — very controlled, very exact — but it is unmistakably there.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Thank you. I think the first thing to say is that Maggie Tulliver is one of the great achievements of the Victorian novel — precisely because she is not a heroine in the conventional sense. She is a problem — to herself, to her family, to the society that cannot accommodate her. And Eliot introduces us to this problem with tremendous economy in that early scene where Maggie watches the boys at their studies. Philip Wakem looks up from his books and catches her eyes — and it is his perception that gives us the key to her character. "her eyes were full of unsatisfied intelligence, and unsatisfied beseeching affection." Two kinds of unsatisfied longing, placed side by side, inseparable — and that is the whole of Maggie in a single clause.

What I keep returning to — as a performer, as someone who has lived in this prose — is how fully Eliot earns the tragedy. She has shown us Maggie's weakness — the furtive gaze across the room, the moment's mutual gaze that they thought of till it came.
