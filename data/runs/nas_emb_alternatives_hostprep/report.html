Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That line is one of the great stealth moves in Victorian fiction — and I mean that as high praise. Listen to how Gaskell builds up to it — she gives you the 'deep lead-coloured cloud,' the 'faint taste and smell of smoke,' those 'long, straight, hopeless streets.' And then she suddenly drops in this extraordinary image — "a great oblong many-windowed factory stood up, like a hen among her chickens, puffing out black 'unparliamentary' smoke." A hen among her chickens — it's comic, it's almost affectionate, it's farmyard. And right in the middle of it she puts 'unparliamentary' in quotation marks — which means she's quoting the Milton workers' own slang for illegal levels of smoke pollution. So in a single image you have charm, comedy, and something close to outrage — and she slides between them without breaking stride.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Oliver is right that it's doing double work — but I'd press on what that farmyard image is actually naturalizing. The hen and her chickens — it's a hierarchy of production that looks organic, maternal, almost benign. But what the image is describing is a factory dominating a street of workers' housing. Gaskell gives you those 'long, straight, hopeless streets of regularly-built houses, all small and of brick' — that word 'hopeless' is doing serious work. "Quick they were whirled over long, straight, hopeless streets of regularly-built houses, all small and of brick." The workers' homes are architecturally stamped with their condition — small, identical, regular, brick. The comedy of the hen is Gaskell's way of making the horror legible without making it unbearable — but the horror is still there.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I don't dissent from either of you on the quality of the image — it is superb. But I want to be careful about what we're asking prose style to carry. Gaskell is giving us Milton through Margaret's eyes — and what that passage really records is the shock of a sensibility encountering something it has no vocabulary for. This passage is where I first understood that Gaskell was writing something genuinely frightening underneath the social-problem surface. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to engage the question you raised before we started — whether this fog is symbol or literal atmosphere, and whether the distinction matters. Because I'd argue very strongly that it matters enormously — and that Edmund will want to read it as Margaret's inner state made visible. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Daniel's diagnosis is bracing, but I think it performs a sleight of hand. The fog and the blank wall are not separable from Margaret's experiencing of them — that is precisely Gaskell's method. She is not a sociologist distributing her attention equally across all strata of suffering. She is a novelist, and her subject in this moment is one woman sitting on a box in a fog-shrouded room, reading a letter from a woman whose life has continued without interruption. The factories matter insofar as they strip away Margaret's comforting illusions — that is the novel's moral engine, not the other way around.

What I'd add — and I think it actually bridges both your positions — is what Gaskell does with the nostalgia.
