Let Oliver set the tone early with a passage read aloud—it warms the room and immediately shows the prose has multiple registers. The key tension to cultivate is whether nature's indifference is primarily funny, theological, or political: all three experts have a stake in that question and none of them fully accounts for the others' reading. Don't let Edmund and Daniel talk past each other on the weather point—that's actually the richest disagreement in the room and it could go somewhere surprising. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think Oliver is right about the timing, though I'd describe what Forster is doing rather differently. The sky passage is theology. Not comedy, not irony — theology. Forster writes that when the sky chooses, glory can rain into the Chandrapore bazaars — a benediction pass from horizon to horizon. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I don't want to be uncharitable, but 'the condition of human existence' is doing quite a lot of work there to smooth over some very specific arrangements. Because the squirrel passage — the one Oliver hasn't read yet, which I think is where Forster really shows his hand — doesn't offer comfort. It offers demolition. That phrase — 'that calls itself human' — is an extraordinary move. It doesn't say 'human beings.' It says the minority that calls itself human. The British colonial project rested on a hierarchy of the human. And Forster, with a subordinate clause, liquidates it.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The question of whether Forster is inside the colonial gaze or outside it has always struck me as slightly the wrong question to put to a novel. A novel is not a position paper. It is an act of imagination — which means it contains more than its author intended and more than its critics extract. What Forster is doing in that opening page — that extraordinary invocation of sky and light and benediction — is placing every character, English and Indian, beneath something they cannot master. Is he on anyone's side? I think he is on the side of honesty about human limitation. And that, in a novel set in British India, turns out to be radical enough.

At this distance and hour they leapt into beauty; they were Monsalvat, Walhalla, the towers of a cathedral, peopled with saints and heroes, and covered with flowers. What miscreant lurked in them, presently to be detected by the activities of the law? Who was the guide, and had he been found yet? What was the “echo” of which the girl complained? He did not know, but presently he would know. Great is information, and she shall prevail. It was the last moment of the light, and as he gazed at the Marabar Hills they seemed to move graciously towards him like a queen, and their charm became the sky’s. At the moment they vanished they were everywhere, the cool benediction of the night descended, the stars sparkled, and the whole universe was a hill. Lovely, exquisite moment—but passing the Englishman with averted face and on swift wings. He experienced nothing himself; it was as if someone had told him there was such a moment, and he was obliged to believe. And he felt dubious and discontented suddenly, and wondered whether he was really and truly successful as a human being. After forty years’ experience, he had learnt to manage his life and make the best of it on advanced European lines, had developed his personality, explored his limitations, controlled his passions—and he had done it all without becoming either pedantic or worldly. A creditable achievement, but as the moment passed, he felt he ought to have been working at something else the whole time,—he didn’t know at what, never would know, never could know, and that was why he felt sad.
