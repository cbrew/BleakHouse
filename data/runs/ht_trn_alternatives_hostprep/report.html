Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I take Daniel's point about the physical precision of that portrait — Dickens is extraordinarily good on the body — but I think we're in danger of reducing Stephen to a specimen. If Stephen's dignity is merely a physiological reflex — what pressure produces before the machine wins — then there is no moral content to it, and the novel becomes a pamphlet. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
It's fair as a challenge, and Edmund is right that I don't want to evacuate the character of all moral content — that would make for a very bad reading of a very good novel. What I'm arguing is that Stephen's moral heroism — and I grant it — is being exercised inside a structure that makes it futile, and Dickens knows it. Think about what Stephen actually does in those pages: he goes to Bounderby to ask how to be freed from an abusive wife. The answer, of course, is that the law offers him nothing — divorce is for people with money, which is to say it is not for Stephen Blackpool at all. And the novel is unsparing about what that costs him — not just comfort, but character itself. Dickens writes of 'the change it made in his character for the worse every day, of the dreadful nature of his existence, bound hand and foot, to a dead woman, and tormented by a demon in her shape.' The system doesn't just deprive Stephen of comfort — it degrades him, daily, morally. That is not a pamphlet. That is an indictment.
