Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What I hear is a writer who has decided that grammar itself is going to be one of his subjects. That first page is mostly noun phrases — no verbs, or almost none. "London." Full stop. "Michaelmas term lately over." No main verb in sight. The world is so mired, so stuck, that the language itself can barely move. And then — just when you've adjusted to this paralysed world — he drops this on you. "As much mud in the streets as if the waters had but newly retired from the face of the earth, and it would not be wonderful to meet a Megalosaurus, forty feet long or so, waddling like an elephantine lizard up Holborn Hill." A dinosaur. On Holborn Hill. The sheer comic audacity of it — that word "waddling" especially — is Dickens saying: I am in complete command of this world I am making. And if I want a prehistoric monster on a London street, there he is, and you will find it entirely plausible.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me is that the comedy and the darkness are completely inseparable — you cannot have one without the other. The Megalosaurus is absurd, yes — but what Dickens is actually saying is that this London is pre-civilisational. We have not, despite all our institutions, our courts, our centuries of law, escaped the primordial mud. He understood what Burke understood — that civilisation is a fragile inheritance, not a permanent achievement. And then comes the fog passage, which I think is among the most sustained pieces of prose in the Victorian novel. "Fog everywhere. Fog up the river, where it flows among green aits and meadows; fog down the river, where it rolls defiled among the tiers of shipping and the waterside pollutions of a great (and dirty) city." The anaphora — that hammering repetition of "fog" — is not rhetoric for its own sake. It enacts the fog's own logic: it penetrates every sentence just as it penetrates every space. There is no escape from it — not for the Greenwich pensioner, not for the cabin boy, not for the reader.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund is right that there's no escape — but I want to push on who, specifically, is being crushed by this fog. Dickens is very precise about hierarchy even in his imagery. The fog is in the eyes and throats of the Greenwich pensioners — the indigent old. It is "cruelly pinching" the apprentice boy's toes and fingers — a child labourer, on deck in November. The wrathful skipper suffers it too — but notice: he's down in his close cabin, with his pipe. Class position determines how much of the fog you have to endure, and with what shelter. And then Dickens moves us directly into Chancery — and this is where his real target comes into focus. "This is the Court of Chancery, which has its decaying houses and its blighted lands in every shire, which has its worn-out lunatic in every madhouse and its dead in every churchyard, which has its ruined suitor with his slipshod heels and threadbare dress borrowing and begging through the round of every man's acquaintance." Every shire. Every madhouse. Every churchyard. Chancery is not an isolated dysfunction — it is a national system that produces suffering at scale.

And what's extraordinary is that Dickens arrives at that indictment through sheer rhetorical momentum. The passage builds — "which has... which has... which has" — it's a relentless accumulation. Just like the mud, building up at compound interest — the phrase he uses a page earlier. The structure of the prose mirrors the structure of the system he's attacking. That is the warning from inside the system, spoken by the only honest men in it. Dickens gives that line to the honourable practitioners — the very people who know it best.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I will grant that Dickens' attack on Chancery is among the most brilliant things in the novel — I would not dream of minimising it. But I would gently resist Daniel's framing of Chancery as a class mechanism, precisely designed to protect the wealthy. The ruin that Chancery visits upon Richard Carstone, after all, is the ruin of a gentleman's son — a man of good family. What Dickens is diagnosing is something more like a moral disease — an institution that corrupts hope itself. The fog is a literary device of the very highest order — a sustained objective correlative for everything the novel will explore. Obscurity, confusion, the impossibility of seeing clearly — in law, in society, in one's own heart.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Thank you. What strikes me first, and strikes me every time I return to this passage, is the precision of Esther's diagnosis. She is not condemning Richard — she genuinely loves him — but she sees, with uncommon clarity, exactly where the fault lies. Dickens writes — and Esther is the vehicle — that the system had enabled Richard to dash through his tasks with fair credit and often distinction, but in a fitful, dazzling way that had confirmed his reliance on those very qualities in himself which it had been most desirable to direct and train. That word — confirmed — is doing tremendous work there. The system has not merely failed to correct Richard's flaws; it has hardened them, ratified them, given them the seal of success. And then comes one of Dickens' great epigrams, and I make no apology for reading it in full. "They were good qualities, without which no high place can be meritoriously won, but like fire and water, though excellent servants, they were very bad masters." Burke himself might have written that sentence. The tragedy is not that Richard lacks gifts — he is positively radiant with them. The tragedy is that his gifts have been allowed to govern him rather than serve him. That is a failure of formation — of education in the deepest sense — and it is a failure that no legal system can repair.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund is right that Esther's diagnosis is precise — but I want to push at where she locates the blame. She identifies a system — the educational system — that addressed all boys identically, regardless of individual character and capacity. That is not an accident of pedagogy. That is the operating logic of a class society that needs certain kinds of people and produces them at scale, regardless of what is lost in the process. Richard is not a singular failure of character — he is a predictable output of a machine that was never designed with his flourishing in mind. And then Chancery gets hold of him, and we see what the second machine does to what the first has already weakened. Dickens gives us an image in Chapter 39 that I think is one of the most quietly horrifying in the novel. Richard walks into Lincoln's Inn and the narrator tells us, with terrible calm: "On many such loungers have the speckled shadows of those trees often fallen; on the like bent head, the bitten nail, the lowering eye, the lingering step, the purposeless and dreamy air, the good consuming and consumed, the life turned sour." It is known for certain that the handsome Lady Dedlock lies in the mausoleum in the park, where the trees arch darkly overhead, and the owl is heard at night making the woods ring; but whence she was brought home to be laid among the echoes of that solitary place, or how she died, is all mystery. Some of her old friends, principally to be found among the peachy-cheeked charmers with the skeleton throats, did once occasionally say, as they toyed in a ghastly manner with large fans—like charmers reduced to flirting with grim death, after losing all their other beaux—did once occasionally say, when the world assembled together, that they wondered the ashes of the Dedlocks, entombed in the mausoleum, never rose against the profanation of her company. But the dead-and-gone Dedlocks take it very calmly and have never been known to object.

There is a hush upon Chesney Wold in these altered days — Dickens begins the chapter with that phrase, and it is entirely earned. Everything in this closing movement of the novel is muted — the great world has gone quiet. And Dickens is wonderfully dry about the attempts to suppress what is known, in that sentence about the rumours. "It is a lame story, feebly whispering and creeping about, and any brighter spark of life it shows soon dies away." "Feebly whispering and creeping about" — Dickens gives the rumour the same quality of miasma he gave the fog in the first chapter. The fog hasn't cleared — it has simply moved indoors, into the corridors of reputation and suppressed scandal.

Edmund's reading is elegant — but I want to name what that suppression actually is. Dickens tells us, quite bluntly, that Sir Leicester paid people to stay silent. "The story goes that Sir Leicester paid some who could have spoken out to hold their peace." The aristocracy's relationship to truth has always been transactional — you purchase the version of events that protects your name. And the brilliant, corrosive detail is that even money can't make it entirely stick. The story still creeps about — lame, feeble, but alive.

And then Dickens does something I find completely irresistible — he brings in the dead Dedlocks themselves. The society ladies, those magnificent grotesques, once suggested that the ancestral Dedlocks in the mausoleum must surely resent Lady Dedlock's presence among them. These are the "peachy-cheeked charmers with the skeleton throats" — one of the most perfectly savage phrases Dickens ever wrote. "Peachy-cheeked charmers with the skeleton throats." The bloom and the bone in the same breath. And then the punchline: "the dead-and-gone Dedlocks take it very calmly and have never been known to object." "The dead-and-gone Dedlocks take it very calmly and have never been known to object." After everything — after all the tragedy and the fog and the death — Dickens refuses to end without a laugh. That's why he's Dickens.
