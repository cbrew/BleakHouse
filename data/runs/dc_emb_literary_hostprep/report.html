Eleanor, most readers treat that opening line — 'Whether I shall turn out to be the hero of my own life' — as charming false modesty. You've argued it's actually the engine of the entire novel. What's going on there?
Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's one of the most quietly radical opening gambits in all of English fiction. Because it doesn't ask whether David will triumph — it asks whether narration itself can constitute a self. The word 'hero' is doing enormous work: heroism in the classical sense, the protagonist of a story — but also moral worth, the right to be taken seriously. And the answer Dickens is reaching toward is: you become a hero by writing your own story. Not living it — writing it. The retrospective act is constitutive. And the novel never lets you forget that David is always doing both simultaneously — living forward and narrating backward. Which is why a passage like the one in chapter thirteen stops me cold every time. He writes: 'When my thoughts go back, now, to that slow agony of my youth, I wonder how much of the histories I invented for such people hangs like a mist of fancy over well-remembered facts.' 'Mist of fancy over well-remembered facts' — that's not a comfortable metaphor. That's a confession. The narrator is telling us: I have invented so much that I no longer know where imagination ends and memory begins.

It was this. My father had left a small collection of books in a little room upstairs, to which I had access (for it adjoined my own) and which nobody else in our house ever troubled. From that blessed little room, Roderick Random, Peregrine Pickle, Humphrey Clinker, Tom Jones, the Vicar of Wakefield, Don Quixote, Gil Blas, and Robinson Crusoe, came out, a glorious host, to keep me company. They kept alive my fancy, and my hope of something beyond that place and time,—they, and the Arabian Nights, and the Tales of the Genii,—and did me no harm; for whatever harm was in some of them was not there for me; I knew nothing of it. It is astonishing to me now, how I found time, in the midst of my porings and blunderings over heavier themes, to read those books as I did. It is curious to me how I could ever have consoled myself under my small troubles (which were great troubles to me), by impersonating my favourite characters in them—as I did—and by putting Mr. and Miss Murdstone into all the bad ones—which I did too. I have been Tom Jones (a child’s Tom Jones, a harmless creature) for a week together. I have sustained my own idea of Roderick Random for a month at a stretch, I verily believe. I had a greedy relish for a few volumes of Voyages and Travels—I forget what, now—that were on those shelves; and for days and days I can remember to have gone about my region of our house, armed with the centre-piece out of an old set of boot-trees—the perfect realization of Captain Somebody, of the Royal British Navy, in danger of being beset by savages, and resolved to sell his life at a great price. The Captain never lost dignity, from having his ears boxed with the Latin Grammar. I did; but the Captain was a Captain and a hero, in despite of all the grammars of all the languages in the world, dead or alive.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
With pleasure. And I think the list itself is the argument. Listen to how Dickens accumulates the titles — it's almost liturgical. 'From that blessed little room, Roderick Random, Peregrine Pickle, Humphrey Clinker, Tom Jones, the Vicar of Wakefield, Don Quixote, Gil Blas, and Robinson Crusoe, came out, a glorious host, to keep me company.' 'They kept alive my fancy, and my hope of something beyond that place and time.' Do you hear the rhythm of those proper names — they have weight, they have bodies? On the page they're a list. In the mouth they're a procession — they arrive one by one, each with a different gait. And that word 'blessed' — it's theological. This room is a sanctuary. These books are not entertainment; they are salvation. Dickens is making the case for fiction itself. For what reading does when the world is crushing a child.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I don't want to be the cold water in this warm bath. But I think the warmth is exactly the problem. A child is being used as cheap, essentially unregulated labour in a blacking warehouse, with no legal recourse whatsoever, no guardian acting on his behalf. And Dickens's answer — the novel's answer — is: he had good books. And they kept alive his fancy. That is a beautiful answer. It is also a deeply convenient one — for anyone who would prefer not to reform the law. The emotional power of that passage does genuine political work — it displaces structural outrage into aesthetic consolation.

Caroline Woodcourt (close_reader)
But James — Dickens was explicitly campaigning for reform his entire career. The outrage is there. The 'I picture myself' passages — and I think about the one in chapter seven — are not consolation. They're accusation. 'I picture myself going up to bed, among the unused rooms, and sitting on my bed-side crying for a comfortable word from Peggotty.' The adult David hasn't escaped those rooms. He is still in them. He is showing them to us and saying: look. That repeated 'I picture myself' — it's not nostalgia. It's the grammar of a wound that hasn't closed.

The passage concerns David's observations inside a model prison, and it reads, on the surface, as standard Dickensian mockery of reformist cant. But look at what he actually says. "I found a vast amount of profession, varying very little in character: varying very little (which I thought exceedingly suspicious), even in words." The parenthetical — 'which I thought exceedingly suspicious' — is where the real argument lives. He is not saying these men are hypocrites despite the system — he is saying the system *produces* and *rewards* a certain kind of performer. 'Their conceit, their vanity, their want of excitement, and their love of deception... all prompted to these professions, and were all gratified by them.'
