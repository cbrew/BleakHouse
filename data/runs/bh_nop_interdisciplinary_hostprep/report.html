Rebecca Martinez
Dickens understands something cosmological here—that from inside a system of fog, you cannot solve the fog problem by clarifying vision or improving management. The fog isn't a problem to be solved. It's the condition we're observing from. That's why the novel is so long and so repetitive. That's the form matching the content in a way that anticipates modernism.
Key points:
  • Dickens renders fog as a physical manifestation of systemic entropy—the same way I observe dust and gas dissipating in protoplanetary disks; disorder isn't a failure of the system but its fundamental nature
  • The legal fog mirrors astronomical observation itself: we're always looking through obscuring media, trying to discern structure in chaos, and sometimes the fog *is* the only honest picture we can get
  • Bleak House opens by refusing to let us see clearly, which is radically honest about how institutions actually function—not as rational mechanisms but as weather systems that wear down individual intention

Sarah Chen
Dickens doesn't just *describe* linguistic chaos and social exclusion—he *embeds* them in his syntax and phonology. When Jo can't be heard and the fog has no subject, it's not a metaphor layered on top of the prose. It *is* the prose structure. You can't separate the content from the linguistic form because the form *is* the argument. That's why close reading of the actual grammar—constituency, agreement, prosodic markers in punctuation—matters as much as plot to understanding what Dickens is doing.
Key points:
  • Dickens's famous fog passage is structurally headless—no finite main clause until paragraph two—which forces readers into the fog's disorientation linguistically, not just imagistically
