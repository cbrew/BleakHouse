Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Well — I stand by it, and I'll tell you why. When a narrator says 'no words can express' something, and then immediately expresses it — at length, with tremendous rhetorical flourish — he is performing. He is not failing to find words. He is choosing the most melodramatic words available. Read the whole sentence — it's gorgeous, it's technically a single breath — let me do it justice. 'No words can express the secret agony of my soul as I sunk into this companionship; compared these henceforth everyday associates with those of my happier childhood—not to say with Steerforth, Traddles, and the rest of those boys; and felt my hopes of growing up to be a learned and distinguished man, crushed in my bosom.' That last phrase — 'crushed in my bosom' — is the most theatrical thing. It's practically opera. But here's what I mean by comedy — not cruelty, not mockery of the boy. Dickens knows exactly what he is doing with this voice. The adult David is narrating his own childhood with a very slight, very affectionate excess of feeling. The suffering is real. And the performance of the suffering is also real. Both things are true at once — and that doubleness is the novel's deepest trick.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Both of my colleagues make interesting observations. But I wonder if they are, in their different ways, evading the simplest and most truthful reading. Dickens is not winking at the reader. He is not deploying ideology. He is recording the experience of a sensitive child whose formation has been violently interrupted. And what makes that interruption so devastating is precisely what we find in the earlier chapters — the quality of what was lost. 'I had been apt enough to learn, and willing enough, when my mother and I had lived alone together.' That is the Eden. And then Murdstone arrives. Dickens gives us the most precise possible image of what a child requires for learning — gentleness, safety, a mother's encouragement — and then shows us its systematic destruction. That, to my mind, is the moral centre of the early chapters. Not class. Lovelessness.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What Dickens is doing in that passage is quite extraordinary, and I think it deserves to be read properly before we argue about it. 'When my thoughts go back, now, to that slow agony of my youth, I wonder how much of the histories I invented for such people hangs like a mist of fancy over well-remembered facts!' 'When I tread the old ground, I do not wonder that I seem to see and pity, going on before me, an innocent romantic boy, making his imaginative world out of such strange experiences and sordid things!' The imagination is not escapism. It is the faculty that allows David to survive what cannot be endured passively. By 'inventing histories' for the strangers around him, he remains a person — a feeling, creating subject — rather than becoming merely a small labouring animal. And the adult narrator who can 'pity' that boy, who can look back with compassion rather than bitterness — that is a man whose character suffering has actually formed, rather than destroyed. That is what I mean by grace. It is earned, not given.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't think Edmund's reading is sentimental — it's genuinely moving, actually — but I think it does something very convenient. It aestheticises the damage. It turns child labour into a school of the soul. The warehouse didn't put David there to build his character. The warehouse put David there to produce blacking. That is its function. What I find genuinely fascinating in Edmund's passage is that phrase: 'a mist of fancy over well-remembered facts.' The 'well-remembered facts' are the structural reality — the exploitation, the class position. The 'mist of fancy' is the narrative David builds over them to make them bearable. Dickens is far more honest about that tension than his admirers often are. He doesn't pretend the mist isn't there.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Murdstone is the villain and the system is the villain — those aren't mutually exclusive. What Murdstone represents is the ideology of 'firmness' — the Victorian belief that severity is virtue, that the weak deserve their suffering. That ideology is not personal to him. It is what the ruling class requires its institutions to teach. What I find most telling in the chapter six passage is this detail: the lessons were 'presided over nominally by my mother, but really by Mr. Murdstone and his sister.' 'They were very long, very numerous, very hard — perfectly unintelligible, some of them, to me — and I was generally as much bewildered by them as I believe my poor mother was herself.' Both mother and child are being subjected to the same regime. The cruelty is not aberrant — it is educational policy. Edmund wants to call that lovelessness. I'd call it the domestic application of a broader system of dominance. Same facts, different name.
