  We've spent this episode pulling at threads — the dual narration, the fog, the institutions, the grotesques — and I want to end by asking how it all holds together.  (+600ms @0.98x *holds together*)
  Bleak House is famously enormous — sixty-seven chapters, a cast of dozens, two narrators running in parallel — and yet it feels, on rereading, like a single intricate mechanism.  (@0.97x *mechanism*)

[Eleanor Hartley]
  The mechanism metaphor is exactly right — and Dickens hands you the key in the very first chapter.  [c1:p16]  (+220ms @1.01x *first chapter*)
  The whole of Chancery is rendered as a machine that produces nothing but its own continuation — and Dickens gives you the image in miniature with one devastating sentence.  [c1:p16]  (@1.01x *miniature*)
  He's describing the lawyers in court — Mr. Tangle's associates — and the setup is almost musical.  [c1:p16]  (@0.98x [setup] *musical*)
  > "Eighteen of Mr. Tangle's learned friends, each armed with a little summary of eighteen hundred sheets, bob up like eighteen hammers in a pianoforte, make eighteen bows, and drop into their eighteen places of obscurity."  [c1:p16]  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *eighteen,pianoforte,obscurity*)
  Eighteen hammers in a pianoforte — it's comic, yes, but it's also precise: these are keys being struck, producing noise, producing nothing.  [c1:p16]  (@1.01x [commentary] *noise,nothing*)
  And the repetition of 'eighteen' is doing structural work — it enacts the very mechanical redundancy the novel is indicting.  [c1:p16]  (@1.02x *repetition,redundancy*)
  That is the whole novel in embryo: a system that replicates itself perfectly and illuminates nothing.  [c1:p16]  (<1200ms> *replicates,illuminates*)

[Oliver Trevelyan]
  And when you read it aloud — which I've done rather too many times — you feel the joke and the horror arriving at exactly the same moment.  [c1:p16]  (+210ms @1.02x *joke,horror*)

  It's written for the voice. Dickens almost always is.  [c1:p16]  (*voice*)
  But what holds the novel together is also that he never lets the comedy anaesthetise you for long.  (*anaesthetise*)
  The machine has real victims. You're laughing at the pianoforte keys, and then Esther takes you somewhere that removes all distance.  [c8:p81]  (<1200ms> @0.99x *victims,distance*)

[Daniel Rosen]
  And that alternation is the structural argument, not just a tonal trick.  (+200ms @0.99x *structural argument*)
  The satire of Chancery and the exposure of poverty are not two different books loosely stitched together — they are two views of the same system.  (@0.99x *same system*)
  Esther's narration gives you the cost in human flesh — and Dickens makes it almost unbearable to look at directly.  [c8:p81]  (@0.98x *cost,unbearable*)
  She describes arriving at a labourer's cottage with Mrs. Pardiggle — the great self-congratulating philanthropist — and the prose is like a cold cloth on the face.  [c8:p81]  (@0.97x [setup] *philanthropist*)
  > "Besides ourselves, there were in this damp, offensive room a woman with a black eye, nursing a poor little gasping baby by the fire; a man, all stained with clay and mud and looking very dissipated, lying at full length on the ground, smoking a pipe; a powerful young man fastening a collar on a dog; and a bold girl doing some kind of washing in very dirty water."   The woman hides her bruised eye. Nobody offers a welcome. Dickens trusts the facts to do the work — and they do.  [c8:p81]  (<1200ms> @0.98x *bruised eye,facts*)

[Eleanor Hartley]
  What Daniel's describing is also a formal choice about distance — and Esther's narration is the instrument Dickens uses to collapse it.  [c8:p81]  (+200ms @1.01x *distance,instrument*)
  The omniscient narrator can frame and satirise Chancery from a height. But Esther is inside the room. She nearly fills it.  [c8:p81]  (*inside,fills*)
  And then, at the very end of the novel, that same narrator — who has given herself so little — is given everything.  [c64:p39]  (@0.99x *given everything*)
  The moment with Jarndyce and Woodcourt is — on the page — almost unbearably restrained.  [c64:p39]  (@0.98x *restrained*)

[Oliver Trevelyan]
  I've read that scene aloud many times, and the difficulty is always 'Nothing, nothing.'  [c64:p39]  (+210ms)
  If you play it too sadly, you betray Jarndyce — because he means it, or is trying to mean it.  [c64:p39]  (*means it*)

[Daniel Rosen]

  The web Dickens constructs — Chancery at the centre, with threads reaching into every class and corner of England — is not a metaphor he invented for effect.  (@0.98x *web,centre*)
  It is the actual structure of mid-Victorian capital: everything connected, everything contaminated, no exit for those at the bottom.  (@0.97x *contaminated,no exit*)
  Dickens understood this more deeply than he could perhaps have articulated — and the novel's form is the understanding.  (<1200ms> @0.97x *form,understanding*)
