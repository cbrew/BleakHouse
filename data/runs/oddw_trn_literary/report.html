James Blackstone (social_historian)
What strikes me about that passage, from a historical vantage point, is how entirely legal Widdowson's rage was — in terms of how the law actually positioned a husband. A wife who left the marital home without cause could be compelled to return — and 'cause' was defined very narrowly indeed. What Gissing captures in those later chapters — the detective, the accusation, the refusal to return — is a woman navigating that legal labyrinth with almost no leverage at all. Consider how Mary Barfoot's letter frames the situation — the language is so meticulously precise about what the detective saw and what he didn't see. "This man saw her go to the flats in Bayswater where Everard lives and knock at his door." She knocked at a door — that is all — and from that gesture a man constructs his wife's destruction. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Caroline's point about silence connects directly to what Gissing does with Rhoda in those same chapters — silence as strategy, but also as suffering. Rhoda is the novel's strongest character — its most intellectually formidable woman — and yet she is brought to an impossible crossroads. Gissing frames it almost like a logical proof — she can't marry with doubt in her mind, and she can't go to Monica without humiliating herself. The sentence that captures the trap most precisely comes right at the end of that passage — listen to the architecture of it. "The incredulity she could not help must either part them for ever, or be to her an occasion of new triumph." 'Occasion of new triumph' — that phrase is extraordinary because triumph is exactly the wrong word for what she feels. It's Rhoda reframing devastation as victory — because that's the only way she can survive it without surrendering who she is.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And there's a legal dimension to Monica's restraint that I think sharpens the comedy considerably. James Blackstone (social_historian)
And I think the place to start is with the sheer material weight of marriage in this novel. Gissing is meticulous about money, property, and what it costs a woman — in pounds and shillings — to step outside the institution. Monica's situation makes this grotesquely clear. She has money — notionally — but as Gissing puts it, the question is how to obtain possession of it. "Unable to reply, she pursued her reflections. She had money, but how to obtain possession of it?" That word — obtain — does a great deal of work. The money is hers in theory, but in practice it belongs to a structure she cannot simply walk out of.
