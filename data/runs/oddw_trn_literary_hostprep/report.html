James Blackstone (social_historian)
It's a fair challenge, and I want to take it seriously rather than deflect it. My argument isn't that Monica has no agency — it's that the conditions under which she exercises it are so devastatingly constrained that admiring the gesture risks sentimentalising the catastrophe. Gissing gives us Virginia's account of the situation, and it is almost excruciating in its specificity. "He has offered to let us have the house to ourselves, but it's no use. He writes to her, but she won't reply." Widdowson is paying their rent, offering reconciliation without conditions, and Monica still refuses. That refusal is real — I don't want to flatten it — but the material situation is that she is destitute, ill, dependent on her abuser's charity even in her resistance. To call that liberation without acknowledging the price feels, to me, like a category error.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and I think this passage is actually the key to everything, including what James and Caroline are both reaching for. Gissing describes Widdowson seizing Monica, throwing her to her knees — and then the narrator does something extraordinary. "It was well that he held her by the garment and not by the neck, for his hand closed with murderous convulsion, and the desire of crushing out her life was for an instant all his consciousness." 'It was well that' — that's not neutral narration. The narrator is intervening — stepping into the scene and saying: here is where we nearly lost her, and here is the margin by which we didn't. That's mercy — not sentimentality, but the narrator choosing to make the near-miss visible rather than the actual killing. And the judgment is in the phrase 'murderous convulsion' — Gissing doesn't let Widdowson off as a man overcome by passion; he names the impulse as murder. That precision is what great narration does — it refuses the exculpatory language before it can form.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I want to push back slightly on the word 'mercy,' because I read that passage through a different lens entirely. Victorian marriage law recognises something very close to what Gissing describes — the idea that a husband's violence toward his wife is a kind of aberration of ownership rather than simple assault. When Gissing's narrator notes that he held her 'by the garment and not by the neck,' the precision is legal as much as moral. The law draws lines too — lines about what degree of violence is comprehensible, defensible, overlooked. I think Gissing knows exactly which line he's describing, and he's not simply condemning Widdowson — he's showing us the shape of the permitted and the impermissible.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
And here is where I want to connect Rhoda's trap to Monica's refusal, because I think they're actually the same structure. Rhoda's choices — marry with doubt, or let him go and be shattered — aren't choices at all, in any meaningful sense. The novel gives us, quite systematically, women who face not decisions but dilemmas — false alternatives constructed by a social and legal order that doesn't need to consider their interiority. Mary Barfoot's letter to Rhoda — reporting Widdowson's accusation, Monica's denial — is full of the same terrible grammar of the impossible. Widdowson says, quite flatly, 'My wife has left me' — as though Monica's departure is simply a fact of his life, an inconvenience to be reported rather than an act she has chosen and is paying for. That grammar — the passive woman, the aggrieved husband — is the law's grammar, not just one man's.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
I want to add something to what James has just said, because I think there's a literary-historical claim here that's worth making explicitly. Those gaps — why Monica won't go home, why Rhoda can't believe Everard — those aren't just psychological realism. They're the places where Gissing refuses to explain his characters, refuses to give the reader the comfortable resolution of motive — and that refusal is where modernism quietly enters the novel. James would say — and he's not wrong — those gaps are what happens when the law makes women's interiority irrelevant. But I'd say the gaps are also a technique — a narrative choice — and the two readings don't cancel each other out; they compound. Gissing uses the social fact as a narrative strategy, and that's the move that makes the novel feel, in 1893, like it's already living in a slightly later century.
