Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Right — and I want to resist the reading that hears that sentence as charming self-deprecation, because I think it sells Dickens short. The word 'whether' is doing enormous work. It's a conditional that refuses to resolve. The narrator is genuinely uncertain — looking back over his own life and not sure he controls its meaning. That is a radical act in 1850, when the bildungsroman — the novel of self-formation — was supposed to end with the hero confirmed in his identity and his place in the world. Dickens blows that up on the first page. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's a big claim and I stand by it — with one qualification, which is that plenty of writers have done the retrospective narrator brilliantly. Nabokov, Ishiguro. They're extraordinary. James Blackstone (social_historian)
He survives because the narrative selects for survival. Ham dies. Steerforth dies. Dora dies. Little Em'ly is exiled. Traddles — good, patient, decent Traddles — gets a modest domestic happiness and we almost miss it. What David gets is narration itself — the ability to make his survival mean something. And that is a class privilege as much as it is a psychological one. Ham cannot narrate. Em'ly cannot narrate. The novel is written by the person who got to write it. I don't say that to demolish it. I say it because I think that is actually the most interesting thing about it.
