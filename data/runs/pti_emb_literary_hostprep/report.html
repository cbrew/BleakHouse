Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
What Forster does in these first pages is something I find almost dizzying when I look at it technically. He's writing a landscape description, yes — but the landscape is simultaneously a theory of the novel. The sky controls everything, then suddenly — there's an interruption. And the interruption is announced in some of the most peculiar syntax in English fiction. Here's the line: "Only in the south, where a group of fists and fingers are thrust up through the soil, is the endless expanse interrupted. These fists and fingers are the Marabar Hills, containing the extraordinary caves." Now — fists and fingers thrust up through soil. That is not how a travel writer describes hills. That's a body below the earth, pushing upward — it's geological, yes, but it's also threatening, almost violent. And then Forster just flatly names them — 'These fists and fingers are the Marabar Hills' — as if we should have known all along. The contract he's signing with the reader is this: expect the uncanny wrapped in the matter-of-fact. Everything that happens in the caves later will feel prepared for — because it was.

James Blackstone (social_historian)
I've been listening to this exchange with great interest and some impatience. Because I think there's a danger — a very English literary-critical danger — of aestheticizing what is, at bottom, a political observation. The hills are not a narrative structure. They are geological fact — and the 'extraordinary caves' they contain are where the real violence of the novel will unfold. But the passage I want to bring to bear is from Chapter Ten — because I think Forster states his position there with a clarity we've been tiptoeing around. Here it is: "It matters so little to the majority of living beings what the minority, that calls itself human, desires or decides." Now — that is not mysticism. That is a legal and political statement delivered in the register of natural philosophy. 'The minority that calls itself human' — that phrase is doing enormous work. It relativizes the entire colonial enterprise. From the squirrel's perspective, the Raj and the independence movement are equally inconsequential. And Forster seems to find that — bracing.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
It's a claim that makes me nervous too, which is probably why I keep pressing it. Look at how Forster introduces McBryde. He's not the brute — he's the best-educated man in Chandrapore. He's read, he's thought, he receives Aziz with what the narrator calls courtesy — almost reassurance. And then — and this is the move — the narrator gives us his theory in free indirect discourse. James Blackstone (social_historian)
I'd grant the technique, Eleanor. I genuinely would. But I want to press on what it costs us to foreground form when the machinery is right there in the open. What Forster has given us in McBryde is something historically very specific: the educated colonizer who has weaponized knowledge. He's not Callendar, the blunt instrument.
