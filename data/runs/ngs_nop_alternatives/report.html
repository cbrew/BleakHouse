The New Grub Street: Ambition, Poverty, and the Literary Marketplace (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to New Grub Street Unpacked, a close reading of George Gissing's great novel of literary life, ambition, and slow ruin. Published in 1891, New Grub Street remains one of the most unflinching portraits of what it actually costs to write for a living. Not what the romantics promised — the garret, the inspiration, the triumph — but the grinding arithmetic of pages and pounds. To help us unpack this novel — its brilliance, its cruelty, its extraordinary relevance — I have three exceptional guests. Oliver Trevelyan is an actor and writer who has voiced more classic novels on audio than perhaps anyone alive. He comes to Gissing as a performer — hearing the rhythms, the comic timing, the moments written for the voice. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don whose lifelong conviction is that great fiction is fundamentally a moral art. He will keep us honest — and occasionally withering — about the difference between social critique and genuine imaginative literature. And Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads every novel as a document of class power — who gains, who suffers, and why. For this novel in particular, I suspect he'll have rather a lot to say. So — New Grub Street. The title itself is an act of deliberate provocation — the old Grub Street was London's notorious hive of hack writers, and Gissing is asking: what has changed? Oliver, as someone who has spent his life inhabiting the voices of literary characters — what kind of world does Gissing drop us into on page one?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The first thing that strikes you — and it strikes you immediately — is the sheer material weight of the world Gissing conjures. Edwin Reardon is introduced to us not in the act of writing but in the act of failing to write. He is staring at blank pages, counting how many he has managed, doing the dreadful arithmetic of whether he can finish the novel before the rent falls due. And what Gissing does — which is both cruel and completely honest — is make that arithmetic the drama. Not the romantic agony of the unfulfilled artist, but the genuinely rather squalid pressure of not having enough money. And then there is Jasper Milvain, introduced in the same early chapters — Reardon's great contrast. Jasper looks at the literary marketplace with the cheerful eyes of a man who has decided not to be ashamed of it. He is going to sell, and sell well, and he sees absolutely no reason to pretend otherwise.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What interests me about that opening — and Oliver has put his finger precisely on it — is that Gissing establishes the moral stakes through character, not through argument. Reardon and Milvain are not simply representatives of competing economic positions. They are two recognisable human types — the man who cannot compromise his sense of what is good, and the man who has decided that compromise is simply intelligence. Gissing is very candid, actually, about Milvain's charm. He does not make him a villain — he makes him rather likeable, which is the far more disturbing choice. Because the reader is in perpetual danger of agreeing with Jasper, even while watching what his philosophy produces in the world around him. Dr. Johnson said that no man but a blockhead ever wrote except for money — Milvain is simply Johnson's maxim given legs and a first-rate social manner.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to press on that word 'philosophy,' because I think Milvain's position isn't really a philosophy — it's a diagnosis. He has looked at the literary marketplace of the 1880s — the three-volume novel collapsing, the cheap periodicals expanding, the reading public being actively manufactured by mass education — and he has simply described what he sees. Very early in the novel, Milvain tells his sisters — quite breezily — that literature nowadays is a trade. He says something to the effect that your successful man of letters is not the inspired genius but the man who has learned the trick of the market and works it with industry. He is the old type of unpractical artist; I am the literary man of 1882. He won’t make concessions, or rather, he can’t make them; he can’t supply the market. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I would not entirely dispute that — but I think one ought to be careful about what 'rewarded' means in this novel. Milvain gets the career, yes. He gets Amy. He gets the income. But Gissing is not celebrating any of that. The novel ends on a note that I would describe as — exhausted lucidity. Milvain has won on the market's terms, and those terms are shown to be impoverished ones — aesthetically, humanly, imaginatively.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Can I just say something about the sheer pleasure of reading Milvain — because I think we're in danger of making him sound grim, and he isn't? He is genuinely funny. He is quick. He has the best lines. Gissing is a writer who gets far too little credit for his comic invention, and Milvain is the proof. Part of what makes the novel so devastating is that the man most adapted to his world is also the most alive to it. Poor Reardon is heroic and admirable and absolutely miserable — and Gissing doesn't let us look away from that.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And that's where I'd locate the real radicalism of the novel — not in any explicit politics, but in the character of Harold Biffen. Biffen is Reardon's friend — another struggling writer, poorer still, living in near-destitution — who is writing a novel of absolute, uncompromising realism. He calls it 'Mr. Bailey, Grocer' — a novel about commonplace people in which nothing extraordinary happens. No one will publish it. No one will read it. And Biffen knows this — and continues anyway. That is Gissing's most uncomfortable figure — not the man who compromises, not the man who struggles and is defeated, but the man who pursues pure artistic integrity straight off a cliff. The market doesn't punish Biffen. It simply ignores him. Which is, of course, worse.
Host (host)
Three men — Reardon, Milvain, Biffen — and between them they map virtually every position a writer can occupy in relation to the market. That structural ambition alone is remarkable — but what makes this novel devastating rather than merely clever is how Gissing embeds that structure in the daily texture of a life. And at the centre of that daily texture is Edwin Reardon — a man watching his talent, his marriage, and his health erode in almost unbearable slow motion. In our next segment, we'll look closely at exactly that process — the machinery of Reardon's decline, and how Gissing makes us feel every turn of the screw.

The Machinery of Decline: Reardon's Spiral into Obscurity (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been talking about how the literary marketplace in New Grub Street rewards calculation over art. Now I want to turn to the figure who pays the highest price for refusing that bargain — Edwin Reardon. His decline is the novel's great tragic engine, and it is worth examining how precisely Gissing constructs it.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me first about Reardon is how completely Gissing refuses him any romantic exemption from the grind. He is not a misunderstood genius rotting in a garret with beautiful contempt for the world. He is a man sitting in front of a blank page, counting the words he has written that day, and finding them wanting. Gissing gives us the actual arithmetic of literary failure — pages per day, shillings per thousand words. It is devastatingly undignified, and that is precisely the point.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think one must be careful not to reduce Reardon's tragedy to economic mechanism alone. What Gissing is really examining is a failure of will — and will is a moral category, not merely an economic one. Reardon knows, with some clarity, what he must do to survive — write the popular thing, compromise the artistic scruple. He cannot bring himself to do it, and Gissing is honest enough not to make that refusal entirely heroic. There is something of paralysis in Reardon — an inability to act that is almost Hamlet-like in its texture.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd push back on framing this as a failure of will, Sir Edmund, because that makes it a private drama. What Gissing shows us is how the market actively produces the conditions that make serious work impossible. Reardon is trapped in a two-room flat in Islington, writing against a deadline imposed by rent and a wife's expectations. The silence necessary for imaginative work has been colonised by anxiety — and anxiety is not a moral failing, it's a structural condition. Gissing himself lived this — he knew that poverty doesn't just make writing hard, it makes the mind itself jagged and unworkable.

His position was illogical—one result of the moral weakness which was allied with his aesthetic sensibility. What kills me about the Reardon-Amy scenes is the terrible politeness of their disintegration. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
One must also speak of Biffen in this context — because Biffen is Reardon's shadow, his alternative fate. Harold Biffen chooses poverty absolutely — he refuses the compromise, lives in conditions of genuine squalor, and writes his realistic novel about the utterly commonplace. And what does he get for it? The manuscript nearly burns in a lodging-house fire. The book is published and sinks without a trace. Gissing sets Biffen up as the purest version of the artistic ideal, and then destroys him with almost clinical efficiency. The novel is quite merciless on this point: integrity, pursued without compromise, leads not to vindication but to oblivion.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Biffen's novel — 'Mr Bailey, Grocer' — is one of the most extraordinary metafictional gestures in Victorian literature. A novel about the absolute banality of lower-middle-class life, written with complete artistic seriousness, published into indifference. Gissing is telling us something precise about what the market does: it doesn't only crush bad work — it crushes good work with equal efficiency. The market is not a meritocracy. It is an apparatus for producing what sells, full stop. And Biffen's gentle end — his suicide in the open air, thinking of Helen — is the novel's most devastating indictment of that apparatus.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Biffen's end is heartbreaking precisely because it is so undramatic — he slips away almost apologetically. And Reardon's death has the same quality — he dies of illness, of exhaustion really, back in poverty, having made his hopeless peace with Amy too late. Gissing gives his heroes no deathbed speeches, no final illuminations — just the body giving out under the accumulated weight of defeat. That is, in its own way, a kind of formal radicalism — Victorian novels are supposed to mean something at the end. Gissing insists that for men like Reardon and Biffen, things simply — stop.

Comic Desperation: Humor and Survival in the Hack's Life (discussion)

Host (host)
We've been tracing Reardon's tragic spiral — the way failure grinds a sensitive man down to nothing. But Gissing is not only writing tragedy — there is a dark, mordant comedy running through this novel that deserves its own reckoning. The hacks, the journalists, the penny-a-liners — they are desperate figures, and yet Gissing finds something grimly, brilliantly funny in their desperation.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
This is where I always want to grab people by the lapels and say: read the Whelpdale scenes. Whelpdale is a figure who fails at absolutely everything he attempts — and yet fails with such magnificent, undefended cheerfulness that you cannot help but adore him. He turns up at one point having conceived a new literary scheme — I believe it's his fourth or fifth — and he announces it with the full confidence of a man who has never once been chastened by experience. Gissing gives him this marvellous line about how the reading public doesn't want literature — it wants something to look at on the train. And you laugh — and then you realise that Whelpdale is absolutely right, and that's where the comedy curdles into something else entirely.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right to single out Whelpdale, though I'd frame him slightly differently. Whelpdale is not merely a comic figure — he is a study in a particular kind of resilience that Gissing treats with genuine ambivalence. The man simply refuses to be destroyed by failure — and in a novel full of men who are destroyed by it, that refusal is not trivial. Whether that resilience represents a kind of moral shallowness or a genuine adaptive virtue — Gissing leaves that question beautifully open. Think of how he contrasts with Reardon — who has all the sensitivity and none of the buoyancy.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to push back a little on the word 'resilience,' because I think it softens what Gissing is actually showing us. Whelpdale's cheerfulness isn't a character trait — it's an adaptation to a market that has no use for genuine literary feeling. The comedy in Whelpdale is the comedy of a man who has correctly read the conditions of his own exploitation and decided to profit from them rather than be crushed by them. His great project — Chit-Chat, the magazine aimed at people who can only concentrate for two minutes — is Gissing's most savage joke about the commodification of the written word. And the joke's punchline is that it succeeds.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That's the precise point where the novel's comedy becomes almost unbearable, isn't it. Gissing describes Chit-Chat as being designed so that nothing in it requires sustained attention — every item snappy, every article brief, every thought pre-digested. And Whelpdale delivers this vision with the enthusiasm of a man who genuinely believes he has solved the problem of literature. The comedy is farcical on the surface and absolutely merciless underneath. It's the kind of joke that leaves a mark.
Host (host)
And Whelpdale isn't the only source of this dark comedy — there's also the whole world of Jasper Milvain's circle, the journalism crowd, the conversations at the Milvain dinner table. Oliver, is there a particular scene or exchange that captures this comic texture for you?
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The conversations between Jasper and his sisters Dora and Maud are something I return to again and again. There's a marvellous early scene where Jasper is lecturing them on the proper attitude toward literary hackwork — quite without shame, quite without irony on his own part. He says something to the effect that the struggle for existence among writers is no different from the struggle among costermongers — only the commodity differs. And he means it as a liberating insight — as if recognising the market frees you from any obligation to feel bad about serving it. The comedy is that Jasper is perfectly correct in his analysis and perfectly hollow in his soul — and he doesn't notice either fact.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The sisters are essential to understanding what Gissing is doing with comedy in this novel. Dora and Maud are not merely bystanders to Jasper's brilliant cynicism — they are themselves aspiring writers grinding through the same machinery. Their situation has a specific comic poignancy — they are educated, they have genuine taste, and they are writing articles for women's magazines about household management. Gissing is far too honest to present this as merely degrading — there is something he respects in the way they simply get on with it. But the comedy of the gap between their capacities and their commissions is very precisely rendered.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The Milvain sisters embody something very specific about the gendered economy of literary labour in this period. Women writers in the 1880s had access to certain markets — the domestic, the sentimental, the instructional — and those markets paid less and were valued less. Gissing doesn't editorialise about this — he just shows it in the texture of their daily work, the rates they're offered, the subjects they're assigned. And yet these women survive — which is more than can be said for Reardon, who had the masculine privilege of being taken seriously and was destroyed by it.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That observation cuts deeply — and it connects to something I find genuinely comic and genuinely devastating in equal measure. There's the figure of Harold Biffen, who is writing what he calls his 'absolutely realistic' novel — a book about a man who is entirely ordinary, doing entirely ordinary things. Biffen describes his project with total seriousness — this will be the novel that captures true working-class life without sentimentality or condescension. And he is clearly right — it probably is a serious work of art. It also has absolutely no chance of being published, read, or noticed by anyone. Gissing gives Biffen a nobility that is comic in its utter impracticality — and tragic in its utter sincerity.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Biffen is one of the finest minor characters in late Victorian fiction, and he is consistently undervalued. His dedication to his novel — Mr. Bailey, Grocer, I believe — is presented with complete moral seriousness by Gissing. There's a scene where his manuscript is nearly destroyed in a fire — he saves it at great personal cost — and the reader is made to feel the full weight of that near-loss. The comedy is not at Biffen's expense — it is at the world's expense for failing to deserve a Biffen.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The Biffen fire episode is one of the moments where the comedy and the horror are absolutely inseparable. He lives in genuine poverty — near-starvation, an attic room, inadequate fuel — and the manuscript he's protecting represents years of labour that will never be compensated. Gissing frames it almost as a set piece — the hapless artist in his garret, the flames, the heroic rescue. But then he refuses to let you rest in the comedy — you remember what the manuscript is worth in market terms, which is nothing. The laughter catches in your throat.
Host (host)
That phrase captures something essential about Gissing's comic mode — the laughter that catches in the throat. He never lets the comedy be comfortable — there's always the economic reality pressing up against the joke and puncturing it. Which leads us, inevitably, to the one character in this novel who has made peace with that economic reality — who has turned it into a philosophy and a strategy. Jasper Milvain doesn't suffer from the literary market — he has mastered it, and next we ask whether that mastery is genius, cynicism, or something more unsettling still.

The novel's central cruelty is precisely that it gives its characters enough intelligence to understand their position — but not enough capital to change it. Edwin Reardon is the perfect case.
