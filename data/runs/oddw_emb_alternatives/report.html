A Woman's Right to Work: Setting the Stakes (opening)

Host (host)
Welcome to The Odd Women Unpacked, a close reading of George Gissing's fierce, funny, and quietly devastating novel of 1893. Over the course of this series, we're going to read the novel with the attention it deserves — pulling apart its arguments, its characters, its surprising comedy, and its moments of real anguish. Gissing published The Odd Women at a moment when the so-called Woman Question was not an abstraction but a daily crisis for hundreds of thousands of women without income, prospects, or protection. His title comes from the statistical surplus — more women than men in Victorian England — and he uses it to ask what those surplus lives are actually worth. Joining me today are three guests whose disagreements are at least as interesting as their agreements. Oliver Trevelyan has recorded more classic audiobooks than almost anyone alive, and he comes to Gissing as a performer first — listening for the rhythms, the comedy, the moments written for the voice. Sir Edmund Leigh is a retired Oxford don who reads novels as stories about individual character under moral pressure — and who will, I suspect, resist any reading that turns Gissing into a pamphleteer. And Dr. Daniel Rosen is a cultural historian who reads every novel as an anatomy of class power, and who believes the most radical novelists are often more radical than they themselves knew. Let's begin with the question the novel poses from almost its first pages: what is a woman's right to work, and who gets to define it?

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right that the line is well-placed. But I want to register a mild dissent about what we should make of it. Rhoda Nunn is a woman of powerful will and genuine intelligence. These are, in the novel, her great virtues. But there is a rigidity in her thinking — a refusal to allow the particular case to complicate the general principle — that Gissing observes with a slightly cooler eye than his admirers allow. Consider the quarrel with Mary Barfoot — this wonderful, crackling exchange where Rhoda refuses the emotional argument entirely. Now, Rhoda believes she is speaking on the side of reason. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I hear Edmund's point, but I think framing Rhoda's position as a limitation rather misses what Gissing is anatomising. The pressure on women in this novel to be emotionally accommodating — to make personal feeling the supreme criterion — is itself a form of social control. When Rhoda insists that movements for change cannot be governed by personal attachment, she is refusing a trap — the trap of sentimentality that keeps women in their place. And then there's the passage where she articulates the affirmative case — not just what she refuses, but what she is building. That phrase — weariness and lamentation — is exactly the script that Victorian ideology handed to single women. Rhoda is refusing to perform that script. And Gissing, I'd argue, is fully on her side in that refusal.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The language of reserves and substitutes is interesting precisely because it is impersonal. These are women as a category, not as individuals. And that, I think, is where one ought to pause. Gissing is a great novelist partly because he notices the cost of programmes as well as their necessity. Rhoda's formulation — train the reserve — has a bracing clarity. But it also, if one listens carefully, treats individual women as instruments of a larger design. The question the novel will go on to explore — with some discomfort — is whether Rhoda herself can survive the logic of her own position.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund raises a real tension, and I don't want to simply dissolve it. The novel does hold Rhoda to account for her abstractions. But I'd insist that the structural conditions producing those abstractions — the sheer material desperation of women without income or training — are the prior fact. Rhoda's project isn't ideology for its own sake. It's a response to a system that has already reduced women to instruments — of marriage, of domestic labour, of male convenience. What Gissing gives us, uniquely, is a heroine who has diagnosed that system and is attempting — however imperfectly — to build something outside it.

The Poppleton scene is brilliant, but I'd argue it functions as a kind of warning Gissing places in Barfoot's mouth.
