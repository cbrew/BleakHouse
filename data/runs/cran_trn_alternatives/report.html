The thing that strikes you immediately, reading Cranford aloud, is the precision of the comedy. Gaskell is not merely amused by her characters — she is delighted by them in the way a great comic writer is delighted — that is, with complete seriousness. There is a supper scene — Miss Barker entertaining the Cranford ladies — and the narrator, Mary Smith, watches a second tray arrive. Oysters, potted lobsters, jelly — and a dish called, magnificently, 'little Cupids.' And Mary's inward cry is simply this: "Oh, gentility! can you endure this last shock?" That exclamation mark, that direct address to an abstraction — Gaskell is doing something very precise there. Gentility is being treated as a person — a nervous, fragile person who might faint at the sight of a macaroon sopped in brandy.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right that the comedy is serious, but I think what Gaskell is doing is something more tender than satirical. She is not mocking these women for their genteel pretensions — she understands why those pretensions exist and what they cost. The real achievement of Cranford is that Gaskell holds comedy and pathos in a single, steady hand without ever losing her grip on either. And that balance is thrown into sharpest relief when tragedy strikes without warning. Miss Matty speaks of calamity with an image so plain it takes your breath away. "Oh! it was an awful time; coming down like a thunder-bolt on the still sunny day when the lilacs were all in bloom." That is Miss Matty's whole character in a sentence — the disaster rendered not in abstract anguish but in the precise sensory detail of a spring garden. She remembers the lilacs because that is how grief actually works — and Gaskell knows it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
What both of you are describing — the comedy, the pathos — are real, but they operate within a very specific social enclosure. Cranford is a town defined by the management of anxiety — anxiety about money, about status, about the threat of disorder from outside. Gaskell makes that anxiety almost literally physical in the nightly security ritual at Miss Matty's house. Listen to the procession: "Miss Matty leading the way, armed with the poker, I following with the hearth-brush, and Martha carrying the shovel and fire-irons with which to sound the alarm." Three women of a household, each carrying a domestic tool repurposed as a weapon — the hearth-brush, the poker, the shovel. It is funny, yes — but what it shows is a community with no access to any form of protection except the improvised and the absurd. These women are not whimsical — they are genuinely afraid, and the institutions that might protect them simply do not reach them.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
And it's worth starting with the economic philosophy that holds this world in place — because Cranford has a very particular way of making poverty acceptable. The narrator gives us the key to the whole edifice right near the opening. "'Elegant economy!' How naturally one falls back into the phraseology of Cranford!" There, economy was always 'elegant,' and money-spending always 'vulgar and ostentatious' — a sort of sour-grapeism which made them very peaceful and satisfied. That phrase — 'sour-grapeism' — is doing enormous ideological work. The women of Cranford have not chosen genteel poverty — it has been assigned to them by a society that offers them almost no economic agency. And the genius of the system — its real cruelty — is that they have internalised its values so thoroughly they call their deprivation elegant.
