Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
David Copperfield is the boy who feels everything at maximum volume and then has to learn, very slowly, to trust those feelings. He is also, crucially, a boy who tells stories about himself — sometimes before he quite understands what has happened to him. What strikes me every time I work with this text is the moment in chapter thirteen where Dickens drops the comedy entirely. David is in the blacking warehouse, and the prose changes register completely. "No words can express the secret agony of my soul as I sunk into this companionship; compared these henceforth everyday associates with those of my happier childhood — not to say with Steerforth, Traddles, and the rest of those boys; and felt my hopes of growing up to be a learned and distinguished man, crushed in my bosom." That word "crushed" — in the bosom, physically — that is Dickens the performer knowing exactly where to land the blow. And then, because this is Dickens and he cannot resist the specific image, David mingling his tears with the bottle-washing water. Grief, labour, and a kind of darkly comic precision — all in the same breath.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to isolate that passage, and I'd add that what makes it remarkable is the retrospective voice. The adult David is not simply remembering — he is puzzling over the relationship between the boy he was and the man who is writing. There is a passage later in the same chapter that I find, in some ways, even more penetrating. "When my thoughts go back, now, to that slow agony of my youth, I wonder how much of the histories I invented for such people hangs like a mist of fancy over well-remembered facts!" A mist of fancy over well-remembered facts — that is a philosophical admission of the first order. Dickens is telling us, with complete candour, that his narrator cannot fully separate what happened from what he imagined about what happened. That is not weakness — that is the condition of all serious self-knowledge.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto something in that phrase — "sunk into this companionship." David doesn't say the work is hard, or even that it's humiliating — he says it is the company that destroys him. Mick Walker and the other warehouse boys are not villains — they are simply working-class children. And David's horror at being counted among them tells us something Dickens perhaps did not intend to say quite so clearly. What is being crushed in his bosom is not merely aspiration — it is class identity. The novel is honest enough to show us that David's suffering is partly constituted by snobbery — and radical enough, I'd argue, to let that stand without resolving it.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
That passage does something very specific about power — the Murdstones don't merely instruct, they occupy the lesson. Dickens tells us they used these lessons as a favourable occasion for giving his mother lessons in what he calls — with superb irony — "miscalled firmness." The child's education is merely the medium through which the mother is broken. That structure — authority performing itself upon the bodies of women and children — is what Dickens renders with absolute fidelity. He doesn't name it as a system, but he shows us the system working — lesson by grinding lesson.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me first, reading these early chapters with any care, is how precisely Dickens anatomises the moment when cruelty becomes policy. Murdstone does not merely dislike the boy — dislike is human, almost forgivable. What Murdstone does is something colder: he works to extinguish the very idea that David has a claim on him. Dickens gives us the sentence with devastating economy. "He could not bear me; and in putting me from him he tried, as I believe, to put away the notion that I had any claim upon him — and succeeded." That final word — 'succeeded' — sits there like a stone. No rage, no protest from the narrator — just the bald acknowledgement of a defeat that has already happened.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
There's a companion passage, slightly earlier, that I find even more unbearable to read — and I use that word with intention. It is the passage where adult David reflects on what a single kind word from Murdstone might have accomplished. "God help me, I might have been improved for my whole life, I might have been made another creature perhaps, for life, by a kind word at that season." There's another scene at Salem House that I think the novel almost passes over too quickly — and it's doing something quite devastating. Steerforth is giving David the lay of the land — school gossip, who's who. And the shame runs in two directions simultaneously — which is what makes the passage so acute. He's ashamed that he doesn't know the games and the books — that he's been put into the lowest form.
