[Edmund Leigh]

### Gwendolen's Reckoning: Choice and Consequence (close_reading)

[Host]
  We've been tracking the machinery that constrains characters in this novel — the class structures, the marriage market, the invisible walls of respectable life.  (+600ms @0.98x *machinery,constrains*)
  But no character feels those walls more acutely — or tests them more dangerously — than Gwendolen Harleth.  (<800ms> @0.97x *Gwendolen,dangerously*)
  What I want to explore now is Gwendolen's moral reckoning — the choices she makes, what she understands about them, and what the novel refuses to let her forget.  (<1200ms> @0.98x *reckoning,refuses*)

[Oliver Trevelyan]
  The great thing about Gwendolen — the thing that makes her irresistible to read aloud — is that she begins the novel absolutely convinced she is the heroine of her own magnificent story.  (+220ms @1.02x *irresistible,magnificent*)
  She's beautiful, she's sharp, she's accustomed to being the centre of every room she enters.  (@1.02x *centre*)
  And then Eliot does something rather devastating.  (@0.97x *devastating*)
  She opens the entire novel with Deronda watching Gwendolen at the roulette table in Leubronn — watching her, studying her, before she even knows who he is.  (*watching,before*)
  The heroine of her own story is immediately made an object of another's scrutiny.  (@0.97x *object,scrutiny*)
  And she feels it — that's the wonderful thing — she feels his gaze like a cold hand on the back of her neck.  (<800ms> @1.02x *feels,gaze*)
  Eliot gives us the question right there in the epigraph to the first chapter: 'Was she beautiful or not beautiful?'  (@0.98x [setup])
  > 'Was she beautiful or not beautiful? and what was the secret of form or expression which gave the dynamic quality to her glance?'  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *dynamic,glance*)
  Even the narrator can't quite pin her down — and that ambivalence never quite resolves itself, does it?  (<1500ms> [commentary] *ambivalence*)

[Edmund Leigh]
  What Eliot is doing with Gwendolen, I think, is something classically moral in the best sense — she is charting the education of a soul.  (+240ms @0.94x *education,soul*)
  Gwendolen begins as a creature almost entirely of self-will — what the older moralists would have called pride, or perhaps more precisely, a kind of spiritual indolence dressed up as vitality.  (@0.94x *self-will,indolence*)
  She recoils from servitude, from dependence, from anything that diminishes her sense of her own sovereignty.  (@0.93x *sovereignty,recoils*)
  And yet the novel's great moral argument is that sovereignty of that kind — sovereignty without responsibility — is not freedom at all.  (@0.93x *sovereignty,freedom*)
  It is, if anything, the most abject form of bondage.  (@0.92x *bondage*)
  Her marriage to Grandcourt is the proof of that.  (@0.94x *proof*)
  She marries him believing she can manage him — believing her wit and her beauty are sufficient instruments of power.  (@0.94x *manage,instruments*)
  And Grandcourt — cold, deliberate, remorseless — proceeds to dismantle that illusion with perfect patience.  (<1500ms> @0.92x *dismantle,patience*)

[Daniel Rosen]
  I'd want to press on something Edmund said — because the language of soul education can obscure what is actually happening to Gwendolen at the material level.  (+220ms @0.99x *material,obscure*)
  Her family loses its money.  (@0.99x *loses,money*)

  The moment Gwendolen's financial position collapses, the entire structure of her autonomy collapses with it.  (@0.99x *collapses,autonomy*)
  Grandcourt is not merely a cruel husband — he is the embodiment of the property system.  (*property,embodiment*)
  He owns her, legally and psychologically, and he knows it with a kind of reptilian satisfaction.  (@0.99x *owns,reptilian*)
  Eliot gives us the line about Grandcourt's will being 'like a torpedo's touch' — absolute, numbing, inescapable.  (@0.98x [setup])
  > 'What she would do must be what he willed.'  (+150ms @0.92x [reading] *willed*)

### Narrative Games and the Unreliable Self (discussion)
