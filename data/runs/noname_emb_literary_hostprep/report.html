Not quite impatience — more a historian's instinct to ask what's prior. Eleanor and Caroline are reading Magdalen's state of mind — and they're both right about what they see. That repetition is the key to everything, I think. Collins writes that phrase — 'Nobody's Children' — four times across the novel. The six-book system is one of the great comic set pieces in Victorian fiction — and its comedy is entirely structural. The accumulation — district by district, victim by victim — is itself the joke, but it's also a genuine system. Listen to this catalogue: "Military District, Clerical District, Agricultural District... Family of an officer who fell at Waterloo; Wife of a poor curate stricken down by nervous debility; Widow of a grazier in difficulties gored to death by a mad bull." The escalation of that list — from Waterloo to a mad bull — is pure comic architecture. But notice: Wragge's system has Districts, Cases, Responses, Handwritings, Subscriptions, a Diary. That is — in structure — exactly how a serialised Victorian novel is built. Mapped territories, character types to appeal to, responses catalogued, forged emotional documents designed to make readers weep. Collins is writing the anatomy of his own craft — and inviting us to notice.
