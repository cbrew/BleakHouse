I don't disagree about the intensity of that passage — it is extraordinary writing. Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Can I come in here, because I think James is absolutely right about the material reality, and Caroline is absolutely right about the spiritual force — and the reason both are true is a technical one. Gissing is doing something in that passage that is, structurally speaking, years ahead of his moment. He starts with a physical fact — the fog penetrating the house — and then he makes a grammatical pivot. He moves from third-person observation to something much closer to free indirect experience — we're suddenly inside the sensation, not watching it. The sentence 'it could be smelt and tasted' — that passive construction is doing the work of erasing the boundary between narrator and character. By the time we reach 'the very reek of the bottomless pit,' we are not being told that Marian feels this. We are feeling it. That is what I mean when I say Gissing was solving a modernist problem before modernism had a name for it.
