Welcome to Middlemarch Unpacked — a close reading of George Eliot's great novel, one of the most ambitious works in the English language. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The moment you open Middlemarch you understand that you're in the hands of someone operating at a different altitude entirely. The Prelude is barely a page — and yet it tells you everything about what the next eight hundred pages intend to do. Eliot introduces St. Theresa of Ávila — a woman born to epic life, who found her epic. And then she turns the knife — she speaks of other Theresas, later-born, who found no coherent social faith and no epic life waiting for them. What a way to announce a novel. You are not simply about to read a story — you are about to witness something that history failed to make room for.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think what makes the Prelude so extraordinary — and Oliver is right to single it out — is that Eliot immediately frames greatness as a problem of circumstance rather than of character. And yet — and this is the tension the whole novel lives inside — she never quite abandons the idea that character determines fate. Dorothea Brooke is not simply a victim of a provincial society that cannot accommodate her. She is also, crucially, a woman whose idealism is imperfectly understood by herself. Eliot sets up that double burden from the first chapter — Dorothea's fervour burning without the object that would dignify it. That is not merely a social critique. That is a moral portrait — one of the finest in all of English fiction.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund frames it as a moral portrait, and I don't entirely disagree — but I think we have to ask: moral portrait set against what backdrop, and who painted the frame? The Prelude is doing something quite specific: it is describing the waste of human capacity — particularly female capacity — under conditions that are historical and material, not accidental. Eliot calls it a 'history of man' — but she means it as an indictment. These later-born Theresas are not unfortunate exceptions — they are the rule under a system that channels women's energy into ornamentation and domestic subordination. By the time we reach Middlemarch the town itself — its gossip, its property relations, its professional hierarchies — is the mechanism of that waste.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me, performing this — and I mean literally sitting in a booth and reading it aloud for an audience — is how much Eliot trusts silence. The opening description of Dorothea and her sister Celia — it's ostensibly about how they dress. But underneath it is a complete social taxonomy — the Puritanism, the beauty that refuses to be ornamental, the jewels that get locked away. And then Eliot gives you a sentence — and here is one that I have read aloud probably forty times and it still lands like a stone dropped into still water: 'Her full nature... spent itself in channels which had no great name on the earth.' Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver puts his finger on something essential there. The novel's structure is indeed elegiac from the outset — but elegiac for what, exactly? I think Eliot mourns not merely the social conditions but the peculiarly human tragedy of misdirected ardour. Dorothea in the opening chapters is almost wilfully blind to what she actually is — which is young, beautiful, passionate, and entirely unprepared for the world as it operates. She is the kind of character who reads Milton for comfort and mistakes an ageing, pedantic scholar for an intellectual cathedral. That is not purely the fault of society. That is a failure of self-knowledge, which is a perennial human failing — and the proper subject of great fiction.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'll grant Edmund that self-knowledge matters — but the question is: what are the conditions under which self-knowledge is even possible? Dorothea has had no education worthy of the name. She's been trained to ornament a drawing room and manage a household — not to exercise the kind of critical judgment that might have saved her from Casaubon. And Casaubon himself is a figure worth examining — he is not merely a personal failure; he is what the learned professions look like when they're entirely insulated from accountability. Middlemarch gives us, right at the outset, two people whose lives will be determined by the institutions — marriage, property, church — that neither of them has chosen with clear eyes.
Host (host)
Three very different entry points into the same novel, and already we can feel the tension between them — which is precisely the tension Eliot herself holds without resolving. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund is right that Eliot is unflinching — but I think we need to ask what structures make that particular blindness not just possible but almost inevitable. Dorothea has a great deal of money and no legitimate sphere in which to exercise her intelligence. The novel explicitly compares her to a great man's daughter who, in an earlier century, might have retreated into a convent and found there a genuine intellectual vocation. In Middlemarch in 1830, that option is gone — what remains is marriage or marginality. So when Dorothea marries Casaubon, she is not simply making a naive mistake — she is doing what the system permits. She intends to assist with his Key to All Mythologies — she will be his secretary, his research assistant, his amanuensis. That is the only form of intellectual work available to a woman of her class and her era.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And Rome — we must talk about Rome. The honeymoon is one of the great passages in the English novel — this young woman arriving in the Eternal City and utterly failing to be transported by it. She had expected revelation and found instead ruined stonework and a husband who retires to the Vatican Library and copies notes. Eliot writes — and this is one of her most astonishing sentences — that Dorothea felt a "stupendous fragmentariness" about Rome that mirrored her own inner collapse. "To those who have looked at Rome with the quickening power of a knowledge which breathes a growing soul into all historic shapes, and traces out the suppressed transitions which unite all contrasts, Rome may still be the spiritual centre and interpreter of the world." To those who have looked at Rome with the quickening power of a knowledge which breathes a growing soul into all historic shapes, and traces out the suppressed transitions which unite all contrasts, Rome may still be the spiritual centre and interpreter of the world. But let them conceive one more historical contrast: the gigantic broken revelations of that Imperial and Papal city thrust abruptly on the notions of a girl who had been brought up in English and Swiss Puritanism, fed on meagre Protestant histories and on art chiefly of the hand-screen sort; a girl whose ardent nature turned all her small allowance of knowledge into principles, fusing her actions into their mould, and whose quick emotions gave the most abstract things the quality of a pleasure or a pain; a girl who had lately become a wife, and from the enthusiastic acceptance of untried duty found herself plunged in tumultuous preoccupation with her personal lot. The weight of unintelligible Rome might lie easily on bright nymphs to whom it formed a background for the brilliant picnic of Anglo-foreign society; but Dorothea had no such defence against deep impressions. Ruins and basilicas, palaces and colossi, set in the midst of a sordid present, where all that was living and warm-blooded seemed sunk in the deep degeneracy of a superstition divorced from reverence; the dimmer but yet eager Titanic life gazing and struggling on walls and ceilings; the long vistas of white forms whose marble eyes seemed to hold the monotonous light of an alien world: all this vast wreck of ambitious ideals, sensuous and spiritual, mixed confusedly with the signs of breathing forgetfulness and degradation, at first jarred her as with an electric shock, and then urged themselves on her with that ache belonging to a glut of confused ideas which check the flow of emotion. Forms both pale and glowing took possession of her young sense, and fixed themselves in her memory even when she was not thinking of them, preparing strange associations which remained through her after-years. Our moods are apt to bring with them images which succeed each other like the magic-lantern pictures of a doze; and in certain states of dull forlornness Dorothea all her life continued to see the vastness of St. Peter’s, the huge bronze canopy, the excited intention in the attitudes and garments of the prophets and evangelists in the mosaics above, and the red drapery which was being hung for Christmas spreading itself everywhere like a disease of the retina.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver's reading of Rome is exactly right, and it connects to something I find extraordinarily moving in Eliot's treatment of Casaubon. He is, for much of the novel's early reception, simply the villain of the piece — the arid pedant who crushes a young woman's spirit. But Eliot will not allow us that comfortable verdict. She gives us the interior of Casaubon's consciousness — and it is a consciousness in genuine torment. There is that chapter — chapter twenty-nine I believe — where she enters Casaubon's point of view and shows us a man who has spent his life building a monument and now suspects, with horrible clarity, that it is built on sand. Eliot writes of his "proud narrow sensitiveness" — a phrase that contains a whole psychology. "He had not had much foretaste of happiness in his previous life, and if it had been asked of him before his marriage whether he expected to be happy, he would probably have replied that he had no such expectation." Chapter 30
He had not had much foretaste of happiness in his previous life. To know intense joy without a strong bodily frame, one must have an enthusiastic soul. Mr. Casaubon had never had a strong bodily frame, and his soul was sensitive without being enthusiastic: it was too languid to thrill out of self-consciousness into passionate delight; it went on fluttering in the swampy ground where it was hatched, thinking of its wings and never flying. His experience was of that pitiable kind which shrinks from pity, and fears most of all that it should be known: it was that proud narrow sensitiveness which has not mass enough to spare for transformation into sympathy, and quivers thread-like in small currents of self-preoccupation or at best of an egoistic scrupulosity. And Mr. Casaubon had many scruples: he was capable of a severe self-restraint; he was resolute in being a man of honor according to the code; he would be unimpeachable by any recognized opinion. In conduct these ends had been attained; but the difficulty of making his Key to all Mythologies unimpeachable weighed like lead upon his mind; and the pamphlets—or “Parerga” as he called them—by which he tested his public and deposited small monumental records of his march, were far from having been seen in all their significance. He suspected the Archdeacon of not having read them; he was in painful doubt as to what was really thought of them by the leading minds of Brasenose, and bitterly convinced that his old acquaintance Carp had been the writer of that depreciatory recension which was kept locked in a small drawer of Mr. Casaubon’s desk, and also in a dark closet of his verbal memory. These were heavy impressions to struggle against, and brought that melancholy embitterment which is the consequence of all excessive claim: even his religious faith wavered with his wavering trust in his own authorship, and the consolations of the Christian hope in immortality seemed to lean on the immortality of the still unwritten Key to all Mythologies. For my part I am very sorry for him. It is an uneasy lot at best, to be what we call highly taught and yet not to enjoy: to be present at this great spectacle of life and never to be liberated from a small hungry shivering self—never to be fully possessed by the glory we behold, never to have our consciousness rapturously transformed into the vividness of a thought, the ardor of a passion, the energy of an action, but always to be scholarly and uninspired, ambitious and timid, scrupulous and dim-sighted. Becoming a dean or even a bishop would make little difference, I fear, to Mr. Casaubon’s uneasiness. Doubtless some ancient Greek has observed that behind the big mask and the speaking-trumpet, there must always be our poor little eyes peeping as usual and our timorous lips more or less under anxious control.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I don't want to be the one who resists sympathy for Casaubon entirely — Edmund is pointing to something real. But let's be clear about the power relation operating in that marriage. Casaubon controls Dorothea's income, her movements, her time, and ultimately — through the codicil to his will — her future choices. That codicil — the provision that Dorothea will lose her inheritance if she marries Ladislaw — is not just a petty act of jealousy. It is a dead man reaching from the grave to continue the project of ownership that defined their marriage. And Eliot shows us Dorothea sitting with that document and choosing, finally, to give up the money. That choice is the moral centre of the novel — but we should not romanticise it without noticing what it costs her materially.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
And then there's Ladislaw — poor, glittering, infuriating Will Ladislaw. He is, I think, the one place where Eliot's usually flawless control slightly slips — she is too fond of him, and we feel it. He functions in the novel partly as a foil — the spontaneous, European, aesthetically alive man against Casaubon's dusty English scholasticism. But the extraordinary thing is what happens to Dorothea when she chooses him. She does not become free — she becomes the wife of a junior politician and disappears from history. The Finale tells us that many who knew her thought it a pity — and Eliot will not entirely disagree. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver raises the question of Ladislaw with characteristic elegance, and I shall be correspondingly direct. The complaint that Ladislaw is inadequate to Dorothea is almost as old as the novel itself — Henry James made it. But I think that misses the point — and misses it rather badly. Dorothea does not need Ladislaw to be a great man — she has had a great man, or a man who wore greatness as a kind of armour, and it nearly destroyed her. What she needs — what the novel quietly argues she deserves — is a man capable of genuine feeling, of being changed by her. And Ladislaw, whatever his limitations, is at least that.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund makes the moral case for Ladislaw, and I accept it up to a point. But there's a structural irony that I think the novel itself is aware of. Dorothea's idealism — her desire to do something large for the world — survives Casaubon, survives Rome, survives the codicil. It is ultimately resolved not through action but through renunciation — she gives up the money, she gives up the ambition, she gives up the possibility of a historically legible life. And Eliot is honest enough to call that both noble and a waste. The novel's most radical gesture is refusing to resolve that tension — refusing to say it was worth it, while refusing to say it was not.

A Society Built on Secrets (deep_dive)

Host (host)
We've been dwelling on Dorothea's idealism and the way Middlemarch slowly, painfully, corrects it. But idealism doesn't operate in a vacuum — it collides, again and again, with a society that runs on concealment. Middlemarch is a town built on secrets — financial, sexual, moral — and George Eliot tracks how those secrets move through a community like a slow poison. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
I want to begin with Bulstrode, because Bulstrode is the engine of the whole thing. He is a man who has built an entire identity — banker, philanthropist, pillar of the Evangelical community — on a buried crime. And what Eliot understands so brilliantly is that the crime isn't the most interesting thing about him. The most interesting thing is how he has learned to pray in the language of Providence so fluently that he has half-convinced himself. Eliot gives us this extraordinary passage where she describes how Bulstrode's religion served as a kind of screen between himself and his own past. She writes that he had notions of his own about what Providence was doing, and that his prayers were a kind of double-entry bookkeeping with God. That is comic, yes — but it is also genuinely frightening.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right to point to the self-deception, and I think Eliot is doing something more precise than social satire there. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to press on something Edmund has raised, because I think the Bulstrode plot is more than a character study in self-deception. It is a study in how capital accumulation requires moral concealment as a structural feature, not an individual failing. Bulstrode's original crime — cheating Will Ladislaw's grandmother out of her inheritance through the pawnbroking business — that is not an aberration. That is how fortunes are made in Middlemarch, and in Victorian England generally. The secret isn't some private moral stain — it's the dirty foundation that all the respectable architecture of the town is resting on. And Raffles — the blackmailer who holds the secret — is not a villain in any conventional sense. He is the return of the repressed — the material reality that bourgeois respectability requires to be permanently buried.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Raffles — and I say this with great affection — is one of the great minor grotesques of English fiction. He arrives in Middlemarch like a bad smell that keeps coming back no matter how many times you air the room. And Eliot is wonderfully precise about his manner — that terrible, leering familiarity he has with Bulstrode. He calls him 'Nick' — Nicholas — and that single syllable strips away thirty years of constructed dignity in an instant. From a purely theatrical standpoint, that is genius — the secret isn't revealed in a speech, it's revealed in a name.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Gossip in Middlemarch is not merely a comic social habit — it is the novel's primary instrument of justice, and of injustice. The town does not need a court to destroy a reputation — it needs only a drawing room and an afternoon. Think of the scene where news of Bulstrode's connection to Raffles begins to circulate — Eliot shows us how the mere association of Lydgate with Bulstrode is enough to taint him. Lydgate has done nothing criminal — he has accepted a loan, and perhaps been negligent with Raffles — but rumour does not distinguish between guilt and proximity. Eliot writes, in that magisterial way she has, that public opinion is always more ready to believe a man guilty than innocent when his name is coupled with a scandal. The destruction of Lydgate's medical career — which is really what follows — is accomplished entirely by whisper and inference. And what I find most painful is that the town is not entirely wrong to be suspicious — it simply cannot know enough to be right.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund has put his finger on something I'd push a little further. The gossip isn't simply a failure of epistemology — not knowing enough to judge correctly. The gossip is structured by class interest. The Middlemarch establishment — Hawley, Chettam, the old Tory interest — they want Lydgate gone because he represents a new kind of professional, beholden to evidence rather than connection. The scandal around Bulstrode gives them the weapon they needed. And look at Harriet Bulstrode — her husband's secret falls on her, though she knew nothing of it. Eliot gives her one of the most devastating scenes in the novel — that moment when she goes upstairs, removes her ornaments, puts on plain clothing, and goes down to take her stand beside her husband. She has chosen to share his shame without sharing his guilt — and that is an act of extraordinary moral dignity that the town will never credit her with.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That scene with Harriet is — I've read it aloud perhaps a dozen times across various productions — and it never fails to stop the room. What Eliot does is something a less courageous novelist would never attempt — she makes the guilty party's wife the moral centre of the scene. And the genius is that Harriet says almost nothing — it's the gesture, the changed dress, the coming downstairs. Eliot writes that Bulstrode felt her presence as something holy — and I think that's exactly right as a reader's experience too. In a novel built on secrets and self-interest and the corrupting logic of concealment, here is one person simply, quietly, doing the right thing. No audience, no applause, no record in the history books — which is, of course, exactly the point Eliot has been building to all along.

Wit and the Machinery of Middlemarch (discussion)

Host (host)
We've been exploring the ironies embedded in Lydgate's ambition — the gap between what he believes himself to be and what the novel quietly reveals. That gap, of course, is precisely where George Eliot's wit lives — and wit is what I'd like us to turn to now.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
What strikes me every time I come back to Middlemarch — and I've read it aloud more times than I can honestly count — is how much of the wit is structural. It's not jokes in the margin. It's the whole machinery of how chapters are arranged, how one character's delusion is immediately followed by another's. Think of Mr. Brooke. The man is a walking comic instrument — he begins every sentence with tremendous confidence and loses the thread before he reaches the verb. Eliot gives him a line I've always adored — something to the effect that he had gone into things at one time, into documents, but that he had not gone into them deeply enough to carry them further. The comedy is in the syntax itself. The sentence performs his incompleteness. That is what I mean when I say the wit is structural — the form of the sentence is the joke.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is quite right about Brooke, and I think the point deserves extending. Brooke is not merely a figure of fun. He is a portrait of a particular English failure — the man of goodwill and no conviction, who mistakes dilettantism for liberality. Eliot is perfectly courteous to him on the surface — and devastating underneath. That is her characteristic mode. I think of the narrator's remark on Casaubon's prose — that it had that kind of ponderous, oracular obscurity which is the stamp of minds formed in an age when the classics were the only gymnastics. One could say something to that effect — the wit is so perfectly calibrated it scarcely announces itself. It is wit in the service of moral characterisation — not wit for its own diversion. That, I would suggest, is what separates Eliot from the merely clever. She earns every laugh.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to agree with both of you, and then push somewhere neither of you has gone quite yet. The wit in Middlemarch is not only moral characterisation — it is also a form of social analysis. Eliot is mapping power through comedy. Take Bulstrode. He is not comic in the way Brooke is comic — there's no warmth in the laughter — but there is a precise, cold wit in the way Eliot anatomises his self-deception. The narrator observes that he was simply a man whose desires had been stronger than his theoretic beliefs, and who had gradually explained the gratification of his desires into satisfactory agreement with those beliefs. That is — the sentence is almost cold in its precision — the mechanism by which a pious fraudster operates. And it is also, if you look at it steadily, a description of how an entire class operates — justifying accumulation through the language of Providence. The wit is doing sociological work. It is not decorative.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel, I think you've put your finger on something — but I'd add that the comedy also works against the narrator herself. There are moments where the wit turns reflexive — where Eliot is laughing at the very enterprise of the omniscient novel. The famous web image — the notion that the narrator can arrange a pier-glass and candle and show how every scratch on the glass seems to form concentric circles around the light. And then she says — quite magnificently — that the scratches are going everywhere impartially, and it is only your candle that creates the illusion of order. She is undercutting the novelist's own authority. With a smile, but undercutting it nonetheless. That takes real nerve. And it takes a very particular kind of wit — the kind that is comfortable enough with its own intelligence to mock it.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver's point about reflexivity is well taken, and I'd connect it to something that I think gets underweighted in discussions of Eliot's comedy. The wit is also compassionate. It never forgets that these figures it is dissecting are genuinely suffering, or will suffer. Think of poor Casaubon — a figure of almost pure comedy in the early chapters, this desiccated scholar with his Key to All Mythologies. And yet Eliot — with one of the most arresting pivots in English fiction — takes us inside his consciousness and shows us his fear. She writes that he lived in a bitter loneliness — that his experience had been of the kind that makes a man shrink from pity. The joke is over. And you feel slightly ashamed of having laughed. That moral management of laughter — knowing when to withdraw the comedy — is, I submit, the highest form of novelistic wit.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
Edmund, I won't disagree with the compassion — but I want to register one caveat. There is a class dimension even to the comedy's targets. The wit falls hardest on men like Brooke and Casaubon — men of property and pretension. It is considerably gentler on Caleb Garth — who is working, who has no pretensions, who simply does things competently and well. Eliot clearly loves Caleb Garth. And Caleb's relationship to work — his near-religious devotion to it — is treated with a warmth she withholds from almost everyone else. She writes that for Caleb, business meant the skilful application of labour — and that he spoke of it with the fervour of a man who has found his vocation. No irony there. No undercutting. Just admiration — direct, unguarded admiration. I think that tells us something important about where Eliot's sympathies actually lie beneath the wit.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Caleb Garth is one of those characters who proves the rule about great novelists. He should be sentimental — the good plain man who loves honest work — and in lesser hands he absolutely would be. But Eliot gives him enough specificity — the way he mutters to himself over his plans, his particular vocabulary for praising things — that he becomes entirely believable. The wit in those scenes is different — it's affectionate, almost domestic. She is gently teasing someone she loves. And that range — from the cold surgical wit she applies to Bulstrode, to this warm domestic comedy around Caleb — that range is what makes the novel feel like a world rather than a set of satirical sketches.

The Art of Eliot's Judgment (close_reading)

Host (host)
We've been talking about the machinery of the novel — the wit, the architecture, the comic timing. But I want to turn now to something even more distinctive about Eliot: the quality of her moral judgment. Because she judges her characters — sometimes severely — and yet the reader never feels the author's thumb on the scale.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
That's the conjuring trick at the centre of the whole enterprise, isn't it. She can render Casaubon utterly, damning — the cold withdrawal, the petty intellectual vanity — and yet the moment you think you've got him pinned, she turns the glass. There's that extraordinary moment in Chapter 42, when Dorothea almost offers Casaubon warmth and he cannot receive it. And Eliot writes — and this is one of the great sentences in English fiction — something to the effect that his experience was of a man who had constructed a shrine in which he kept the sacred vessels of his own honour. The pity is real. The contempt is real. And they inhabit the same sentence. That is not a trick you learn from a handbook on characterisation.
Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to dwell on Casaubon, because he is the severest test of Eliot's generosity as a moralist. The lesser novelist makes him simply a villain — a dry obstruction to the heroine's happiness. Eliot does something far more demanding: she requires us to see that Casaubon's wound is genuine. His fear of Will Ladislaw, his suspicion of Dorothea's pity — these are not the calculations of a cold machine but the terrors of a man who knows he is diminished. The sentence Eliot gives us — I'm thinking of that passage where she enters his consciousness directly — she speaks of the 'intensely human' character of his suffering. 'Intensely human' — two words that arrive like a verdict and an acquittal simultaneously. That is the quality I would call moral imagination — the capacity to enter a consciousness one finds in many respects repellent.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd push back on one thing, though — not on the compassion, which is undeniable, but on how that compassion is distributed. Eliot's judgment is most searching when it turns on characters who have real interiority — Casaubon, Bulstrode, Lydgate. Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)

There's a moment in the Prelude — that extraordinary opening gambit — where she speaks of 'unhistoric acts' and the obscure lives that do not find a public record. As a performer, I find Bulstrode the most technically demanding subject of her judgment — because you must play him as a hypocrite while also playing a man who genuinely believes. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
The Bulstrode case is really where one sees Eliot's judgment at its most Shakespearean, I think. Eliot does explain — that is one of the things that marks her as a Victorian, as a novelist rather than a dramatist. But the explanation never reduces the mystery — it deepens it. He fails because he chose Rosamond — that's the obvious reading. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
That passage is, to my mind, one of the great endings in the English novel. And its force comes precisely from the fact that it is earned — you have spent eight hundred pages watching ordinary people try and partly fail and partly endure. The word I always return to in that finale is 'faithfully.' Not brilliantly. Not heroically. Faithfully. That is Eliot's moral vocabulary — the virtues she prizes are the quiet, consistent ones, the ones that do not announce themselves. And poor Dorothea is its emblem. She wanted to be Saint Theresa and became instead someone who made two or three lives better in ways that went largely unnoticed. Eliot calls this, in the Prelude, the 'unhistoric acts' of those with no 'coherent social faith' to channel them. That is not a tragedy, in the classical sense. It is something closer to the texture of actual lives.
Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold Edmund to account on that word 'faithfully,' because I think we should ask: faithful to what, and at what cost? Dorothea's second marriage — to Will Ladislaw — is conventionally read as a kind of diminishment, even a surrender. Eliot tells us Dorothea gave up a great deal of her income, lost the good opinion of her family, and was written out of Middlemarch society. And Eliot's own tone at that point is so carefully ambiguous — she calls it 'a life of mistakes' but refuses to condemn it outright. What I find most radical in that ending is the suggestion that the system — Middlemarch society, the Church, the laws of inheritance — was simply too strong. Dorothea's 'unhistoric acts' are unhistoric because history, as constituted, has no room for women's moral ambition. That isn't consolation to me — it's an indictment dressed in the most beautiful prose imaginable.
Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Daniel's right that Eliot doesn't soften the structural critique — but I'd resist the idea that the consolation is therefore false. Think of Lydgate. Here is a man of genuine scientific talent who ends up treating gout in fashionable patients and writing a treatise on rheumatic fever. Eliot calls Rosamond the 'hampering' of his life — she says he had 'a terrible tenacity about him' and yet it came to nothing much. Now — is that an indictment of Rosamond alone? The story can be told without many words. It happened when he was studying in Paris, and just at the time when, over and above his other work, he was occupied with some galvanic experiments. One evening, tired with his experimenting, and not being able to elicit the facts he needed, he left his frogs and rabbits to some repose under their trying and mysterious dispensation of unexplained shocks, and went to finish his evening at the theatre of the Porte Saint Martin, where there was a melodrama which he had already seen several times; attracted, not by the ingenious work of the collaborating authors, but by an actress whose part it was to stab her lover, mistaking him for the evil-designing duke of the piece. Lydgate was in love with this actress, as a man is in love with a woman whom he never expects to speak to. She was a Provencale, with dark eyes, a Greek profile, and rounded majestic form, having that sort of beauty which carries a sweet matronliness even in youth, and her voice was a soft cooing. She had but lately come to Paris, and bore a virtuous reputation, her husband acting with her as the unfortunate lover. It was her acting which was “no better than it should be,” but the public was satisfied. Lydgate’s only relaxation now was to go and look at this woman, just as he might have thrown himself under the breath of the sweet south on a bank of violets for a while, without prejudice to his galvanism, to which he would presently return. But this evening the old drama had a new catastrophe. At the moment when the heroine was to act the stabbing of her lover, and he was to fall gracefully, the wife veritably stabbed her husband, who fell as death willed. A wild shriek pierced the house, and the Provencale fell swooning: a shriek and a swoon were demanded by the play, but the swooning too was real this time. Lydgate leaped and climbed, he hardly knew how, on to the stage, and was active in help, making the acquaintance of his heroine by finding a contusion on her head and lifting her gently in his arms. Paris rang with the story of this death:—was it a murder? Some of the actress’s warmest admirers were inclined to believe in her guilt, and liked her the better for it (such was the taste of those times); but Lydgate was not one of these. He vehemently contended for her innocence, and the remote impersonal passion for her beauty which he had felt before, had passed now into personal devotion, and tender thought of her lot. The notion of murder was absurd: no motive was discoverable, the young couple being understood to dote on each other; and it was not unprecedented that an accidental slip of the foot should have brought these grave consequences. The legal investigation ended in Madame Laure’s release. Lydgate by this time had had many interviews with her, and found her more and more adorable. She talked little; but that was an additional charm. She was melancholy, and seemed grateful; her presence was enough, like that of the evening light. Lydgate was madly anxious about her affection, and jealous lest any other man than himself should win it and ask her to marry him. Hidden actresses, however, are not so difficult to find as some other hidden facts, and it was not long before Lydgate gathered indications that Laure had taken the route to Lyons. He found her at last acting with great success at Avignon under the same name, looking more majestic than ever as a forsaken wife carrying her child in her arms. He spoke to her after the play, was received with the usual quietude which seemed to him beautiful as clear depths of water, and obtained leave to visit her the next day; when he was bent on telling her that he adored her, and on asking her to marry him. He knew that this was like the sudden impulse of a madman—incongruous even with his habitual foibles. No matter! It was the one thing which he was resolved to do. He had two selves within him apparently, and they must learn to accommodate each other and bear reciprocal impediments.
