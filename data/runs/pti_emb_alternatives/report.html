Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The first thing you notice, reading it aloud, is that Forster gives the sky grammatical authority. Not the earth, not the people — the sky. He opens with a declaration that stops you in your tracks: "The sky settles everything — not only climates and seasons but when the earth shall be beautiful." "Settles everything" — that's an imperial verb, isn't it? The sky as absolute governor, and the earth lying prostrate beneath it — the architecture of domination is already present in the first sentence. And then Forster does something quite wonderful — he makes all of that magnificence suddenly strange and threatening. He writes: "Only in the south, where a group of fists and fingers are thrust up through the soil, is the endless expanse interrupted. These fists and fingers are the Marabar Hills, containing the extraordinary caves." Fists and fingers — not an invitation, a threat. The whole novel is folded inside that image before a single character has appeared.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
Oliver is right to linger on that word — extraordinary. Forster uses it as a kind of warning flare — these are not simply unusual caves, they are beyond the ordinary categories of experience. What strikes me, however, is the theological texture of this passage. "Glory can rain into the Chandrapore bazaars or a benediction pass from horizon to horizon." Glory, benediction — these are words from the liturgy, not the travel memoir. Forster is positioning India as a kind of spiritual trial — a place that will test not one's politics, but one's soul. The characters who come here seeking connection will encounter something that exceeds their capacity for connection — and that is a moral story, not a colonial one.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I want to hold onto that phrase Edmund used — spiritual trial — because I think it does some ideological work we should examine. When the landscape is rendered as sublime, as beyond comprehension, it becomes very difficult to ask material questions about it. Who owns this earth that lies flat and "heaves a little"? Forster does something remarkable later — he shows the indifference of the natural world to the entire human drama of empire with devastating precision. He writes, of the heat pressing down after a scene of political wrangling: "It matters so little to the majority of living beings what the minority, that calls itself human, desires or decides." That clause — "that calls itself human" — is extraordinary. Forster is not merely being whimsical about squirrels and coppersmith birds — he is puncturing the self-importance of the colonial project from the perspective of geological time.

The coppersmith bird — the "ponk ponk" — is one of my favourite moments in the whole novel. Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
The individual and the systemic are not mutually exclusive — Forster can do both, and does. What the heat passage reveals is that the social performance of colonial authority is genuinely fragile — it depends on conditions, on infrastructure, on the suppression of the inarticulate world. Forster says the inarticulate world is "closer at hand and readier to resume control as soon as men are tired." That is not merely satire — that is a structural argument about what empire costs to maintain.

Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
Fielding is one of those characters who arrives on the page fully formed — you hear him immediately. Forster gives us this beautiful, slightly rueful description of a man who believes in education with the same uncomplicated confidence that other men believe in God or in cricket. And then the line that quietly detonates everything he stands for among the Anglo-Indians: "ideas are fatal to caste, and he used ideas by that most potent method — interchange." Interchange! That single word is his entire philosophy — and Forster delivers it like a verdict. The man doesn't lecture. He converses. And in Chandrapore, that turns out to be the most subversive act imaginable.

His career, though scholastic, was varied, and had included going to the bad and repenting thereafter. By now he was a hard-bitten, good-tempered, intelligent fellow on the verge of middle age, with a belief in education. He did not mind whom he taught; public schoolboys, mental defectives and policemen, had all come his way, and he had no objection to adding Indians. Through the influence of friends, he was nominated Principal of the little college at Chandrapore, liked it, and assumed he was a success. He did succeed with his pupils, but the gulf between himself and his countrymen, which he had noticed in the train, widened distressingly. He could not at first see what was wrong. He was not unpatriotic, he always got on with Englishmen in England, all his best friends were English, so why was it not the same out here? Outwardly of the large shaggy type, with sprawling limbs and blue eyes, he appeared to inspire confidence until he spoke. Then something in his manner puzzled people and failed to allay the distrust which his profession naturally inspired. There needs must be this evil of brains in India, but woe to him through whom they are increased! The feeling grew that Mr. Fielding was a disruptive force, and rightly, for ideas are fatal to caste, and he used ideas by that most potent method—interchange. Neither a missionary nor a student, he was happiest in the give-and-take of a private conversation. The world, he believed, is a globe of men who are trying to reach one another and can best do so by the help of good will plus culture and intelligence—a creed ill suited to Chandrapore, but he had come out too late to lose it. He had no racial feeling—not because he was superior to his brother civilians, but because he had matured in a different atmosphere, where the herd-instinct does not flourish. The remark that did him most harm at the club was a silly aside to the effect that the so-called white races are really pinko-grey. He only said this to be cheery, he did not realize that “white” has no more to do with a colour than “God save the King” with a god, and that it is the height of impropriety to consider what it does connote. The pinko-grey male whom he addressed was subtly scandalized; his sense of insecurity was awoken, and he communicated it to the rest of the herd.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
What strikes me about Fielding is that Forster doesn't romanticise him — he's quite precise about the source of his liberal temperament. He has no racial feeling, Forster tells us, not because he is morally superior to his brother civilians, but because he had matured in a different atmosphere, where the herd-instinct does not flourish. That is an honest and rather cool thing to say about one's own hero. Forster resists the temptation to make virtue innate — Fielding's decency is a product of circumstance, not character. And that matters enormously, I think, for how we read his friendship with Aziz.

Daniel Rosen (marxist_critic)
I'd push on that a little further. Fielding is a product of circumstance, yes — but the circumstance Forster carefully omits to examine is the one of material advantage. When he's challenged — almost backed into a corner — on the justification for British presence in India, listen to what he actually says. "However big a badmash one is — if one's happy in consequence, that is some justification." Now Forster frames that as honesty — as the zeal for honesty eating Fielding up, in his own phrase. But happiness as justification for empire is not radicalism — it is imperialism with better manners.

Precisely — and that ambiguity is precisely what makes Fielding so interesting as a moral study. He is genuinely good-willed — and yet his goodwill has a ceiling, and he knows it. Fielding sat down by the bed, flattered at the trust reposed in him, yet rather sad. He felt old. He wished that he too could be carried away on waves of emotion. The next time they met, Aziz might be cautious and standoffish. He realized this, and it made him sad that he should realize it. Kindness, kindness, and more kindness—yes, that he might supply, but was that really all that the queer nation needed? Did it not also demand an occasional intoxication of the blood? What had he done to deserve this outburst of confidence, and what hostage could he give in exchange? He looked back at his own life. What a poor crop of secrets it had produced! There were things in it that he had shown to no one, but they were so uninteresting, it wasn’t worth while lifting a purdah on their account. He’d been in love, engaged to be married, lady broke it off, memories of her and thoughts about her had kept him from other women for a time; then indulgence, followed by repentance and equilibrium.
