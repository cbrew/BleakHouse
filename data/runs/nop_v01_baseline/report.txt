### Crossing Class Lines: Jo and the Weight of Social Machinery (close_reading)

[Host]
  Welcome back to Bleak House Unpacked, the podcast where we spend serious time with one of the great novels of the English language.  (+500ms @0.98x *serious,great*)
  Today's segment takes us to one of the most devastating threads in the entire novel: Jo, the crossing-sweeper of Tom-all-Alone's.  (@0.97x *Jo,crossing-sweeper*)
  Jo is, by any measure, Dickens' most unsparing portrait of what it means to exist at the absolute floor of Victorian society.  (@0.98x *unsparing,floor*)
  With me to unpack all of that are three people whose thinking I trust enormously on this material.  (@0.98x)
  Dr. Eleanor Hartley is a novelist and creative writing teacher who reads Dickens with a craftsperson's eye — she sees the machinery behind the effects.  (@0.98x *craftsperson's,machinery*)
  Prof. James Blackstone is a legal historian who can tell us exactly what the Victorian institutions surrounding Jo actually were and how they worked.  (@0.98x *legal,institutions*)
  And Caroline Woodcourt is a book critic who has loved this novel since she was a teenager and brings the reader's lived experience of it.  (@0.98x *loved,reader's*)
  Let's begin with first principles. Eleanor — what is Dickens actually doing when he introduces Jo?  (<1500ms> @0.98x *introduces*)

[Eleanor Hartley]
  Jo's introduction is one of the great rhetorical gambits in Victorian fiction, and I don't think people fully appreciate how constructed it is.  (+220ms *rhetorical,constructed*)
  Dickens comes at the reader almost accusingly.  (*accusingly*)
  He sets up this devastating little catechism — what does Jo know? Almost nothing. What can Jo do? Almost nothing.  (@1.01x *catechism,nothing*)
  And then he delivers that extraordinary line about Jo being a dirty, ugly, disagreeable boy — and pivots immediately to ask us what we expect.  (@0.98x [setup] *pivots,expect*)
  > "Turn that dog's descendants loose, who are his parents, brothers, sisters? What chances or mischances have made him thus?"  (+150ms @0.93x [reading] *chances,mischances*)
  The syntax enacts the argument. Those questions tumble out as if they've been suppressed and can no longer be held back.  ([commentary] *syntax,suppressed*)
  Dickens is not sentimentalizing Jo — he's indicting the system that produced him, by making us look, really look, at what that system creates.  (<1500ms> @1.01x *indicting,look*)

[James Blackstone]
  And the indictment is precise — which is what makes it so damning, as opposed to merely sentimental.  (+220ms @0.96x *precise,damning*)
  Jo exists in a specific legal and administrative void that Dickens understood better than most journalists of his day.  (@0.96x *legal,void*)
  Under the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834, relief was tied to the parish of settlement — and Jo has no parish, no settlement, no recorded existence whatsoever.  (@0.96x *settlement,recorded*)
  He is ordered to "move on" not as a metaphor — that is the actual language of the Vagrancy Acts.  (@0.96x *Vagrancy,language*)
  Constables had statutory authority to move on anyone who could not demonstrate a fixed abode or lawful occupation.  (@0.96x *statutory,fixed,abode*)
  So when Dickens writes that Jo is constantly being moved on, he is being technically accurate about a machinery of exclusion that was entirely real.  (@0.96x *technically,machinery,exclusion*)
  And what the novel captures — with extraordinary fidelity — is that this machinery has no malicious intent. It simply runs.  (<1500ms> @0.94x *malicious,runs*)

[Caroline Woodcourt]
  That phrase — "it simply runs" — is exactly what makes reading Jo's chapters so difficult. There's no villain to blame.  (+220ms @0.97x *villain,blame*)
  And I think what Dickens does with the "move on" refrain is something close to musical in its effect.  (@0.97x *musical,refrain*)
  Each time it comes back, it has gathered more weight. By the time Jo is dying, that phrase has become almost unbearable.  (@0.95x *weight,unbearable*)
  There's a passage where the narrator addresses Jo directly — and the tenderness of it just stops me every time I reach it.  (@0.96x [setup] *tenderness,directly*)
  > "Dead, your Majesty. Dead, my lords and gentlemen. Dead, Right Reverends and Wrong Reverends of every order. Dead, men and women, born with Heavenly compassion in your hearts. And dying thus around us every day."  (+150ms @0.92x [reading] *Dead,compassion,dying*)
  Every repetition of "Dead" is a hammer blow. Dickens is not letting you mourn quietly and move on yourself.  (@0.97x [commentary] *hammer,mourn*)
  He is holding your face to it.  (<1500ms> @0.93x *holding,face*)

[Eleanor Hartley]
  That passage is technically an apostrophe — the narrator turning from the story to address the reader and the ruling class simultaneously.  (+220ms @1.01x *apostrophe,simultaneously*)
  And it's in the third-person narrator's voice, not Esther's. The switch matters enormously.  (@1.01x *switch,matters*)
  Esther could grieve for Jo personally — and she does. But this apostrophe requires the omniscient narrator's authority to work.  (*omniscient,authority*)
  It has to come from a voice that can address "Right Reverends and Wrong Reverends" — a voice with the range to indict an entire society.  (*indict,range*)
  The dual narration is one of the novel's great structural inventions, and moments like Jo's death are precisely where it pays off.  (<1500ms> @1.01x *dual,pays off*)

[James Blackstone]
