### Magdalen's Calculated Transformation (deep_dive)

[Oliver Trevelyan]

### The Mechanics of Deception: Captain Wragge and the Art of the Con (close_reading)

[Host]
  We've been talking about Magdalen's transformations — the way she remakes herself, costume by costume, identity by identity.  (+500ms @0.98x *transformations,remakes*)
  But she doesn't do it alone — and the man who teaches her, or perhaps enables her, is one of the most extraordinary comic creations in Victorian fiction.  (@0.98x *enables,extraordinary*)
  Captain Wragge: swindler, showman, self-described moral agriculturist.  (@0.96x *Wragge,moral agriculturist*)
  Oliver, I want to start with you — because you said before we recorded that Wragge is the secret heart of this novel, the only character who's genuinely honest about how the world works.  (@0.99x *honest,world*)
  But here's my push: by making him so funny, so rhythmically brilliant, is Collins letting us off the hook? Are we laughing with the con, or are we being conned ourselves?  (<1500ms> *conned,ourselves*)

[Oliver Trevelyan]
  Both, simultaneously, and that is precisely the genius of it.  (+220ms @1.02x *simultaneously,genius*)
  Wragge is the only person in the novel who never pretends to be better than he is — and Collins rewards him for it with the most glorious prose in the book.  (*pretends,glorious*)
  He describes himself, with perfect serenity, as a moral agriculturist — a man who cultivates the field of human error.  (@1.01x *serenity,cultivates*)
  Now, that phrase — moral agriculturist — is not a joke dressed up as a metaphor. It is a metaphor that exposes the joke the respectable world is already running.  (@1.02x *exposes,respectable*)
  He's not hiding from us. He's showing us the machinery that everyone else is pretending isn't there.  (@1.03x *machinery,pretending*)
  And yes — we are complicit in the joke. Collins makes sure of it. The moment you laugh, you've agreed to the terms.  (<1500ms> @1.02x *complicit,terms*)

[Edmund Leigh]
  I'd want to be a little more precise about what sort of honesty we're attributing to him.  (+200ms @0.94x *precise,honesty*)
  Wragge is candid about his methods. He is not, I think, honest in any morally significant sense of the word.  (@0.93x *candid,morally*)
  What makes him genuinely interesting — and I think Oliver is right that Collins invests him with exceptional vitality — is that he clearly perceives the difference between virtue and its counterfeit.  (@0.94x *perceives,virtue,counterfeit*)
  He is not a man who cannot see goodness. He is a man who sees it — and turns away.  (@0.92x *sees,turns away*)
  That is a different kind of character — and a rather more troubling one — than a man who is simply blind to moral distinction.  (@0.93x *troubling,blind*)
  Dr. Johnson would have called it a failure of the will. And Collins, I believe, would not have entirely disagreed.  (<1500ms> @0.93x *Johnson,will*)

[Daniel Rosen]
