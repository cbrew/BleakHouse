Oliver Trevelyan (performer_and_wit)
The moment I read that sentence aloud for the first time in a recording booth, I stopped the engineer and said — he's done it in one. Because what Dickens is doing isn't false modesty — it's a theatrical announcement. The phrase 'hero of my own life' — that word 'hero' lands like a stage direction. He's not saying 'I may be important' — he's saying 'watch me.' And there's something very specific about 'these pages must show' — not 'will show,' not 'shall reveal.' 'Must.' There's an obligation in that word — a performance contract between the narrator and the reader that's already been signed before chapter one has properly begun. And the beauty is — we don't know yet whether David will be adequate to that contract. Neither does he. That's the suspense Dickens builds from the very first word.

Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
I think Oliver is entirely right, and I think he has also, characteristically, stopped one step short of the deepest thing. The theatrical gesture is real — but what gives it its extraordinary force is that the narrator genuinely doesn't know the answer to his own question. He is looking back across decades of his own life, and the question of whether he became the hero of it remains, in some fundamental sense, open. That is the metaphysical vertigo I mean — a consciousness holding its own history at arm's length and declining to pronounce the verdict. Edmund Leigh (traditionalist_critic)
My answer is that Dickens is emphatically not letting anyone off the hook.
