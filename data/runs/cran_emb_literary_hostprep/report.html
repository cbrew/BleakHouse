Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and it's a genuinely radical thing Gaskell is doing, though she disguises it as perfect naturalness. The surface narrator — Mary Smith — is witty, brisk, a little ironic, thoroughly in control of the comedy. She is the narrator who tells us, with barely suppressed delight, that Cranford is a town of Amazons. But running beneath Mary Smith's voice is a second narrator — Miss Matty — who is warmer, slower, more elegiac. She doesn't narrate in the conventional sense — she surfaces in moments of confession, of memory, of a tear that drops on someone's hand. And Gaskell is extraordinarily precise about when she lets Miss Matty's temperature into the prose and when she pulls it back. The ironic surface is what protects the emotional depth — strip the comedy away, and the sadness would become intolerable. That's the architecture. Two voices, two temperatures, one text holding them in suspension.

Eleanor Hartley (literary_critic)
Yes — and once you see it, you can't unsee it, which is a mark of how clever the architecture is. We get the news first: Jenny, the white face, the outburst. Pure shock. Then we get the eyewitness — the man who was actually there. And this is where Gaskell does something extraordinary with voice. She renders it in dialect — unpolished, stumbling, phonetically real — and the effect is almost unbearable. "And he looked up sudden, at the sound of the train coming, and seed the child, and he darted on the line and cotched it up, and his foot slipped, and the train came over him in no time." "Seed" and "cotched" — those are not mistakes. That's a man reliving something too fast to refine. And then the third telling: the county newspaper. Official language, institutional distance. The gallant gentleman. Each version buries a little more of the actual man under the telling of him. That's the structural argument: Gaskell is showing us how death gets processed by language, and how inadequate every version is.
