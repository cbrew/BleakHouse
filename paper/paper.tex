\begin{abstract}
What holds an LLM-generated multi-voice conversation together, and what
can a designer manipulate to reshape it?  We investigate these questions
through a system that generates literary discussion podcasts about
novels, identifying four levers that independently shape conversational
coherence: (1)~\emph{expert personas}---stereotyped perspectives whose
demand profiles control what each speaker seeks in the source material;
(2)~\emph{passage grounding}---the selected passages that anchor
quotation, prevent confabulation, and create material for cross-expert
engagement; (3)~\emph{conversational design instructions}---prompt-level
specifications for quote handling, reactive language, turn structure, and
prosodic annotation; and (4)~\emph{segment continuity}---the mechanisms
by which individually generated segments cohere into a single episode.
We test the system on five novels---Dickens's \textit{Bleak House} and
\textit{Our Mutual Friend}, Eliot's \textit{The Mill on the Floss},
Gaskell's \textit{North and South}, and Forster's \textit{A Passage to
India}---generating 196 multi-voice episodes across five passage
selection conditions and multiple expert panel configurations.  Key
findings: expert personas dominate script character so strongly that
\emph{different} passage selection algorithms (transport vs.\
embedding; Jaccard~0.048) produce \emph{convergent} scripts; passage
grounding of any kind prevents confabulation (+69 percentage point
quote verification advantage over ungrounded generation); and
conversational design instructions produce measurable, stable
effects---100\% quote pattern compliance, 59 reactive markers per
episode, stable expert airtime proportions across novels.  Where the
passage selection method does matter is in \emph{manipulability}:
formulating selection as min-cost flow makes every editorial choice
visible as a cost and a constraint, enabling systematic exploration at
zero marginal LLM cost.  A planned prompt ablation study will isolate
which conversational design instructions are necessary vs.\ emergent
from the shared-passage architecture.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Google's NotebookLM \citep{notebooklm2024} popularised LLM-generated
podcast conversations, but the conversational design behind such
systems is largely unexamined.  What makes a multi-voice
LLM-generated discussion \emph{cohere}---sound like a genuine
conversation rather than parallel monologues?  What levers does a
designer have, and which ones actually matter?

We investigate these questions through a system that generates literary
discussion podcasts about novels.  The system exposes four
independently manipulable levers:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Expert personas.}  Stereotyped perspectives (literary
  critic, social historian, close reader, etc.)\ with demand profiles
  that control what each speaker seeks in the source material.
  Swapping panels or adjusting demands reshapes the conversation's
  intellectual character.
\item \textbf{Passage grounding.}  The selected passages that anchor
  quotation, prevent confabulation, and create shared material for
  cross-expert engagement.  We compare five selection methods including
  a min-cost flow formulation that makes every editorial choice visible
  as a cost and a constraint.
\item \textbf{Conversational design instructions.}  Prompt-level
  specifications for quote handling, reactive language, turn structure,
  and utterance-level prosodic annotation.  These are the instructions
  that tell the LLM to produce a \emph{conversation}, not an essay.
\item \textbf{Segment continuity.}  The mechanisms by which
  independently generated segments cohere into a single episode:
  bridging from the previous topic, previewing the next, avoiding
  redundant introductions.
\end{enumerate}

We test the system on five novels spanning different authors, eras, and
literary traditions: Dickens's \textit{Bleak House}
\citep{dickens1853bleak} and \textit{Our Mutual Friend}
\citep{dickens1865omf}, Eliot's \textit{The Mill on the Floss}
\citep{eliot1860mill}, Gaskell's \textit{North and South}
\citep{gaskell1855north}, and Forster's \textit{A Passage to India}
\citep{forster1924passage}, generating 196 multi-voice episodes across
five passage selection conditions and multiple expert panel
configurations.

The central finding is a \emph{dominance hierarchy} among levers.
Expert personas dominate script character so strongly that different
passage selection algorithms (transport vs.\ embedding; passage
Jaccard~0.048) produce convergent scripts.  Passage grounding of any
kind prevents confabulation (+69 pp quote verification).
Conversational design instructions produce stable, measurable
effects---but a planned ablation study (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) will
determine which are prompt-driven vs.\ emergent from the shared-passage
architecture.  Where the passage selection \emph{method} matters is in
manipulability: the min-cost flow formulation enables systematic
editorial exploration at zero marginal LLM cost, a capability that
embedding-based retrieval cannot match.

\subsection{Enrichment Layer}
\label{sec:enrichment}

Each novel is split into passages (one per paragraph or small group of
paragraphs) and each passage is annotated by an LLM (Haiku) with 20
structured fields: an interest score (0--5), seven provision dimensions
(character\_development, plot\_advancement, thematic\_depth,
social\_critique, humor\_entertainment, atmosphere\_setting,
narrative\_technique---each rated none/weak/strong as 0/1/2),
characters present, themes, emotional register, plot function,
quotability, and a best\_quote extraction.

\subsection{Min-Cost Flow Formulation}
\label{sec:transport}

Passage selection is a min-cost flow problem solved independently per
provision dimension (Figure~\ref{fig:flow}).  Each eligible passage is
a supply node whose supply equals its provision strength (strong${}={}$2,
weak${}={}$1).  Each expert with positive demand for the dimension is a
demand node.  A single null sink absorbs all supply that the solver
does not route to experts, but at a cost equal to the passage's
interest score (0--5); discarding high-interest passages is expensive.  The OR-Tools \texttt{SimpleMinCostFlow} solver requires exact
balance (total supply $=$ total demand $+$ null sink demand) and
raises an error otherwise.

Costs encode editorial preferences.  On passage-to-cluster arcs,
strong provisions cost~1 and weak cost~3; an interest penalty
($\max(0, 5 - \text{score})$) further discourages dull passages.
Cluster-to-expert arcs are $k$ parallel arcs of capacity~1 with costs
$0, \lambda, 2\lambda, \ldots$, creating a convex diversity penalty
that discourages loading one expert with passages from the same
chapter cluster.  The solver minimises total cost, naturally preferring
strong, interesting, diverse passages.

A passage is \emph{selected} if it wins a competition in any dimension
for any expert.  The same passage may be assigned to multiple experts
(each can discuss it).  Results across all seven dimensions are merged;
when the same passage--expert pair appears in multiple dimensions, the
lowest-cost assignment is kept.

One flow is solved per provision dimension; results are merged into
per-expert passage assignments via a survival rule: a passage is
selected if it wins in any dimension for any expert.  A second
min-cost flow assigns selected passages to episode segments.  Because
solving a flow is cheap ($<$1 second, zero LLM tokens), the user can
adjust demand profiles and re-solve interactively---the core of our
manipulability hypothesis.

\subsection{Five Selection Conditions}
\label{sec:conditions}

All conditions share the same segment design (Phase~0, Haiku) and
script generation (Phase~3, Sonnet).  They differ only in what
passages are provided to Phase~3:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{No passages}: the LLM generates from prior knowledge
  alone.
\item \textbf{Random}: 32 passages sampled uniformly, assigned
  round-robin.
\item \textbf{Plain RAG}: top-$k$ passages by cosine similarity
  between expert persona embeddings and raw passage text.
\item \textbf{Embedding + LLM curation}: contextual embeddings
  \citep{anthropic2024context} with Sonnet chain-of-thought
  curation ($\sim$15K tokens per run).
\item \textbf{Transport}: min-cost flow over enrichment features
  (0 LLM tokens for selection).
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Audio Rendering (Phase~4)}
\label{sec:audio}

The structured script is rendered to audio using Google's Gemini 2.5
TTS models (\texttt{gemini-2.5-flash-preview-tts} or
\texttt{gemini-2.5-pro-preview-tts}).  The separation of script
generation (Phase~3) from audio rendering (Phase~4) is deliberate:
the structured output schema carries enough annotation to drive TTS
without further LLM inference, and cached audio segments survive
script iteration.

Each speaker is assigned a distinct Gemini voice (e.g., Sulafat for
the host, Zephyr for Hartley, Gacrux for Blackstone) with an accent
direction embedded as a natural-language stage direction in the TTS
prompt (``Home Counties accent, BBC Radio~4 style'' for the host;
``Edinburgh accent, dry and authoritative'' for Blackstone).

The utterance-level annotations from Phase~3---rate multiplier, pause
timing, emphasis words, and quote mode---are translated into
natural-language prosody directions rather than SSML parameters:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Rate}: a multiplier of 0.93 becomes ``Speak slowly and
  deliberately''; 1.04 becomes ``Speak with brisk energy.''
\item \textbf{Quote mode}: ``setup'' $\to$ ``Build anticipation---this
  leads into a literary quotation''; ``reading'' $\to$ ``Read this as
  a direct literary quotation with weight and relish.''
\item \textbf{Pauses}: \texttt{pause\_before\_ms} and
  \texttt{pause\_after\_ms} are realised as synthesised silence
  segments inserted during post-processing, not via TTS timing.
\item \textbf{Emphasis}: up to 3 words per utterance, passed as
  ``Give slight emphasis to: \emph{Chancery}, \emph{fog}.''
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiment}

For \textit{Bleak House}, 4 representative panels are run under
transport (with updated demands and interest-based discard cost),
while 20 panels from the original architecture are retained for
embedding, no-passages, RAG, and random conditions: 84 BH episodes.
For each of the other four novels, 4 panels are run under transport
and 8 panels under embedding and no-passages: 80 cross-novel episodes.
The total is 186 episodes across five novels.  Across all conditions:
identical segment design, identical script generation prompt, identical
persona descriptions, identical Pydantic output schema.  The only
manipulation is what appears in the ``Assigned Passages'' section of
the Phase~3 prompt.

\textbf{Metrics.}  Character mention density (per 1{,}000 words,
against each novel's named character list), character entropy
\citep{shannon1948mathematical}, unique characters per episode, quotes
per episode, per-expert TF-IDF vocabulary signatures (cross-condition
and cross-novel cosine), quote verification (fuzzy 5-word subsequence
matching against source text), and material similarity (Jaccard,
Jensen-Shannon divergence \citep{lin1991divergence}, maximum mean
discrepancy \citep{gretton2012kernel}).

\textbf{Additive (high-arc).}  Increasing character arc demands adds
passages proportionally to the extra demand units.  Character mentions
increase for the targeted characters, but the podcast is qualitatively
unchanged.  The manipulation is \emph{predictable}: added passages
trace directly to added demand units.

\textbf{Substitutive (peaked).}  Peaking each expert's demand
vector---amplifying the defining dimension while zeroing
secondaries---reshapes the passage set substantially.  An expert's
passages become dominated by their peak dimension, while secondary
dimensions are reallocated to other experts.  The manipulation is
\emph{transformative}: it reshapes the editorial character of the
episode through demand profile changes alone.

The embedding pipeline has no equivalent mechanism.  The user cannot
express ``what if Rosen demands only social critique?'' as a retrieval
query.  The only recourse is to rewrite the curation prompt and hope
the LLM responds systematically.

\section{Planned: Host Preparation as a Conversational Lever}
\label{sec:host-prep}

The weakest conversational metric in Table~\ref{tab:conversational}
is question frequency: 1.7 questions per segment.  The host steers
with statements and transitions rather than questions, producing
discussion that flows but rarely probes.  This exposes a fifth
potential lever: \emph{host preparation}---giving the host structured
knowledge of what each expert will want to say before the conversation
begins.  We hypothesise that this will increase question frequency,
improve cross-referencing, and produce more targeted conversational
steering.

\subsection{Design}

Phase~2.5 inserts two sub-stages between passage assignment (Phase~2)
and script generation (Phase~3), as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:hostprep}.

  (Sonnet), producing a HostBrief that is injected into Phase~3.
  Dashed arrow shows the current pipeline without preparation.}

\textbf{Phase~2.5a: Pre-interviews.}  For each expert--segment pair,
the host (Haiku) conducts a short interview: ``Given these passages,
what strikes you?  Where might you disagree with the other experts?
Which passage would you most want to quote?''  Each interview produces
a \texttt{PreInterviewResponse} (key points, potential quotes,
disagreement angles).  Calls are parallel ($\sim$22 per run: 3
experts $\times$ 7.5 segments), costing $\sim$1K Haiku tokens each.

\textbf{Phase~2.5b: Question planning.}  For each segment, the host
(Sonnet) synthesises all three experts' pre-interview responses and
plans 3--5 questions designed to: draw out each expert's strongest
take, set up productive disagreements, create cross-referencing
opportunities, and target specific passages for quotation.  Output is
a \texttt{HostBrief} (planned questions, steering notes,
cross-engagement targets).  One Sonnet call per segment ($\sim$3K
tokens each).

\textbf{Phase~3 integration.}  The HostBrief is appended to the
Phase~3 user message alongside the passage assignments.  The prompt
instructs: ``The host has prepared these questions---weave them
naturally into conversation, not as rigid Q\&A but as informed
steering.''

\subsection{Expected Cost}

Phase~2.5a: $\sim$22K Haiku tokens per run (parallel, cheap).
Phase~2.5b: $\sim$22K Sonnet tokens per run.  Total: $\sim$44K
additional tokens, a 43\% increase over the current per-run budget
but still dominated by Phase~3 script generation.

\subsection{Hypotheses and Planned Evaluation}

We will compare host-prepared vs unprepared episodes across at least 3
panels $\times$ 3 conditions (9+ paired comparisons):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Question frequency} increases from 1.7 to 3--5 per
  segment.
\item \textbf{Cross-expert referencing} increases beyond the current
  61.4\%, as questions are designed to create cross-engagement.
\item \textbf{Reactive language markers} increase, as experts respond
  to targeted questions rather than free-associating.
\item \textbf{Quote pattern compliance} is maintained or improves, as
  the HostBrief targets specific passages for quotation.
\item \textbf{Expert identity} is not diluted---experts should still
  exhibit characteristic vocabulary despite more structured steering.
\end{enumerate}

\emph{Status: designed, data models and pipeline integration planned,
not yet implemented.}


\section{Planned: What Is Prompt-Driven vs.\ Architecture-Driven?}
\label{sec:ablation}

The conversational design measurements in
Table~\ref{tab:conversational} show that the Phase~3 prompt's
instructions produce their intended effects.  But the central
scientific question remains open: which conversational behaviours are
\emph{prompt-driven}---collapsing when the instruction is
removed---and which are \emph{architecture-driven}---emerging from the
shared-passage design regardless of prompting?  Does the model produce
expert-to-expert transitions because the prompt says ``this is a
conversation, not parallel monologues,'' or because the shared-context
architecture (all experts see all passages) makes cross-engagement
natural?  Does the three-part quote pattern emerge from the explicit
specification, or would the model structure quotes similarly without
it?

sharing and all other instructions intact.

\subsection{Expected Findings}

The ablation study will classify each instruction into one of three
categories:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Prompt-driven behaviours}: metrics that collapse when
  the instruction is removed.  We expect the three-part quote pattern
  (A2) and rate/pause guidance (A3) to be prompt-driven---the model
  has no reason to produce these specific patterns without instruction.
\item \textbf{Architecture-driven behaviours}: metrics that survive
  ablation because the shared-context architecture produces them
  naturally.  We hypothesise that cross-expert referencing (A1) may
  partially survive, since experts who see each other's passages have
  natural reason to engage with them regardless of the prompt
  instruction.
\item \textbf{Redundant instructions}: metrics that are unaffected
  because the model's default behaviour already matches the
  instruction.  Sentence type classification (A4) may be redundant if
  the model naturally produces functional variety in literary
  discussion.
\end{enumerate}

The most interesting potential finding is a dissociation between A1
and the cross-referencing metric: if removing ``experts react to each
other'' does \emph{not} reduce cross-referencing, then the
shared-passage architecture---not the prompt instruction---is the
active ingredient.  This would strengthen the system design argument
that passage sharing is the key architectural decision.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{A Dominance Hierarchy of Conversational Levers}

The results reveal a clear hierarchy among the four levers that shape
conversational coherence:

\textbf{Personas dominate.}  Expert identity signatures are
remarkably stable across novels, selection conditions, and panel
configurations.  The same persona discussing different passages about
different novels produces overlapping vocabulary and consistent
airtime proportions.  This is the strongest force shaping what a
conversation \emph{sounds like}.

\textbf{Grounding prevents confabulation.}  Any form of passage
grounding (+69 pp quote verification) dwarfs the difference between
grounding methods.  The shared-passage architecture---all experts see
all passages---may also be the active ingredient for cross-expert
engagement, though the ablation study (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) is
needed to confirm this.

\textbf{Conversational instructions produce measurable effects.}
Quote pattern compliance, reactive markers, and prosodic annotation
are all prompt-driven.  But which instructions are \emph{necessary}
vs.\ which are redundant given the architecture remains an open
question---precisely the question the ablation study addresses.

\textbf{Passage selection method matters for manipulability, not
quality.}  Transport and embedding produce comparable scripts from
almost entirely different passages.  The transport formulation's
contribution is not better scripts but \emph{editorial control}: the
demand-profile mechanism allows systematic, predictable manipulation
at zero marginal LLM cost.

\subsection{Stereotypes as Editorial Objects}

The expert personas are deliberately stereotypical.  Following
\citet{boden2004creative}, the system performs combinational
creativity---but the interesting creativity is the user's, exploring
the configuration space and confronting the stereotypes that make the
machinery work.  Making stereotypes explicit as demand vectors, rather
than implicit in a prompt, turns editorial bias into a first-class
object that can be inspected, compared, and argued about.

\subsection{Limitations}

All evaluation is automated; no human listeners have judged the
generated episodes.  Statistical comparisons are descriptive; bootstrap
CIs would strengthen the claims.  The manipulability advantage assumes a
user who wants to explore configurations---for one-shot generation, the
transport pipeline offers no quality advantage over plain RAG.  The
five-novel study uses the same automated metrics throughout; human
evaluation remains future work.  All five novels are from the English
literary canon (1853--1924); the pipeline has not been tested on
non-literary text, non-English sources, or contemporary works.

\subsection{Implications}

The central insight is that multi-voice LLM-generated conversations
are shaped by a hierarchy of design levers, not a single algorithmic
choice.  Personas, grounding, conversational instructions, and segment
continuity each contribute independently, and understanding which
levers dominate---and which are redundant---is essential for
principled system design.  This framing applies beyond literary
podcasts to any domain where LLMs generate structured multi-party
discourse: legal panel discussions, curriculum dialogues, exhibit
narration, or simulated expert consultations.
