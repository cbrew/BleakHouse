Not In Our Time

Transport-based content selection as an inspectable, manipulable alternative to RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation retrieves by embedding similarity and feeds results to an LLM. This is effective but opaque: the user cannot see why a passage was selected, cannot adjust selection criteria, and cannot explore how different editorial priorities reshape the output.

We present an alternative in which passage selection is a min-cost flow problem, making every selection decision visible as a cost, a flow, and a constraint. Because solving a flow is cheap compared to regenerating with an LLM, the system gives its user a flexible thinking tool for rapid exploration of alternatives.

We demonstrate it by generating imitation panel discussions in the style of BBC Radio 4's In Our Time, across 15 Victorian and early-modern novels by seven authors (Dickens, Eliot, Gaskell, Forster, Collins, Gissing, Oliphant) — 60,774 passages, 180 experimental conditions, and 9 rendered audio episodes.

What the system does

The system operates over passages enriched by an LLM with structured metadata (20 fields covering narrative function, thematic weight, and emotional register). The first transport problem selects passages to satisfy demand profiles declared by fictional expert personas — a novelist, a legal historian, a close reader, a performer, a Marxist, a traditionalist. The second assigns them to episode segments. An LLM generates multi-voice dialogue with speaking-style annotations for TTS rendering.

The expert personas are, deliberately, stereotypes: the Marxist always finds class struggle; the performer always finds comedy; the close reader always finds prose rhythm. By making these stereotypes explicit and adjustable — encoded as demand vectors rather than implicit in a prompt — the system turns editorial bias into a first-class object that can be inspected, compared, and argued about.

Why this matters beyond literature

The original motivation for this work comes from legal and other professional information services. RAG is now a standard approach for grounding LLM output in source documents — but in professional applications, core RAG is typically complemented by a rich background of metadata, curated secondary material, expert taxonomies, and editorial judgement. The question is: what happens as this supporting infrastructure becomes less available?

Victorian novels provide a safe exploration space for this question. Like legal corpora, they are large, structurally complex, and vary in how much secondary material exists to support retrieval. Some texts (Bleak House, Middlemarch) have extensive critical apparatus — study guides, Wikipedia articles, academic commentary — analogous to the headnotes, annotations, and practice guides that surround well-litigated areas of law. Others (Hester, No Name) have almost none, analogous to emerging or under-documented legal domains. By comparing how the system performs across this spectrum — from richly supported to minimally supported texts — we can observe the degradation patterns that matter for any RAG deployment: when does grounding hold, when does it fail, and what does failure look like?

The answer, as the research findings show, is that failure is not random. It is graduated, vocabulary-preserving, and correlated with the volume of analytical reinforcement available. Understanding this pattern — and being able to inspect it passage by passage — is the practical contribution of this work.

How this was built

This project was built almost entirely through conversation with Claude Code (Anthropic's agentic coding tool). The pipeline, the webapp, the experiment orchestration, the paper drafts, and this site were all produced in extended Claude Code sessions using Steve Yegge's Beads for issue tracking across sessions.

The development process follows the patterns described in Simon Willison's Agentic Engineering Patterns guide: iterative refinement, human-in-the-loop decision-making, and systematic verification. Claude Code wrote the code, ran the experiments, generated the reports, drafted the papers, and deployed this site — with the human providing direction, catching errors, and making editorial judgements at every step.

The code is at github.com/cbrew/BleakHouse.
