Making It Sound Right

Structured output for text-to-speech rendering

Part 5 of the Not In Our Time series

The script generation prompt is approximately 250 lines long. Most of that isn't about what the experts should say — it's about how they should sound.

Sentence-level structured output

Each speaker turn is broken into individual utterances — one sentence or short clause each. Every utterance carries annotations that the TTS engine will use directly:

  • sentence_type: intro, question, quote_setup, quote_reading, analysis, punchline, transition, closing
  • quote_mode: none, setup, reading, commentary — controls the three-phase quote pacing
  • rate: speaking rate multiplier (0.90–1.05 relative to speaker's base)
  • pause_before_ms / pause_after_ms: silence before and after each utterance
  • emphasis_words: 0–3 words deserving slight stress

The quote handling pattern is critical. When a speaker reads a quote from the novel:

  1. Setup (quote_mode="setup"): leading text, rate 0.98, slight pause at end
  2. Reading (quote_mode="reading", is_quote=true): the quote itself, slowed to 0.93, pause before and after
  3. Commentary (quote_mode="commentary"): immediate reaction, back to normal rate

This three-phase pattern is what makes the generated audio sound like someone reading a passage rather than merely saying it.

Inter-speaker timing

The prompt includes a timing table:

  • Same speaker continuation: 120–180ms
  • Speaker switch after analysis: 180–260ms
  • Speaker switch after joke/sting: 260–420ms
  • Before segment pivot: 500–900ms

These pauses are set via pause_before_ms on the first utterance of each turn. The LLM learns to use them contextually — longer pauses after a weighty quote, shorter ones for rapid agreement.

Voice assignment

Audio rendering uses Gemini TTS (flash tier). Each speaker is assigned a voice from Google's voice library and an accent direction embedded in the TTS prompt:

  • Host: Sulafat — "warm Home Counties accent, like a BBC Radio 4 presenter"
  • Hartley: Zephyr — "lively Cambridge accent"
  • Blackstone: Sadaltager — "measured Edinburgh accent"
  • Woodcourt: Achernar — "gentle Bristol accent"

The interdisciplinary panel uses American voices: Chen gets "clear California accent," Martinez gets "soft American Southwest accent," Volkov gets "crisp American East Coast accent." This required adding entries to three hardcoded dictionaries in render_audio.py — a design debt we've filed for refactoring (voice/accent should come from the ExpertPersona object, not separate dicts).

The hardcoded voices problem

When we first rendered the interdisciplinary panel, all three Americans came out with British accents. The voice assignment dicts (SPEAKER_VOICES, SPEAKER_ACCENTS, SPEAKER_VOICE_POLICIES) only had entries for the literary-critical panels. New experts fell through to defaults. The fix was straightforward — add entries — but the architecture should read from the persona definition instead.

Caching

Each turn's audio is cached by a hash of the TTS prompt + voice name + model ID. Re-rendering an episode that hasn't changed hits cache for every turn. Changing one segment only re-renders that segment's turns. This makes iterative refinement practical — you can adjust a prompt, re-generate one segment, and re-render in minutes rather than the ~30 minutes a full episode takes.

Assembly

Segments are concatenated with 2-second silence breaks between them. The final podcast.mp3 is exported via pydub at 192kbps. Typical episode: 7 segments, 80–100 minutes, 60–120MB.

Audio manifests (timing data per turn) are generated either from actual TTS durations (when audio exists) or estimated from character counts (~15 chars/second). The manifests power both the audio player (synchronised transcript) and the timing displays in the tracker.
