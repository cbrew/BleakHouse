From Scanned PDFs to Structured Legal Intelligence

How we built a system that transforms messy legal documents into richly annotated data

Part 12 of the Not In Our Time series

Note: This post describes the legal document analysis work that provided the technical inspiration for the Not In Our Time project.

The Problem: Legal Documents Are a Mess

Legal documents arrive in every imaginable format: scanned PDFs with OCR artifacts, Word documents with inconsistent formatting, emails exported as HTML. Before any AI can reason about the law, it needs clean, structured text.

But cleaning isn't enough. A sentence like "Defendant breached the employment agreement on March 15, 2023" contains multiple layers of meaning:

  • Facts: A breach occurred
  • Timeline: March 15, 2023
  • Party information: The defendant
  • Contractual: Employment agreement terms

Our pipeline extracts all of this, turning documents into structured knowledge graphs that power downstream legal analysis.

Architecture Overview

The pipeline follows a multi-stage process from extraction to enrichment. Documents are segmented into blocks, corrected for OCR errors using LLMs, and then enriched with multidimensional metadata. This metadata includes seven "provision dimensions" — facts, legal_basis, party_info, timeline, financials, contractual, and admissions — which allow us to model document content as a vector space.

Stage 1: Text Extraction and Normalization

Legal PDFs often come from document management systems that produce complex HTML output. We use pdfplumber and pypandoc, followed by BeautifulSoup to preserve structural information via XPaths. We also use ftfy (Fixes Text For You) to handle Unicode chaos and custom logic for dehyphenation of OCR-generated line breaks.

Stage 2: LLM-Powered Sentence Correction

Raw OCR text has errors that simple regex can't fix. We use Claude to perform intelligent correction, focusing on segmentation review, OCR error correction (like rn→m), and offset tracking to maintain mappings to the original text. Using Claude's structured output mode guarantees valid JSON that matches our Pydantic models.

Stage 3: Enrichment—The Heart of the Pipeline

Here's where documents become intelligent. Each sentence gets classified across multiple dimensions. We model legal content as a 7-dimensional vector:

DimensionWhat It Captures
factsEvents, circumstances, actions
legal_basisStatutes, case law, legal authorities
party_infoParties, relationships, roles
timelineDates, sequences, causation
financialsMoney, damages, payments
contractualContract terms, obligations
admissionsAcknowledgments, concessions

Each dimension has a strength: none, weak, or strong. Beyond dimensions, we classify *how* each source contributes (DEFINES, ALLEGES, ADMITS, DENIES, EVIDENCES, REFERENCES). Admissions are weighted highest in matching — when a party admits something, you don't need other evidence.

Stage 4: Document Profiles

After enrichment, we aggregate sentence-level data into document profiles. This gives us a queryable summary: "Which documents have strong financials provisions?" or "What defines party relationships?" and allows us to assess coverage across the sections of an Early Case Assessment (ECA) report.

Stage 5: Match Matrix

The match matrix maps what's needed (target report sections) to what's available (source documents). We compute coverage scores for each dimension and explicitly identify gaps. Gaps are features, not bugs — they tell the attorney exactly what evidence is missing from the case file.

Stage 6: ECA Report Generation

Finally, we generate the report using Claude, with explicit gap signaling. We use different models based on section complexity (Haiku for straightforward sections, Sonnet for strategic synthesis). The generation prompt enforces strict rules: only state what sources support, include citations, and insert [GAP: description] markers where evidence is missing.
