Research Findings

Key results from 180 experimental conditions across 15 novels

1. Framework Dominance

Two algorithmically distinct reading strategies — synoptic (min-cost flow, 59 passages from 28 chapters) and analytical (embeddings, 32 from 20 chapters) — select nearly disjoint passage sets (Jaccard 0.057) yet produce discussions whose character is determined by the interpretive framework, not the reading strategy.

Expert airtime is stable to ±2% across all 15 novels: Hartley 32–37%, Blackstone 30–37%, Woodcourt 29–34%.

Caveat: Framework dominance is realised through a pipeline that privileges expert identity at every stage. Whether this reflects interpretive discourse or pipeline engineering is an open question.

2. The Knowledge Gradient

Without source passages, the LLM's ability to quote novels from memory ranges from 57% (Middlemarch) to 0% (Hester). All novels are on Project Gutenberg; the gradient reflects analytical reinforcement, not textual absence.

Measuring analytical exposure

The attention score is a composite of three proxies, each capturing a different facet of a novel's analytical footprint:

  1. Goodreads ratings count (log-normalised) — present-day readership. Range: 548 (Hester) to 261,000 (David Copperfield).
  2. Wikipedia article word count (linear-normalised) — encyclopaedic coverage. Longer articles contain more plot summary and quoted excerpts. Range: ~850 (Miss Marjoribanks) to ~19,000 (David Copperfield).
  3. Study guide presence (binary: SparkNotes, LitCharts, etc.) — curriculum presence. Study guides systematically excerpt key passages, producing the passage-level duplication that Carlini et al. predict will strengthen memorisation.

The composite is the unweighted mean (0–100 scale). It correlates with nop verification at r = 0.72 (all 15 novels) or r = 0.89 excluding the North and South outlier. North and South's Goodreads popularity is inflated by the 2004 BBC adaptation, which generates discussion of the adaptation without reinforcing the novel's specific passages.

We report the score as one defensible operationalisation, not a validated instrument. Different reasonable choices of proxies or weighting would produce different numbers. All data sourced March 2026.

Within-author evidence

The gradient persists within authors, controlling for prose style:

  • Dickens: Bleak House (53%) > David Copperfield (44%) > Hard Times (39%) > Our Mutual Friend (29%)
  • Eliot: Middlemarch (57%) > Mill on the Floss (37%) > Daniel Deronda (30%)
  • Gissing: New Grub Street (20%) > The Odd Women (6%)

3. Pastiche, Not Hallucination

Of 726 invented quotes (0% match with any passage):

  • 82% draw 90–100% of their words from the novel's vocabulary
  • Mean word overlap: 95.1%
  • Minimum: 67% — even the worst confabulations use the novel's word stock

Example: "I am very weak, but I shall begin the world" — every word appears in Bleak House, but this sentence does not exist. Classic Dickensian pastiche.

4. Host Preparation Effect

Host prep raises questions per segment from ~1 to ~11, uniformly across all 15 novels.

The effect is architectural, not content-dependent. Scaffolding transforms monologue into dialogue regardless of the novel.

5. The Interdisciplinary Panel

Replacing literary scholars with a computer scientist (NLP), an astronomer, and a musicologist produced recognisably different readings but structurally convergent discussions.

Key finding: the experts brought their metaphors but not their methods. The host preparation prompts import literary-critical norms regardless of the persona. See the full report.
