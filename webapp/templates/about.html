How It Works

From a novel on the shelf to a podcast you can listen to

Every episode starts with a novel and ends with a multi-voice discussion you can listen to, read as a transcript, or inspect at every stage. Here is what happens in between.

1. Read and annotate the novel

The novel is split into short passages (50–80 words each). An LLM reads every passage and annotates it: how interesting is it? What does it contribute — character development, social critique, atmosphere, plot, humour? Which characters appear? What are the key themes? Is there a quotable line?

This produces a structured description of the entire novel — 15 novels, about 60,000 passages, each with a 20-field annotation. Think of it as a very detailed index that a computer can reason about.

2. Design the episode

An LLM designs 5–8 segments for the episode: an opening, several deep dives into different aspects of the novel, and a closing. Each segment has a title, a focus (close reading, character study, thematic exploration), and a rough budget for how many passages it needs.

3. Choose the passages

This is the part we experiment with most. We have three ways to decide which passages the experts will discuss:

Synoptic selection

Each expert says what they want to talk about (more character development, or more social critique). A mathematical solver finds the best assignment of passages to experts — like a scheduling algorithm, but for literary discussion. No AI involved in the selection itself, just optimisation. Fast and completely transparent.

Analytical selection

A search engine finds passages that match each expert's interests by meaning (not keywords). Then an LLM curates the results, picking the passages most worth discussing. This is closer to how a research assistant might prepare for a seminar.

No passages

The experts discuss the novel from memory, with no passages provided. This tests what the AI already "knows" about each novel — a lot for Bleak House, very little for Hester.

The surprise: the first two methods select almost entirely different passages (fewer than 6% in common) but produce discussions that are hard to tell apart. We call this framework dominance — the expert personas and host questions shape the conversation more than the raw material. See the research findings.

4. Prepare the host

This step makes a dramatic difference to the quality of the discussion. Without it, the host asks generic questions and the experts deliver monologues. With it, the host asks sharp, targeted questions that draw out disagreements and cross-references between experts.

How it works:

  1. Pre-interviews. Each expert is interviewed privately about the upcoming segment. What strikes you about these passages? Where would you disagree with the other panellists? What's your strongest claim?
  2. Scholarly research (in later versions). During the pre-interview, experts can search OpenAlex and Wikipedia for real scholarly works. They read abstracts and article extracts, and propose references. These are then verified against the APIs and winnowed to a short reading list a listener could actually follow up on.
  3. Question planning. The host reads all the pre-interview responses and plans 3–5 questions per segment, designed to draw out the best material and create genuine conversation.

Effect: question density rises from ~1 to ~11 per segment. The discussion becomes a conversation rather than a sequence of speeches.

5. Generate the script

One LLM call per segment produces a complete multi-voice script. The output is structured down to the individual sentence: who speaks, how fast, where to pause, which words to emphasise, when to quote from the novel. The expert personas — their perspective, speaking style, and voice — shape every turn.

In later versions, the script generation sees a shorter description of each expert (perspective and voice, not a methods inventory) to prevent experts from repetitively performing their methodology. The full descriptions are used in pre-interviews, where methodological depth is appropriate.

6. Produce the output

Each episode produces several outputs:

  • A transcript you can read, with every quoted passage linked back to its source in the novel
  • An inspectable report showing the host's preparation, pre-interview responses, and passage metadata — you can see exactly why each passage was chosen and how the host used it
  • A verified reading list (when scholarly tools were used) with works the experts cited, checked against real databases
  • Audio (for selected episodes) rendered by Google's Gemini TTS with per-speaker voice assignment

The experiment

We run this process 180 times: 15 novels × 2 expert panels × 3 selection methods × 2 host-preparation settings. The experiment matrix shows all 180 conditions. Later versions explore persona design, scholarly tools, and reading list generation.

Everything is designed to be inspected and questioned. If you think the experts are wrong, or the passages are poorly chosen, or the host's questions miss the point — you can see exactly where and why, and change it.
