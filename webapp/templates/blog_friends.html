What If I Put My Friends in the Podcast?

Non-expert experts: an NLP scientist, an astronomer, and a musicologist discuss Bleak House

Part 7 of the Not In Our Time series

The default panelists are literary scholars: a formalist, a social historian, a close reader. They're stereotypes — deliberately — and they produce reliably interesting literary-critical discussion. But what happens when you replace them with experts from entirely different fields?

The panel

Sarah Chen — computer scientist specialising in speech recognition and NLP. Trained in formal linguistics. When she says "syntax" she means actual phrase structure — constituency, dependency relations — not a literary metaphor. She notices that Dickens's fog passage has no main clause.

Rebecca Martinez — observational astronomer studying protoplanetary disks. Reads fiction for vast perspectives — time, mortality, cosmic indifference. Finds the fog eerily resonant with the opacity of stellar nurseries. "Clarity, in this novel, is the temporary accident."

Elena Volkov — musicologist and cultural historian. Studies how music functioned as soft power during the Cold War. Reads novels as political artefacts — alert to institutions that consume the people they serve. "Dickens's fog is gorgeous. That's the problem."

What happened

The discussions are recognisably different from the literary-critical baseline. Chen analyses agent deletion in the opening sentences. Martinez compares Chancery to a quasi-stable physical system. Volkov draws parallels between Victorian institutional corruption and Soviet conservatories.

But two things are worth noting:

Framework dominance still holds. Despite radically different personas, expert airtime remains balanced and the discussions are structurally similar to the literary-critical ones. The framework dominates even when the framework is non-literary.

Metaphors but not methods. The astronomer does not propose measurements or hypotheses. The NLP scientist does not run a parser or report n-gram statistics. The musicologist does not analyse rhythmic structure or counterpoint. They produce literary-critical discourse decorated with disciplinary imagery — because the host preparation prompts ("what strikes you about these passages?") import literary-critical norms regardless of the persona.

The persona engineering

Getting Chen right required iteration. The initial persona description said she "reads novels the way she reads corpora" — vague enough that the LLM produced literary gobbledygook uses of "syntax" as a prestige word. The fix was both positive examples and negative guardrails:

When she says 'syntax' she means actual phrase structure — constituency, dependency relations, argument structure — not 'the way the prose is organised.' She would never say 'the syntax of the narrative'; that is a literary critic's metaphor, not a linguist's usage. She is mildly irritated when others use linguistic terms loosely.

After this sharpening, Chen's language became genuinely precise: "a noun phrase with four pre-nominal modifiers, each compounded — and then 'brazen-faced' pulls the floor out."

The prompt critique

The host preparation prompt asks "what strikes you about these passages?" — a literary-critical question regardless of the persona. A prompt designed for genuinely interdisciplinary encounter might ask: "what would your discipline's tools reveal about this passage?"

This is itself a finding about where creative constraint operates. The persona defines who the expert is. The prompt defines how they engage. Both shape the output, and the prompt's literary-critical norms are strong enough to override disciplinary identity.
