Min-Cost Flow vs Embeddings

Two ways to read a novel — and why they converge

Part 3 of the Not In Our Time series

The core question: given a novel with thousands of enriched passages and an expert panel with specific analytical interests, which passages should we select for discussion?

We implement two fundamentally different answers and then compare what happens.

Transport: min-cost network flow

Each expert declares a demand profile over the seven provision dimensions. Eleanor Hartley (literary critic) wants narrative_technique and character_development. James Blackstone (social historian) wants social_critique. Caroline Woodcourt (close reader) wants character_development and thematic_depth.

Each passage has a supply: the provision dimensions the enrichment schema rated as "strong" or "weak". A strong match costs 1; a weak match costs 3. Every passage also has a connection to a null sink at a cost equal to its interest score — so interesting passages are expensive to discard.

Google's OR-Tools SimpleMinCostFlow solver finds the optimal assignment. Budget: up to 60 passages, 8–25 per expert. Solve time: under 100 milliseconds. LLM tokens: zero.

The result is a synoptic reading: 59 passages from 28 chapters on average. Because the demand is multi-dimensional — each expert needs passages from several different dimensions — the solver draws from across the novel. No single chapter supplies all seven dimensions, so coverage must be broad.

Embedding: contextual retrieval

Following Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval approach, each passage is embedded with context from its surrounding chapter. Each expert's profile description is also embedded. Candidate passages are retrieved by cosine similarity to each expert's embedding.

A pool of ~100 candidates is then curated by Claude Sonnet, which selects the 32 most discussion-worthy passages. This costs ~15K Sonnet tokens and ~10 seconds.

The result is an analytical reading: 32 passages from 20 chapters. Because retrieval is similarity-driven, passages cluster around each expert's dominant theme. A social historian's profile embeds close to passages about institutional critique; the retrieved candidates are topically concentrated.

Transport over-selects character and plot (breadth). Embedding over-selects social critique and thematic depth (depth). These are recognisable scholarly strategies — the seminar leader who assigns the whole novel versus the one who curates a dossier.

Is the difference systematic?

Yes, but with a caveat. The passage counts (60 budget vs 32 target) are design parameters. You could set them equal. But the qualitative character — multi-dimensional optimisation producing breadth, similarity retrieval producing depth — would persist at any matched budget. Transport distributes across dimensions; embedding concentrates within themes. This is inherent to the algorithms.

The convergence

Despite a Jaccard similarity of just 0.057 (fewer than 6% of passages shared), the resulting discussions are nearly identical in character. Expert airtime is stable to ±2% across all 15 novels and both methods.

This is the framework dominance finding: the interpretive framework — embodied in the expert personas — determines what gets said, regardless of which passages are selected. The reading strategy matters far less than the reader.

A caveat: framework dominance is realised through a pipeline that privileges expert identity at every stage. Whether it reflects a deep property of interpretive discourse or the pipeline's engineering is an open question.

When to use which

Transport when you want: inspectability (every selection is a cost), manipulability (change demands, see different passages), editorial exploration (dozens of configs in seconds), zero API cost.

Embedding when you want: semantic relevance without pre-defined dimensions, to test whether similarity-based retrieval produces different discussions (answer: it doesn't, much), or to benchmark against the RAG standard.

Both when you want: the convergence finding itself — evidence that the framework dominates.
