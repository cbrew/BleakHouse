\begin{block}{The Question}
    \textit{A reader who surveys a novel broadly and a reader who studies it narrowly will assemble different evidence for discussion. Do they arrive at different discussions?}

    Google's NotebookLM popularised LLM-generated podcast conversations, but the conversational design behind such systems is largely unexamined. What makes a multi-voice discussion cohere---sound like a genuine conversation rather than parallel monologues? What levers does a designer have, and which ones actually matter?

    We implement two reading strategies as passage-selection algorithms applied to \textbf{15 Victorian novels}, then generate structured multi-voice literary discussions from each selection.

    \vspace{10pt}
    \textbf{15 novels} \hfill \textbf{60,774 passages} \hfill \textbf{180 conditions}
    \vspace{5pt}

    Seven authors (Dickens, Eliot, Gaskell, Forster, Collins, Gissing, Oliphant), seven decades (1850--1924).
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Two Reading Strategies}
    \textbf{Synoptic} (min-cost flow): 59 passages from 28 chapters. Favours character development (+8.3pp) and plot (+10.2pp). Zero LLM tokens, <1s.

    \textbf{Analytical} (embeddings + LLM curation): 32 passages from 20 chapters. Favours social critique (+13.4pp) and thematic depth (+6.9pp).

    \textbf{No-passages}: LLM discusses from prior knowledge alone.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Why Not RAG?}
    Standard RAG retrieves by embedding similarity and feeds results to an LLM. Effective but opaque: the user cannot see why a passage was selected, cannot adjust criteria, and cannot explore how different priorities reshape the output.

    Min-cost flow makes every decision visible as a \textbf{cost}, a \textbf{flow}, and a \textbf{constraint}. Solving a flow costs <1s and zero LLM tokens---a thinking tool for rapid exploration of alternatives.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{The Reader Makes the Reading}
    \textit{The pipeline privileges expert identity at every stage --- enrichment dimensions match demand profiles, the selector optimises for demand satisfaction, host preparation targets specific experts, and the script prompt reinforces perspectives. Convergence follows from this engineering.}

    The system exposes four independently manipulable levers: \textbf{expert personas}, \textbf{passage grounding}, \textbf{conversational design}, and \textbf{segment continuity}.

    \vspace{5pt}
    \textbf{Expert Airtime Stable to $\pm$2\%}:
    Eleanor Hartley: 32--37\%; James Blackstone: 30--37\%; Caroline Woodcourt: 29--34\%.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Two Panels Discuss \textit{Bleak House}}
    \textbf{Literary Panel}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Eleanor Hartley}: ``What strikes me about that Nemo sentence is the grammatical audacity of it...''
        \item \textbf{James Blackstone}: ``The fog is not decoration. It is the Court of Chancery made visible...''
        \item \textbf{Caroline Woodcourt}: ``Readers sometimes use the fog as a kind of permission... What moves me is something far quieter...''
    \end{itemize}

    \textbf{Interdisciplinary Panel}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Sarah Chen (NLP Scientist)}: ``That is a noun phrase with four pre-nominal modifiers...''
        \item \textbf{Elena Volkov (Musicologist)}: ``Institutions don't announce their corruption---they announce their solidity...''
        \item \textbf{Rebecca Martinez (Astronomer)}: ``The ships of Law and Equity aren't sunk, they're in reserve...''
    \end{itemize}

    The experts bring their \textbf{metaphors} but not their \textbf{methods} --- the scaffold imports literary-critical norms regardless of the persona.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Deliberate Stereotypes}
    The expert personas are deliberately caricatures: the literary critic always finds prose rhythm; the social historian always finds institutional critique. By making the stereotypes explicit---encoded as \textbf{demand vectors} ---the system turns editorial bias into a first-class object that can be inspected, compared, and argued about.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Pastiche in Action}
    \textit{``Fog everywhere. Fog up the river...''} --- \textbf{Verified 100\%} (LLM memory).

    \textit{``I am very weak, but I shall begin the world.''} --- \textbf{Invented (100\% vocab)}. Every word appears in the novel, but this sentence does not exist.

    \textit{``I don't know nothink.''} --- \textbf{Invented (80\% vocab)}. Jo's dialect remembered; phrasing reconstructed.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Key Findings}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Framework dominates strategy.} Two reading algorithms select <6\% overlapping passages yet produce discussions with identical expert airtime ($\pm$2\%).
        \item \textbf{Pastiche, not hallucination.} Ungrounded LLMs write \textit{in the style of} a novel, not \textit{from} it. Fidelity tracks training-data exposure.
        \item \textbf{Scaffolding is content-independent.} Host preparation raises questions uniformly across all 15 novels.
    \end{enumerate}

    \vspace{5pt}
    \textbf{Framework $>$ Grounding $>$ Strategy}
\end{block}
